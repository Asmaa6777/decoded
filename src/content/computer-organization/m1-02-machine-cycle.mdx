# The Machine Cycle

Here is everything a CPU does, its entire life, in one loop:

1. **Fetch** the next instruction from memory.
2. **Decode** it, to work out what it's asking for.
3. **Execute** it.
4. **Store** the result.
5. Go back to step 1.

That's it. A web browser and a video game are this loop, run billions of times per second.

## What to notice

**Memory doesn't know what's what.** Cell 0 and cell 4 are both just values. The only reason `LOAD 4` is treated as an instruction is that the PC pointed at it. That's the stored-program idea in action.

**The PC moves before the instruction runs.** By the time `ADD 5` executes, the PC already says 2. This is what makes a jump simple: a jump instruction only has to overwrite the PC, and the next fetch happens wherever it now points. Every loop and `if` statement in every program depends on this.

**Most of the work is moving data around.** Count the steps that only copy a value from one place to another, against those where the ALU does arithmetic. The CPU spends most of its time on trips to memory, which is why so much of [computer architecture](/courses/computer-architecture/cache-mapping) is about making those trips shorter.

<Callout tone="sky" label="The clock keeps time">
Each small step above takes one or more ticks of the CPU's [clock](/courses/logic-design/signals). A 4 GHz processor ticks 4 billion times a second. Clock speed isn't the whole story of speed, though, as the [performance lesson](/courses/computer-architecture/measuring-performance) shows.
</Callout>
