# Quick Sort: Pick a Pivot

[Merge sort](/courses/algorithm-design-and-analysis/merge-sort#merge-sort-in-one-paragraph) does the easy part first (split blindly down the middle) and the hard part last (merge carefully). Quick sort reverses that order. It does the careful work **up front**, so afterwards there's nothing left to combine.

## The idea

1. Pick one element, the **pivot**.
2. **Partition** the array: move everything smaller than the pivot to its left and everything bigger to its right. The pivot is now in its **final position** and will never move again.
3. Quick sort the left part, then quick sort the right part.

There's no combine step. Once both sides are sorted the whole array is sorted, because everything on the left is smaller than the pivot and everything on the right is bigger. All the work happens in partitioning.

It's still [divide and conquer](/courses/algorithm-design-and-analysis/merge-sort#divide-and-conquer): divide is "partition," conquer is "recurse," and combine is empty.

## Partitioning with two pointers

There are several ways to partition. The lab uses the **first element as the pivot** and two pointers walking towards each other:

- `right` starts at the end and moves left **while** its element is ≥ the pivot (that element is already on the correct side).
- Then `left` starts at the beginning and moves right **while** its element is ≤ the pivot. Its first step passes over the pivot itself.
- When both have stopped, `right` is on something too small for the right side and `left` is on something too big for the left side. **Swap them**, which puts both on the correct side, and keep going.
- When the pointers meet, swap the pivot from the front into the meeting point.

The purple bar is the pivot. Each partition ends with one bar turning mint, meaning that pivot is locked into its final spot. Then the window shrinks to one side and the same thing happens on a smaller piece.

### Why `right` has to move first

At the end, the pivot swaps with whatever sits where the pointers met. That value goes to the front, onto the left side, so it must be **no bigger than the pivot**. Moving `right` first guarantees it: `right` only stops on a value smaller than the pivot, and if it runs into `left` instead, `left` is standing on the pivot or on a small value it already swapped in.

Move `left` first and it can stop on a big value with nothing left for `right` to do, so the final swap throws that big value to the front. With [25, 10, 30], `left` walks past 25 and 10 and stops on 30, where it meets `right`. The swap gives 30, 10, 25: the 30 ended up left of 25. That's the bug students most often write. (With a **last**-element pivot it's the other way round: `left` moves first.)

### The notes' version: loc, left and right

The notes trace a different two-pointer method on the array 25, 10, 30, 15, 20, 28. Here the pivot doesn't wait at the front. It **moves with every swap**, and `loc` always points at it. Whichever pointer isn't on the pivot does the walking:

- When `loc` = `left`, walk `right` leftwards while `a[loc] < a[right]`. When `a[loc] > a[right]`, swap them, and `loc` moves to `right`.
- When `loc` = `right`, walk `left` rightwards while `a[left] < a[loc]`. When `a[left] > a[loc]`, swap them, and `loc` moves to `left`.
- Stop when `loc`, `left` and `right` all point at the same place.

| Step | What happens | Array | loc, left, right |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| start | pivot 25 is at the front | **25** 10 30 15 20 28 | 0, 0, 5 |
| 1 | 25 < 28, move `right` | **25** 10 30 15 20 28 | 0, 0, 4 |
| 2 | 25 > 20, swap; pivot goes right | 20 10 30 15 **25** 28 | 4, 0, 4 |
| 3 | 20 < 25, move `left` | 20 10 30 15 **25** 28 | 4, 1, 4 |
| 4 | 10 < 25, move `left` | 20 10 30 15 **25** 28 | 4, 2, 4 |
| 5 | 30 > 25, swap; pivot goes left | 20 10 **25** 15 30 28 | 2, 2, 4 |
| 6 | 25 < 30, move `right` | 20 10 **25** 15 30 28 | 2, 2, 3 |
| 7 | 25 > 15, swap; pivot goes right | 20 10 15 **25** 30 28 | 3, 2, 3 |
| 8 | 15 < 25, move `left` | 20 10 15 **25** 30 28 | 3, 3, 3 |

All three meet at index 3, so 25 is in its final place, with 20, 10, 15 on its left and 30, 28 on its right. The lab's method, in the visualizer above, also puts 25 at index 3, but leaves the left side as 15, 10, 20. Both are correct: partitioning only promises which **side** each element is on, not the order within a side.

The notes also give the textbook's pseudocode for the same idea, named **Hoare partition** after Tony Hoare, who invented quick sort. It is the lab's two-pointer method written with `repeat … until` loops. Here is the textbook's top level, where `s` is the **split position**, the pivot's final index:

```text
ALGORITHM Quicksort(A[l..r])
if l < r
    s ← Partition(A[l..r])     // s is a split position
    Quicksort(A[l..s − 1])
    Quicksort(A[s + 1..r])
```

## Analysis: it depends on the pivot

Partitioning a piece of size $n$ takes $O(n)$: each pointer step does one comparison, and together the pointers cross the piece once. The running time depends on *how evenly* the pivot splits the array.

**Good case: the pivot lands near the middle.** Each partition cuts the problem roughly in half. As with merge sort, that gives about $\log_2 n$ levels of $O(n)$ work each, so **$O(n \log n)$**. This is what usually happens on random data, so the **average case is $O(n \log n)$**.

**Bad case: the pivot is always the smallest or biggest element.** Then one side is empty and the other has $n - 1$ elements, so $O(n)$ work has made the problem only one element smaller. Do that $n$ times:

$$
n + (n-1) + (n-2) + \dots + 1 = O(n^2)
$$

With a first-element pivot, this happens on data that's **already sorted in ascending order**, which is the case the notes name: every pivot is the smallest remaining element, so `right` walks all the way down and the left side is empty. Reverse-sorted data is just as bad, since every pivot is then the largest. Try `1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8` in the visualizer and watch every partition peel off just one element.

The notes write the worst case as $f(n) = n \times (n-1)$. The exact count is the sum above, about $n^2/2$, but both are $O(n^2)$, which is the answer that matters. Likewise their best case, $f(n) = n \times \log_2 n$, describes the ideal where every pivot splits its piece exactly in half.

So the input that's best for [insertion sort](/courses/algorithm-design-and-analysis/insertion-sort#the-idea) is the worst for naive quick sort.

### Picking a better pivot

The fix is to choose the pivot more carefully. Common choices:

- **First** or **last** element: simple, but bad on sorted input. The lab uses the first.
- **Middle** element: handles sorted input well.
- **Random** element: makes the worst case extremely unlikely on *any* input.
- **Median of three**: the median of the first, middle and last elements. A cheap way to avoid bad pivots.

<Callout tone="violet" label="Quick sort vs merge sort">
Merge sort is always $O(n \log n)$ and [stable](/courses/algorithm-design-and-analysis/selection-and-bubble-sort#stability), but needs $O(n)$ extra memory. Quick sort is $O(n \log n)$ on average and $O(n^2)$ in the worst case, and it isn't stable. It sorts **in place**, though, and is usually faster in practice because it moves less data around and uses the CPU cache well.
</Callout>

## Strengths and weaknesses

Quick sort is often the fastest general-purpose sort in practice. It sorts in place. The notes say it needs "no temporary memory", meaning no second array like merge sort's; it still uses $O(\log n)$ extra space on average for the [recursion stack](/courses/algorithm-design-and-analysis/counting-steps#space-is-a-cost-too). It's also easy to parallelize, since after partitioning the two sides are completely independent.

Against that, its worst case is $O(n^2)$, and it isn't stable: the long-distance swaps can reorder equal elements.

Advanced Algorithms proves the expected running time when the pivot is chosen at random, in [randomized quicksort](/courses/advanced-algorithms/quicksort#randomized-quicksort).

## The lab version

```java
public static void quickSort(int[] array, int lowIndex, int highIndex) {
    if (lowIndex >= highIndex) return;

    int pivot = array[lowIndex];
    int pivotIndex = partition(array, lowIndex, highIndex, pivot);
    quickSort(array, lowIndex, pivotIndex - 1);
    quickSort(array, pivotIndex + 1, highIndex);
}

public static int partition(int[] array, int lowIndex, int highIndex, int pivot) {
    int leftPointer = lowIndex;
    int rightPointer = highIndex;

    while (leftPointer < rightPointer) {
        while (array[rightPointer] >= pivot && leftPointer < rightPointer)
            rightPointer--;
        while (array[leftPointer] <= pivot && leftPointer < rightPointer)
            leftPointer++;
        swap(array, leftPointer, rightPointer);
    }
    swap(array, lowIndex, rightPointer);
    return rightPointer;  // pivot's final index
}
```

Call it as `quickSort(array, 0, array.length - 1)`. Then run the experiment that shows the worst case: time it on 50,000 random numbers, then on 50,000 numbers that are already sorted. If the sorted run crashes with a `StackOverflowError`, you've hit the worst case: $n$ levels of recursion instead of $\log n$.
