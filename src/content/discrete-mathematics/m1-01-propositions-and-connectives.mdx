# Propositions and Connectives

Every `if` statement you've written is a small piece of logic. `if (user.isAdmin || post.authorId === user.id)` decides who may edit a post, and if that condition is wrong, the wrong people can. Logic is the study of conditions like that one: how to build them, how to evaluate them and how to tell when two of them mean the same thing.

## Propositions

A **proposition** is a statement that is either true or false, never both and never neither.

- "7 is prime." True.
- "Paris is in Spain." False, but still a proposition.
- "x > 3." Not a proposition yet. Its truth depends on $x$, which the [lesson on quantifiers](/courses/discrete-mathematics/predicates-and-quantifiers) deals with.
- "Close the door." Not a proposition. A command is neither true nor false.

We name propositions with letters, usually $p$, $q$ and $r$, the way code names a boolean with a variable. Then we combine them with **connectives**, the operators of logic.

## The five connectives

| Name | Symbol | Read as | In code | True when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Negation | $\lnot p$ | not $p$ | `!p` | $p$ is false |
| Conjunction | $p \land q$ | $p$ and $q$ | `p && q` | both are true |
| Disjunction | $p \lor q$ | $p$ or $q$ | `p \|\| q` | at least one is true |
| Implication | $p \to q$ | if $p$ then $q$ | `!p \|\| q` | $p$ is false, or $q$ is true |
| Biconditional | $p \leftrightarrow q$ | $p$ if and only if $q$ | `p === q` | they have the same value |

These are the same operations as the NOT, AND and OR [logic gates](/courses/logic-design/logic-gates) in hardware. A gate is a connective built out of transistors, and $1$ and $0$ play the parts of true and false.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Or means at least one">
In everyday English, "tea or coffee?" usually means one but not both. In logic, $p \lor q$ is also true when both are true. The "one but not both" version has its own name, **exclusive or** (XOR), written $p \oplus q$.
</Callout>

## Implication, the one that trips everyone up

$p \to q$ makes a promise: *whenever $p$ is true, $q$ is true too.* It is false in exactly one case, when $p$ is true and $q$ is false, because that's the only case where the promise is broken.

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \to q$ |
|---|---|---|
| T | T | T |
| T | F | F |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | T |

The last two rows are the ones people argue with. Take the promise "if it rains, I'll bring an umbrella." On a dry day you might bring one or not. Either way you haven't broken your word, so the statement counts as true. An implication with a false $p$ is called **vacuously true**.

You rely on this all the time without noticing. A test that says "every item in the list is positive" passes on an empty list, because there is no item that could break the promise.

In $p \to q$, $p$ is the **hypothesis** and $q$ the **conclusion**. Three related statements come up so often they have names:

- The **converse** is $q \to p$.
- The **inverse** is $\lnot p \to \lnot q$.
- The **contrapositive** is $\lnot q \to \lnot p$.

Only the contrapositive always means the same as the original. "If it's a square, it has four sides" is true; its converse "if it has four sides, it's a square" is false (think of a rectangle). The [next lesson](/courses/discrete-mathematics/equivalence-and-laws#the-contrapositive) proves this with a truth table.

<Callout tone="sky" label="Necessary and sufficient">
$p \to q$ is also read "$p$ is **sufficient** for $q$" and "$q$ is **necessary** for $p$." Having a valid ticket is necessary to board a plane, but not sufficient: you also need ID. $p \leftrightarrow q$ says each is both necessary and sufficient for the other.
</Callout>

## Precedence

Like $\times$ before $+$ in arithmetic, connectives have an order: $\lnot$ binds tightest, then $\land$, then $\lor$, then $\to$, then $\leftrightarrow$. So

$$
\lnot p \land q \lor r \;\text{ means }\; ((\lnot p) \land q) \lor r.
$$

Implication groups to the right: $p \to q \to r$ means $p \to (q \to r)$. Most programming languages follow the same order for `!`, `&&` and `||`. When in doubt, add brackets. The reader of your code will thank you.

## Tautologies and contradictions

A formula that is true on every row is a **tautology**. $p \lor \lnot p$ is the simplest: whatever $p$ is, one side holds. A formula false on every row is a **contradiction**, like $p \land \lnot p$. Everything else is a **contingency**: its value depends on the inputs.

Try $(p \to q) \land p \to q$ in the table above. It's a tautology called *modus ponens*: if $p$ implies $q$, and $p$ is true, then $q$ is true. Every proof you'll read uses this step.
