# The Problem With How You Study

## On the Memory of This Course

I have a sister named Luzan. She is my eldest sister, and as a child I was her opposite in almost every way: playful, barely studying, average all the way through school, never really committing to what the system wanted from me. I was good at making teachers and classmates happy. I could perform well socially, and I could get assignments done. Exams were a different thing. They had a weight I did not know what to do with.

My mother used to mention Luzan whenever I stumbled, as a reference point I could not ignore. She would tell me that even as a little girl, during exam season, when the other students were panicking and cramming and barely sleeping, Luzan would be watching television. Calm. She wasn't indifferent, she was a good student, but she was genuinely unbothered. That continued through university. Girls around her would be losing sleep and she would be having a normal evening.

I heard this story so many times it started to sound like a myth, something you tell children to make them feel worse, or a rare quality some people are born with and others are not. By the time I was old enough to watch Luzan's life up close, she had already finished university, so I never saw it in real time.

I kept thinking about it, though. As I got older and started paying attention to how people perform under pressure, in exams and in anything else that matters, I came to see what Luzan had as a relationship with pressure that most people never build, because nobody shows them how. Fear did not move her. And that can be learned.

That realization is part of what made me want to write this course.

## Studying That Feels Productive

You probably already know how to study. Read the material. Make notes. Highlight. Maybe re-read. Maybe make a summary. Repeat until the exam.

This is what I like to call studying that "feels productive." It mostly isn't.

That has nothing to do with you being lazy or doing it wrong in some obvious way. The way most students study is built around a feeling more than a result. The feeling is *familiarity*. The result you actually need is *recall*. Confusing the two is where most exam performance is lost.

## The Asset (Memory)

A German psychologist named [Hermann Ebbinghaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Ebbinghaus) spent years memorizing nonsense syllables, sequences like "DAX" and "BOK", and then testing himself at different intervals to see how fast he forgot them. Nobody had done this before. The result is now called the **forgetting curve**, and it is one of the most replicated findings in psychology.


![Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve](https://res.cloudinary.com/dkmutnahg/image/upload/v1778579659/forgetting-curve-mosalingua_q1fipb.jpg)
[Source: MosaLingua](https://www.mosalingua.com/)

Without any review, you forget roughly half of what you learned within the first hour. By the next day, you remember maybe 30%. Within a week, almost nothing.

It surprises me that this was in 1885. We have known it for over a century, and people still do the same exam preparation, reading everything the night before, as if this curve did not exist.

<Callout tone="orange" label="The core problem">
Re-reading feels like studying because the material looks familiar. Being able to retrieve something under pressure is a different skill. When you re-read, your brain is recognizing, and the exam asks you to recall.
</Callout>

## How Memory Actually Works

Your memory is not a storage room where you put things and later take them out **unchanged**. It's closer to a muscle: it gets stronger the more you *use* it, and weaker when you only *look* at it.

Every time you successfully recall something, pulling it out of your memory without seeing it in front of you, you strengthen the connection. Every time you simply re-read it, you strengthen nothing, because the information is right there and your brain does not need to do the work. It just scans.

This is called **[retrieval practice](https://psychology.ucsd.edu/undergraduate-program/undergraduate-resources/academic-writing-resources/effective-studying/retrieval-practice.html)**, and the research on it is overwhelming. We were taught that testing yourself is a way to check what you know, but testing yourself is also how you learn. The effort of reaching for an answer, even when you get it wrong, is what makes it stick.

There is a second mechanism too: **spacing**. Reviewing material across several sessions, with time between them, works far better than spending the same total time on it in one sitting. I do this a lot and it makes my life ten times easier. The explanation is that your brain consolidates memory during rest, and cramming the night before skips that consolidation entirely.

<Callout tone="violet" label="The two mechanisms that actually work">
**Retrieval practice:** test yourself instead of rereading.

**Spaced repetition:** spread your sessions over days, not hours.

Together, these two are the most researched study interventions in educational psychology.
</Callout>

## Why You Keep Studying the Wrong Way

Don't take this as a character flaw. There is a reason it keeps happening.

When you reread a chapter and it feels familiar, your brain sends a signal that says *I know this*. You know that signal well, and it feels good, like progress. It has a name: **the fluency illusion**. The easier information is to process, the more confident you feel that you know it, even when that confidence has nothing to do with what you will actually retain.

Testing yourself is uncomfortable by comparison. You reach for something and it is not there, and you feel like you do not know the material, with all the frustration that comes with it. That feeling is the learning happening, but it feels like failure, so students avoid it. I did too.

For a long time I thought exam performance came down to how much you study. It turns out to depend on how you study, when you study, and, just as much, what happens in your mind the moment the exam is in front of you. The next lessons cover all three.
</Callout>
