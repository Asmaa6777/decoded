# The One-Week Plan

There is no perfect plan. I want to say that first, because the internet is full of color-coded weekly schedules that look very organized and have nothing to do with how exams feel when you are inside them.

The way we work, the time we have, almost everything varies from one person to the next, so pinning down a single schedule isn't wise. What I can give you are principles, things that hold regardless of your subject, your schedule, or how badly your preparation has gone so far. The plan you build has to be yours, but it should rest on something real.

## Before You Plan Anything: Build the Map

Before I touch any material, I get a picture of the whole subject in my head. I skip the details and the equations and look only at its shape: what it is fundamentally about, how many topics it has, and roughly how much weight each one carries.

This sounds like a small step you could skip. It isn't.

When you sit down to study something without this picture, your brain is doing two jobs at once: learning the material, and trying to figure out what the material even is. The second job runs in the background the whole time and adds a layer of stress that has nothing to do with the content. You feel overwhelmed because you cannot see the subject's edges, since nobody ever told you what it is about, and the subject itself may be perfectly manageable.

A practical way to do this is to take the course name, the outline, the table of contents, or whatever map the subject gives you, and read it in one sitting without studying anything. Just understand the structure. How many main topics are there? What connects them? What is the central idea the whole subject is built around? LLMs can help with this today.

<Callout tone="violet" label="The Feynman test">
Take the course's main idea and each main topic and try to explain them out loud, in your own words, as simply as possible, as if you were talking to someone who has never heard of them. The point is to test yourself. If you cannot do it without vague language, you do not understand it yet, and that is where you start.
</Callout>

This matters even more for technical subjects. A student of numerical analysis who memorizes methods without realizing that the whole subject is about *approximation* (finding values close enough to be useful when exact values are impossible) will struggle with every new type of problem. A student who understands that core idea has a framework that catches almost everything else underneath it.

The map does not take long: an hour, maybe two if you are slow. I never skip it, and it saves far more time than that later.

## Now Plan the Week

Once you have the map, you can actually plan. This is how I think about it.

**Start with a checklist.** Write down every topic you need to cover, all of it. One of the main sources of stress during finals is the background feeling of not knowing how much is left, more than the studying itself. A visible checklist removes that. You cross things off and watch the load get smaller. Most important, you can estimate and plan your time from it.

**Follow the exam order and plan backwards from the exam dates.** It sounds obvious, but it's worth confirming: the first exam on your schedule gets the most preparation time. Don't start with the last one because you like it more, because it feels easier, or because it's the hardest. Study in the order the calendar gives you and fit the rest around it.

**If one subject is very large and its exam is not first**, slice it. Block some hours for it across the days you are preparing for other subjects. A subject that gets two focused hours every other day for a week is in much better shape than one that gets eight hours the night before. We covered [why](./01-the-problem-with-how-you-study.mdx#your-brain-consolidates-during-rest) in the first lesson: your brain consolidates during rest, and you need that to happen more than once.

**Prioritize by two things:** the topics that appear most often in past exams, and the topics where you are weakest. They are not always the same list, and both matter. A topic you understand well that rarely appears is not where your time should go. A topic you don't understand that shows up every year is.

<Callout tone="orange" label="A practical shortcut">
Take the past two to five years of exams for the subject and give them to an LLM. Ask it to identify the topics that appear most consistently across years. Exams are written by people with habits, and those habits show up in the archive.
</Callout>

## The Environment Is Not a Detail

Some people use techniques for this, and Pomodoro is a good one. I don't use it personally; I study in one-hour blocks and that works for me. What I am very firm about is taking the phone out of the room entirely. Turning it face down doesn't count if you still pick it up every five minutes.

The [research](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/691462) here is clear: just having a smartphone on the desk, even switched off, reduces your available cognitive capacity. Your brain spends a small but real amount of attention resisting the urge to check it, and that attention is not available for studying.

There's a related idea called **attentional residue**, which clicked for me when I learned it a year ago: part of your mind stays attached to something else while you try to focus on the task in front of you. The phone does not have to be on for this to happen. It just has to be there.

Remove it, and use website blockers if you need them. Protect the hours you actually sit down to work, because there are fewer of them than it feels like.

## What to Do the Night Before

Very little that is new.

The night before an exam, or the last few hours, are not for learning. I hate to break it to you, but your professor was right: that time is for light review, going over your notes, testing yourself on things you already know, and confirming your understanding. If you run into something completely unfamiliar the night before, do not try to learn it from scratch. Flag it, note that it exists, and move on. New material picked up under stress the night before rarely survives to the exam.

What you actually need the night before is sleep. That part is biology. Memory consolidation happens during sleep, especially in its deep stages. Staying up until three in the morning to read the same chapter a fourth time trades the consolidation of everything you already know for a few hours of anxious scanning. And I think you know what it's like to sit an exam on no sleep.

<Callout tone="mint" label="The honest summary">
Build the map. Make the checklist. Study in the order the calendar demands, not the order you're comfortable with. Protect your focus hours. Sleep before the exam. None of this is revolutionary, but most people ignore at least two of these, and that is where the marks go.
</Callout>
