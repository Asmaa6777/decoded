# Stress & Anxiety

You studied. You slept. You knew the material the night before. Then you sat down in that room, read the first question, and something happened. The material seemed to go somewhere else, or start to fade. You knew that you knew it, but it wasn't there, and you lost your grip on what you thought you understood. You started overthinking it, and fearing it.

Once you understand what happened there, exams feel different, and so does the way you prepare for them.

## Introducing Cortisol

When your brain detects a threat, whether it's a dog running towards you or an exam paper, it triggers the release of cortisol. Cortisol is a steroid hormone produced by your adrenal glands, and its job is to prepare your body to deal with the threat in front of it. You feel alert, your heart rate goes up, and blood flow shifts toward your muscles. You might feel restless or tense as your attention narrows.

One way to learn to spot cortisol is by logging your emotions. I have been using the [How We Feel app](https://howwefeel.org/) for around four years now, and it took me years to connect "restless" and "tense" to cortisol. An Apple Watch helped too, since some apps track your stress levels. With both together, I got much better at noticing when I'm stressed and bringing it down.

*Screenshot from my account*

Nothing about that system is broken. It kept your ancestors alive. It was just built for a different kind of threat. When the threat is a lion, narrowed attention and physical readiness are exactly what you need. When the threat is a question about numerical methods, they are the last thing you need.

The prefrontal cortex, the part of your brain responsible for complex reasoning, working memory and retrieving information, is suppressed under high cortisol. It isn't damaged and nothing is erased; it's **suppressed**. Your brain is running the right program for the wrong situation.

<Callout tone="orange" label="What blanking actually is">
When your mind suddenly feels empty in an exam, and you're staring at a question you studied but nothing comes out, that's cortisol. It has narrowed your attention and partly suppressed the prefrontal cortex. The information is still there, but your access to it is temporarily reduced. It's a physical event, and it doesn't mean you don't know the material.
</Callout>

## Balancing Extremes of Anxiety

Cortisol doesn't simply make things worse as it rises. The relationship between stress and performance follows what psychologists call the **Yerkes-Dodson curve**, an inverted U.

![Yerkes-Dodson curve](https://res.cloudinary.com/dkmutnahg/image/upload/v1778893904/graph.002_ayktx3.png)
[Image Source: Diamond D.M., et al. (2007)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1906714/)

With too little stress you perform poorly because you aren't alert or motivated. With too much you perform poorly because your system is overwhelmed. Peak performance sits in the middle: enough pressure to focus, not enough to flood the system.

So a small amount of exam anxiety is on your side, because it keeps you sharp. The trouble starts when anxiety crosses into the high-cortisol zone and your body starts treating the exam like a survival situation.

Struggling under pressure is usually not a sign of weakness. Students struggle because they don't realize the goal is a balance between two extremes. They read every spike of anxiety as evidence that something is wrong, which produces more anxiety and pushes cortisol even higher, and the spiral feeds itself.

## Negativity Bias

A second problem during exams makes the cortisol and anxiety worse: **negativity bias**.

Your brain doesn't treat all information equally. It gives negative experiences more weight, more attention and more space in memory than positive ones. In an exam, that means your brain is working against your own sense of how you did.

You might finish an exam having answered nineteen questions well and one badly. On the walk home, you replay the one you got wrong. You reconstruct it, overthink it, and catastrophize about what it means for your grade. The nineteen good answers are in your memory too, but they carry no weight, while the one wrong answer set off an emotional response with real texture to it.

This is built into how the brain processes experience, and knowing about it lets you work around it.

<Callout tone="violet" label="The negativity bias in real time">
When you leave an exam convinced you failed, ask yourself: *Am I remembering everything, or only what went wrong?* Your brain will always make the negative more available than the positive, so account for that before you reach a conclusion.
</Callout>

## "I Have Never Seen This Before"

This is where most of the damage happens. You hit a question that looks completely unfamiliar, and your first reaction is usually panic.

What happens next is often the wrong thing: freezing, spending too long on that one question, or deciding the exam is lost and giving up on the rest.

Exams from serious institutions almost always contain questions that most students won't fully solve. They are there to separate grades, not to trick you, so a question you cannot answer is a normal part of how the exam is designed. The students who do best move past it without letting it spill into the rest of their performance.

The practical response is mechanical: **mark it, leave it, continue**, and come back at the end if there's time. That's hard to do under cortisol, because your brain wants to resolve the threat before moving on. Practicing it, or even just knowing it's your plan, makes it easier to reach for when you need it.

<Callout tone="mint" label="The reframe that actually helps">
An unfamiliar question means the exam is doing its job, and says little about how prepared you are. Your job is to do well on everything else and not let one hard question borrow time and attention from the ones you can answer.
</Callout>

## What You Can Actually Do

**Before the exam:** the most effective way to keep cortisol down is good preparation. Retrieval practice builds real confidence, based on evidence that you can recall the material. Good sleep matters too.

**In the exam room:** if you feel a spike of anxiety, the most effective physical fix is a long, slow exhale, which activates the parasympathetic nervous system. The best-studied version is the **physiological sigh**: two inhales through the nose, the second one topping up the first, then a long exhale through the mouth. Andrew Huberman's lab at Stanford has studied it.

**After the exam:** put a number on your negativity bias. Ask yourself *how many questions did I handle reasonably well?* and force an estimate. It will almost always be higher than your mood suggests.
