# Signals: Why Computers Use Only Two Values

In electronic hardware, an electric **signal** is a physical quantity, specifically an electric voltage or current, that varies over time to carry data or instructions through a [circuit](/courses/logic-design/signals).

**Voltage** (measured in volts, $\text{V}$) is the electrical potential difference between two conductors. It acts like electrical pressure pushing electrons through a wire. In digital systems, every voltage is measured relative to a common zero-volt reference line called **ground** ($\text{GND}$, $0\text{ V}$).

A dimmer switch moves smoothly across a continuous range of brightness levels, from completely off to fully bright. A standard wall switch has only two stable positions: ON and OFF.

An **analog** signal works like the dimmer switch: it changes continuously over time and can take any value within a range. When sound waves hit a microphone diaphragm, the microphone produces a smooth, changing voltage that follows the air pressure.

A **digital** signal works like the standard switch: it uses a separate set of values. In binary digital systems, the signal uses only two states, labeled **HIGH** and **LOW**, **1** and **0**, or **TRUE** and **FALSE**.

```
Analog (Continuous Voltage):
   Voltage
      ^    _ . - . _
      |  /           \       _
      | /             \     / \
      |/               \- -/   \
      +----------------------------> Time

Digital (Discrete Binary Levels):
   Voltage
      ^   +-------+       +---+
HIGH -|---|       |       |   |
      |   |       |       |   |
 LOW -+---+       +-------+   +----> Time
```

Most electronic systems move between both realms:

- A **DAC** (digital-to-analog converter) turns stored bits into a smooth voltage that drives headphones or a loudspeaker.
- An **ADC** (analog-to-digital converter) converts continuous analog voltages into digital numbers in two steps:
  1. **Sampling:** Measuring the analog voltage at regular time intervals. You can explore how audio signals are converted in [sampling](/courses/computational-mathematics/sound-and-sampling#sampling-what-44100-per-second-means).
  2. **Quantization:** Rounding each measured analog voltage to the nearest allowable binary value.

## Why two values?

Why did computer engineers build computing machines out of only two values instead of ten, or twenty?

Electricity in real circuits is noisy. In electronics, **noise** is an unwanted electrical fluctuation added to a signal. Real circuits suffer from several physical noise sources:
- **Thermal noise (Johnson-Nyquist noise):** Random movement of electrons inside copper wires and silicon resistors caused by heat.
- **Electromagnetic interference (EMI):** Wires act as antennas, picking up electromagnetic radiation from $50\text{ Hz}$ or $60\text{ Hz}$ AC power lines, radio signals, electric motors, and power supplies.
- **Capacitive and inductive coupling:** Rapidly changing signals on neighboring copper traces on a **printed circuit board** (PCB), an insulating board with copper pathways connecting components, couple energy into adjacent lines.

Because of this interference, the voltage arriving at the receiving end of a wire rarely matches the exact voltage sent by the transmitter.

### The fragility of decimal hardware

If a circuit represented the ten decimal digits $0$ through $9$ using ten voltage steps between $0.0\text{ V}$ and $5.0\text{ V}$, each step would sit only $0.5\text{ V}$ apart:
- Digit 0: $0.0\text{ V}$
- Digit 1: $0.5\text{ V}$
- Digit 2: $1.0\text{ V}$
- Digit 3: $1.5\text{ V}$
- Digit 4: $2.0\text{ V}$
- ...
- Digit 9: $4.5\text{ V}$

The decision boundary between digit 3 and digit 4 would sit halfway between their nominal voltages:

$$V_{threshold} = \frac{1.5\text{ V} + 2.0\text{ V}}{2} = 1.75\text{ V}$$

If the transmitter outputs digit 3 at $1.50\text{ V}$, the maximum upward noise spike the wire can tolerate before flipping is:

$$\Delta V_{tolerance} = V_{threshold} - V_{nominal} = 1.75\text{ V} - 1.50\text{ V} = 0.25\text{ V}$$

A small voltage spike of $+0.26\text{ V}$ lifts the wire to $1.76\text{ V}$, passing $1.75\text{ V}$. The receiving gate misreads the digit as 4 instead of 3. In a computer running billions of operations every second, calculations would quickly corrupt.

```
10 Decimal Voltage Levels (Fragile):
   5.0 V -- [ 9 ]
   4.5 V -- [ 8 ]
   4.0 V -- [ 7 ]   <-- A small 0.26 V noise spike flips a 3 into a 4
   3.5 V -- [ 6 ]
   3.0 V -- [ 5 ]
   2.5 V -- [ 4 ]
   2.0 V -- [ 4 ]
   1.75V ---------------- Threshold between 3 and 4
   1.5 V -- [ 3 ]
   1.0 V -- [ 2 ]
   0.5 V -- [ 1 ]
   0.0 V -- [ 0 ]

2 Binary Voltage Bands (Tolerant):
   3.3 V --+
           | HIGH band (Valid 1)
   2.0 V --+
   ------- Forbidden Region (Undefined) -------
   0.8 V --+
           | LOW band (Valid 0)
   0.0 V --+
```

By restricting the system to only two values, engineers establish wide buffer zones called **logic levels**. A circuit does not have to measure an exact voltage; it only needs to detect whether a voltage falls into the upper band or the lower band.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Mistake students make">
**Assuming binary was chosen because base-2 math is mathematically superior.** Ternary (base-3) arithmetic is slightly more efficient in theory, and working decimal computers have existed. Binary became standard because of transistor physics. Building a transistor that acts as a simple ON/OFF switch is much simpler, cheaper, and less sensitive to heat or manufacturing differences than building a switch with three or ten distinct levels.
</Callout>

## Logic levels and threshold voltages

Every digital **integrated circuit** (IC), a network of transistors, diodes, and resistors built onto a single silicon chip, defines voltage boundaries for inputs and outputs.

Four standard parameters govern these boundaries:

1. **$V_{OH(min)}$ (Voltage Output High Minimum):** The lowest voltage guaranteed at the output of a driving gate when sending a logic 1.
2. **$V_{OL(max)}$ (Voltage Output Low Maximum):** The highest voltage guaranteed at the output of a driving gate when sending a logic 0.
3. **$V_{IH(min)}$ (Voltage Input High Minimum):** The lowest input voltage guaranteed to be recognized as a valid logic 1 by a receiving gate.
4. **$V_{IL(max)}$ (Voltage Input Low Maximum):** The highest input voltage guaranteed to be recognized as a valid logic 0 by a receiving gate.

The positive power supply connected to the chip is labeled $V_{CC}$ (in bipolar and TTL circuits) or $V_{DD}$ (in CMOS circuits). The zero-volt reference is labeled $\text{GND}$ ($0\text{ V}$).

```
Logic Level Boundaries:

       Transmitter Output                     Receiver Input
  V_CC +------------------+              V_CC +------------------+
       |   Valid HIGH     |                   |   Valid HIGH     |
V_OH --+------------------+                   |   (Reads 1)      |
       |                  |             V_IH -+------------------+
       |                  |                   |                  |
       |  Output Margin   |                   | Forbidden Region |
       |                  |                   |  (Indeterminate) |
       |                  |             V_IL -+------------------+
V_OL --+------------------+                   |   Valid LOW      |
       |   Valid LOW      |                   |   (Reads 0)      |
   GND +------------------+               GND +------------------+
```

### The forbidden region and shoot-through current

The gap between $V_{IL(max)}$ and $V_{IH(min)}$ is called the **transition region**, **indeterminate region**, or **forbidden region**. If an input voltage sits in this zone, the gate's behavior is electrically undefined.

In **CMOS** (Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor) chips, logic gates are built from pairs of transistors acting as electrical switches:
- A **PMOS** pull-up transistor connects the output to the positive supply rail ($V_{DD}$) when the input is LOW.
- An **NMOS** pull-down transistor connects the output to ground ($\text{GND}$) when the input is HIGH.

Under normal operation, one transistor is ON while the other is completely OFF, drawing almost no DC current from the power supply.

If an input signal sits in the forbidden middle region near $V_{DD}/2$, both the PMOS and NMOS transistors turn partly on at once. This creates a low-resistance path straight from $V_{DD}$ to $\text{GND}$. The resulting surge of current is called **shoot-through current** (or crowbar current). It drains power, drops the supply voltage, and heats the silicon chip enough to damage it permanently.

<Callout tone="sky" label="Why unused CMOS pins must never float">
Because CMOS inputs draw almost no current, an unconnected (floating) input pin acts like a tiny antenna. Stray electric fields charge the pin to a voltage in the forbidden region. This turns both transistors partly on, causing shoot-through current and erratic oscillations. Connect every unused CMOS input directly to $V_{DD}$ or $\text{GND}$.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="orange" label="Mistake students make">
**Expecting a gate to output an error signal or half-voltage when its input is in the forbidden region.** Logic gates have no third output pin for errors. When an input lands in the forbidden region, internal transistor gain forces the output to settle randomly on 0 or 1, or toggle rapidly back and forth.
</Callout>

## Noise margins

The **noise margin** is the difference between the voltage a transmitter guarantees to output and the voltage a receiver needs to read that state correctly. It measures the largest noise voltage a wire can tolerate before the receiver misreads the bit.

Digital circuits have two distinct noise margins:

1. **High-level noise margin ($V_{NH}$):** The margin on a logic 1 line against negative noise spikes:
   $$V_{NH} = V_{OH(min)} - V_{IH(min)}$$
   The transmitter promises at least $V_{OH(min)}$, and the receiver requires at least $V_{IH(min)}$ to recognize a 1. The downward voltage drop the wire can endure before falling below $V_{IH(min)}$ is the difference between these two bounds.

2. **Low-level noise margin ($V_{NL}$):** The margin on a logic 0 line against positive noise spikes:
   $$V_{NL} = V_{IL(max)} - V_{OL(max)}$$
   The transmitter promises not to exceed $V_{OL(max)}$, and the receiver accepts anything up to $V_{IL(max)}$ as 0. The upward voltage spike the wire can endure before rising above $V_{IL(max)}$ is the difference between these two bounds.

The overall noise margin $V_N$ of a logic family is the smaller of the two:

$$V_N = \min(V_{NH}, V_{NL})$$

A digital line must carry both 0s and 1s reliably. If $V_{NL} = 0.4\text{ V}$ and $V_{NH} = 0.9\text{ V}$, a noise spike of $+0.5\text{ V}$ on a LOW line causes an error. The circuit is only as reliable as its weaker margin.

### Comparing standard logic families

Different chip technologies use different supply voltages and tolerate different amounts of noise:

| Logic Family | Technology Type | Supply Voltage ($V_{CC}$) | $V_{OH(min)}$ | $V_{OL(max)}$ | $V_{IH(min)}$ | $V_{IL(max)}$ | $V_{NH}$ | $V_{NL}$ | Overall Margin ($V_N$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **5V Standard TTL** | Bipolar (BJT) | $5.0\text{ V}$ | $2.4\text{ V}$ | $0.4\text{ V}$ | $2.0\text{ V}$ | $0.8\text{ V}$ | $0.4\text{ V}$ | $0.4\text{ V}$ | **$0.4\text{ V}$** |
| **5V CMOS (74HC)** | CMOS (MOSFET) | $5.0\text{ V}$ | $4.4\text{ V}$ | $0.1\text{ V}$ | $3.5\text{ V}$ | $1.5\text{ V}$ | $0.9\text{ V}$ | $1.4\text{ V}$ | **$0.9\text{ V}$** |
| **3.3V LVCMOS** | Low-Voltage CMOS | $3.3\text{ V}$ | $2.4\text{ V}$ | $0.4\text{ V}$ | $2.0\text{ V}$ | $0.8\text{ V}$ | $0.4\text{ V}$ | $0.4\text{ V}$ | **$0.4\text{ V}$** |

5V CMOS provides more than double the noise immunity of 5V TTL ($0.9\text{ V}$ compared to $0.4\text{ V}$). CMOS outputs pull nearly all the way to the supply rails ($4.4\text{ V}$ and $0.1\text{ V}$), while bipolar TTL outputs drop voltage across internal transistors, limiting $V_{OH}$ to roughly $2.4\text{ V}$ to $2.7\text{ V}$.

<Callout tone="mint" label="Interfacing mismatched families: 74HCT">
Connecting a 5V TTL output directly to a 5V 74HC CMOS input causes problems:
- The TTL output guarantees $V_{OH(min)} = 2.4\text{ V}$.
- The 74HC input requires $V_{IH(min)} = 3.5\text{ V}$ ($0.7 \times V_{CC}$).

Because $2.4\text{ V} < 3.5\text{ V}$, a valid TTL HIGH falls directly into the CMOS forbidden region. The receiver cannot reliably read a 1.

To bridge this gap, chip makers created the **74HCT** family: CMOS chips built with modified inputs that match standard TTL thresholds ($V_{IH(min)} = 2.0\text{ V}$, $V_{IL(max)} = 0.8\text{ V}$).
</Callout>

### Worked example: surviving interference

Consider a standard 5V TTL driver connected to a receiving gate over a circuit board trace ($V_{OH(min)} = 2.4\text{ V}$, $V_{OL(max)} = 0.4\text{ V}$, $V_{IH(min)} = 2.0\text{ V}$, $V_{IL(max)} = 0.8\text{ V}$).

**Case 1: Negative noise spike on a HIGH line**
1. The driver outputs a nominal HIGH of $V_{OH} = 2.7\text{ V}$.
2. Electromagnetic coupling induces a negative noise spike of $\Delta V = -0.5\text{ V}$.
3. Voltage arriving at the receiver:
   $$V_{in} = V_{OH} + \Delta V = 2.7\text{ V} - 0.5\text{ V} = 2.2\text{ V}$$
4. Result: Because $2.2\text{ V} \ge V_{IH(min)} = 2.0\text{ V}$, the receiver reads a clean logic 1. The data is preserved.

**Case 2: Positive noise spike on a LOW line**
1. The driver outputs a nominal LOW of $V_{OL} = 0.3\text{ V}$.
2. Inductive coupling induces a positive noise spike of $\Delta V = +0.35\text{ V}$.
3. Voltage arriving at the receiver:
   $$V_{in} = V_{OL} + \Delta V = 0.3\text{ V} + 0.35\text{ V} = 0.65\text{ V}$$
4. Result: Because $0.65\text{ V} \le V_{IL(max)} = 0.8\text{ V}$, the receiver reads a clean logic 0.

**Case 3: Excessive noise causing failure**
1. If the positive noise spike on the LOW line increases to $\Delta V = +0.65\text{ V}$:
   $$V_{in} = 0.3\text{ V} + 0.65\text{ V} = 0.95\text{ V}$$
2. Result: Because $0.95\text{ V} > V_{IL(max)} = 0.8\text{ V}$, the voltage enters the forbidden region ($0.8\text{ V} < 0.95\text{ V} < 2.0\text{ V}$). The receiver output is unpredictable, and data is corrupted.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Mistake students make">
**Inverting the low-level noise margin formula.** Students often write $V_{NL} = V_{OL(max)} - V_{IL(max)}$, which gives a negative number like $0.4\text{ V} - 0.8\text{ V} = -0.4\text{ V}$. Noise margins are always positive tolerances: subtract the driver limit from the receiver threshold ($V_{NL} = V_{IL(max)} - V_{OL(max)} > 0$). Calculating $V_{OH} - V_{OL}$ is also a mistake; that measures the total output voltage swing, not the noise margin.
</Callout>

## Positive and negative logic conventions

In digital electronics, the relationship between electrical voltages and binary numbers is a convention chosen by the system designer:

- **Positive logic (active-HIGH):**
  - $\text{HIGH voltage} \to \text{Logic } 1$ ($\text{TRUE}$, asserted)
  - $\text{LOW voltage} \to \text{Logic } 0$ ($\text{FALSE}$, unasserted)
- **Negative logic (active-LOW):**
  - $\text{LOW voltage} \to \text{Logic } 1$ ($\text{TRUE}$, asserted)
  - $\text{HIGH voltage} \to \text{Logic } 0$ ($\text{FALSE}$, unasserted)

Digital design defaults to positive logic unless stated otherwise. However, active-LOW signals are common for control lines such as system resets ($\overline{\text{RESET}}$ or `RESET_N`), chip selects ($\overline{\text{CS}}$ or `CS#`), and write enables ($\overline{\text{WE}}$). On circuit schematic diagrams, an active-LOW pin is marked by an **inversion bubble** (a small circle) on the gate input or chip pin.

### Push-pull vs open-drain outputs and pull-up resistors

Active-LOW signals are widely used because of how output circuits are built:

```
Push-Pull Output (Totem Pole):         Open-Drain Output with Pull-Up Resistor:
         V_CC                                    V_CC
          |                                       |
       [PMOS] (Pulls UP)                     [Pull-up Resistor R]
          |                                       |
Output ---+                            Output ----+--------- Bus Wire
          |                                       |
       [NMOS] (Pulls DOWN)                     [NMOS] (Only pulls DOWN)
          |                                       |
         GND                                     GND
```

1. **Push-pull outputs (totem pole):** Use an active pull-up transistor and an active pull-down transistor. Connecting two push-pull outputs directly together is forbidden. If Gate A outputs HIGH ($5\text{ V}$) while Gate B outputs LOW ($0\text{ V}$), current flows directly from $V_{CC}$ to $\text{GND}$ through the chips. This short circuit is called **bus contention**, and it burns out the transistors.
2. **Open-drain outputs:** Omit the upper pull-up transistor entirely. The chip can only pull the wire to $\text{GND}$ (LOW) or turn OFF completely, entering a high-impedance state (Hi-Z).
3. **Pull-up resistor:** A single external resistor (typically $2.2\text{ k}\Omega$ to $10\text{ k}\Omega$) connects the shared wire to $V_{CC}$.
   - When all chips on the bus are idle (switches open), the resistor pulls the wire up to $V_{CC}$ (inactive state).
   - If any chip on the bus acts, it turns its NMOS transistor ON, pulling the wire down to $0\text{ V}$ (active-LOW).

This hardware configuration creates a **wired-AND** (or wired-OR) connection. Multiple chips, such as a CPU, a manual push button, and a power monitor, can all drive the same $\overline{\text{RESET}}$ line without damaging each other.

<BusContentionVisualizer />

<Callout tone="mint" label="Fail-safe power-on property">
Active-LOW reset lines provide a natural physical fail-safe. When a computer turns on, power supply voltages take milliseconds to ramp up, and unpowered chips sit near $0\text{ V}$. An active-LOW reset line holds the CPU in reset until $V_{CC}$ stabilizes and the pull-up resistor lifts the line to HIGH, preventing accidental instruction execution during power brownouts.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="orange" label="Mistake students make">
**Confusing active-LOW signals with negative voltages.** Active-LOW signals do not use negative supply rails like $-5\text{ V}$. All voltages remain positive, between $0\text{ V}$ and $+5\text{ V}$ or $+3.3\text{ V}$. "LOW" simply means electrical ground ($0\text{ V}$). Another common lab error is omitting the pull-up resistor on an open-drain bus like I2C. Without the resistor, the line can be pulled LOW, but it floats when released and never returns to HIGH.
</Callout>

## Bits, nibbles, bytes, and words

A single binary digit is called a **bit** (short for *binary digit*), coined by mathematician John Tukey in 1946. Bits are grouped to represent quantities, characters, and instructions:

- **Bit:** 1 binary digit ($0$ or $1$). Can represent $2^1 = 2$ states.
- **Nibble:** A group of 4 bits. Can represent $2^4 = 16$ states ($0$ through $15$), which corresponds to one hexadecimal digit ($0\text{ to }\text{F}$).
- **Byte (or octet):** A group of 8 bits. Can represent $2^8 = 256$ states ($0$ through $255$). The byte is the standard addressable unit of memory in modern computer architectures.
- **Word:** The natural data unit processed by a computer CPU **datapath** (the internal network of registers, ALU, and buses) in a single instruction cycle. On an older 16-bit processor, a word is 16 bits (2 bytes). On a 32-bit processor, a word is 32 bits (4 bytes). On 64-bit architectures, a quadword is 64 bits (8 bytes).

### Positional notation: MSB and LSB

Within any binary string, individual bit positions carry different positional weights:
- **MSB (Most Significant Bit):** The leftmost bit in the binary sequence, which carries the highest power of 2 ($2^{n-1}$).
- **LSB (Least Significant Bit):** The rightmost bit in the binary sequence, which carries the lowest power of 2 ($2^0 = 1$).

For example, in the 8-bit unsigned byte `10000001`:
- $\text{MSB} = 1$, representing $1 \times 2^7 = 128$
- $\text{LSB} = 1$, representing $1 \times 2^0 = 1$
- Total value $= 128 + 1 = 129$

An $n$-bit register can represent $2^n$ unique states. When representing unsigned non-negative integers, the values range from $0$ to $2^n - 1$.

The full rules for translating patterns of bits into integers, negative numbers, and text are explored in the [number systems lesson](/courses/logic-design/number-systems#positional-number-representation-and-the-radix).

<Callout tone="orange" label="Mistake students make">
**Confusing total state count ($2^n$) with maximum value ($2^n - 1$).** An 8-bit byte can represent $2^8 = 256$ distinct combinations, but because counting starts at 0, the maximum representable value is $255$. Confusing $256$ with $255$ causes off-by-one errors and integer overflow bugs in device drivers and hardware counters.
</Callout>

## The clock

Most digital systems follow a repeating pulse train called the **clock**. The clock coordinates when gates evaluate and when storage elements capture new bits.

```
Periodic Clock Waveform:
   Voltage
      ^    <-- t_w -->
      |   +-----------+               +-----------+
HIGH -|---|           |               |           |
      |   |           |               |           |
 LOW -+---+           +---------------+           +-----> Time
          <---------- Period (T) ------>
```

Four fundamental measurements describe a periodic clock signal:

1. **Period ($T$):** The time required to complete one full cycle of the waveform, measured in seconds ($\text{s}$).
2. **Frequency ($f$):** The number of complete cycles per second, measured in hertz ($\text{Hz}$):
   $$f = \frac{1}{T}, \quad T = \frac{1}{f}$$
3. **Pulse width ($t_w$):** The duration during which the pulse remains at its active HIGH voltage level, measured at the $50\%$ amplitude points of the rising and falling edges.
4. **Duty cycle:** The fraction of the total period spent at the active HIGH level, expressed as a percentage:
   $$\text{Duty Cycle} = \frac{t_w}{T} \times 100\%$$

A square wave has a symmetric duty cycle of $50\%$ ($t_w = T/2$). If a clock has a period of $T = 10\text{ ns}$ and is HIGH for $t_w = 2\text{ ns}$, its duty cycle is:

$$\text{Duty Cycle} = \frac{2\text{ ns}}{10\text{ ns}} \times 100\% = 20\%$$

### Standard digital frequency and period conversions

Working with digital hardware requires fast, fluent conversion between clock frequencies and clock periods:

| Frequency ($f$) | Period ($T$) | Common Application |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$1\text{ kHz}$** ($10^3\text{ Hz}$) | **$1\text{ ms}$** ($10^{-3}\text{ s}$) | Keyboard matrix scanning, switch debounce timers |
| **$1\text{ MHz}$** ($10^6\text{ Hz}$) | **$1\ \mu\text{s}$** ($10^{-6}\text{ s}$) | Microcontroller peripherals, I2C fast mode |
| **$10\text{ MHz}$** ($10^7\text{ Hz}$) | **$100\text{ ns}$** ($10^{-7}\text{ s}$) | Ethernet (10BASE-T), SPI buses |
| **$100\text{ MHz}$** ($10^8\text{ Hz}$) | **$10\text{ ns}$** ($10^{-8}\text{ s}$) | FPGA system clocks, SDRAM |
| **$1\text{ GHz}$** ($10^9\text{ Hz}$) | **$1\text{ ns}$** ($10^{-9}\text{ s}$) | Modern CPU clock cycles, DDR memory |
| **$2.5\text{ GHz}$** ($2.5 \times 10^9\text{ Hz}$) | **$0.4\text{ ns}$** ($400\text{ ps}$) | Modern CPU cores, PCIe gen 1 |

*Worked conversion:* If a CPU core operates at $2.5\text{ GHz}$, what is its clock period in picoseconds?
$$T = \frac{1}{2.5 \times 10^9\text{ Hz}} = 0.4 \times 10^{-9}\text{ s} = 0.4\text{ ns} = 400\text{ ps}$$

### Real edges: rise time and fall time

In circuit diagrams, clock transitions look like vertical lines. In physical hardware, voltages cannot change instantly because wires, connector pins, and transistor gates carry parasitic capacitance and inductance that resist sudden changes:
- **Parasitic capacitance ($C$):** Charges and discharges through wire resistance, creating a sloped voltage ramp governed by time constant $\tau = RC$.
- **Parasitic inductance ($L$):** Generates an opposing voltage whenever current changes rapidly ($\Delta V = L \frac{di}{dt}$).

```
Real-World Pulse Transitions:

     Voltage
       ^             Overshoot
       |                .-.
 100% -| - - - - - - - / - \ - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
  90% -| - - - - - - -/-- - - - - - - - -\ - - - - - - - -
       |             /                    \
  50% -| - - - - - -/- - - - - - - - - - - \ - - - - - - -
       |           /                        \
  10% -| - - - - -/ - - - - - - - - - - - - -\- - - - - -
   0% -+---------+                            \-----.-.--> Time
                 |<-t_r->|                    |<-t_f>|  Undershoot
```

Two timing parameters quantify how quickly a real signal switches:

- **Rise time ($t_r$):** The time required for the pulse to transition from $10\%$ to $90\%$ of its steady-state amplitude.
- **Fall time ($t_f$):** The time required for the pulse to transition from $90\%$ to $10\%$ of its steady-state amplitude.

*Why $10\%$ to $90\%$?* The outer $10\%$ tails near ground and $V_{CC}$ curve slowly and are sensitive to noise. Measuring between $10\%$ and $90\%$ isolates the steep, linear switching region.

Transitions are categorized by direction:
- **Rising edge (or leading edge):** The low-to-high transition ($0 \to 1$).
- **Falling edge (or trailing edge):** The high-to-low transition ($1 \to 0$).

These fast transitions govern edge-triggered circuits. In sequential logic, [flip-flops](/courses/logic-design/flip-flops#clock-edge-terminology-and-circuit-symbols) sample their inputs exclusively during one of these edges.

High-speed signals that transition in fractions of a nanosecond can run into signal integrity issues:
- **Overshoot:** The signal surges beyond the steady-state HIGH voltage before settling.
- **Undershoot:** The signal dips below ground ($0\text{ V}$) upon falling.
- **Ringing:** Damped oscillatory voltage ripples following a transition, caused by transmission line impedance mismatches where traveling voltage waves bounce between driver and receiver.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Mistake students make">
**Prefix errors in frequency and period calculations.** Students frequently invert frequency incorrectly, writing $T = 1 / (50\text{ MHz}) = 20\ \mu\text{s}$ instead of $20\text{ ns}$ ($1 / (50 \times 10^6) = 2 \times 10^{-8}\text{ s} = 20\text{ ns}$). Another common error is calculating duty cycle as $t_{low} / T$, or forgetting that period $T$ is the sum of HIGH time and LOW time ($T = t_{high} + t_{low}$).
</Callout>

## Moving bits: serial and parallel transmission

When binary data moves between two chips or across a network, two architectures transport the bits:

```
Parallel Transmission (8 bits in 1 clock cycle):
   Wire 7: Bit 7 ------------------------->
   Wire 6: Bit 6 ------------------------->
   Wire 5: Bit 5 ------------------------->
   Wire 4: Bit 4 ------------------------->
   Wire 3: Bit 3 ------------------------->
   Wire 2: Bit 2 ------------------------->
   Wire 1: Bit 1 ------------------------->
   Wire 0: Bit 0 ------------------------->
   GND:    Common Ground Return ----------->

Serial Transmission (8 bits in 8 clock cycles):
   Single Wire: Bit 0, Bit 1, Bit 2, Bit 3, Bit 4, Bit 5, Bit 6, Bit 7 --->
   GND:         Common Ground Return -------------------------------------->
```

*Note on Ground:* In both serial and parallel transmission, a common ground reference line is strictly required. Voltage is an electrical potential difference between two conductors. Without a shared ground return line, the receiver cannot measure incoming voltage and current cannot loop back to complete the circuit.

### Serial transmission
Bits are sent sequentially, one after another, over a single conductor (plus a shared ground reference line).
- **Advantages:** Requires only one signal wire (or one differential pair), keeping cables thin, connectors compact, pin counts low, and circuit boards uncrowded.
- **Disadvantages:** At equal clock rates, transmitting an 8-bit byte requires eight sequential clock cycles.

### Parallel transmission
All bits of a byte or word are sent simultaneously across multiple physical conductors, with one bit assigned to each wire.
- **Advantages:** A full byte or word transfers in a single clock cycle, providing high throughput at modest clock rates.
- **Disadvantages:** Requires many physical wires, large connectors, and runs into two physical bottlenecks at higher frequencies:
  1. **Clock skew:** Light and electrical signals in circuit board copper travel at roughly $15\text{ cm/ns}$ (about $6.7\text{ ps/mm}$). Microscopic differences in trace lengths cause bits launched at the same instant to arrive at receiving registers at slightly different times.
  2. **Crosstalk:** Rapid current pulses on one parallel trace induce capacitive and inductive voltage spikes on adjacent wires.

<SerialParallel />

<Callout tone="sky" label="Why modern high-speed technology went serial">
For decades, wide parallel buses connected most computer parts: 40-pin and 80-conductor ribbon cables ran to hard drives (IDE), and 32-bit or 64-bit slots connected expansion cards (PCI).

When clock speeds passed $100\text{ MHz}$, clock skew and crosstalk made wide parallel transmission unreliable over distances longer than a few centimeters. The industry replaced wide parallel ribbons with fast point-to-point serial links:
- IDE was replaced by **SATA** (Serial ATA).
- PCI was replaced by **PCI Express** (PCIe).
- Parallel printer ports were replaced by **USB** (Universal Serial Bus).

Modern serial interfaces rely on two techniques:
1. **Differential signaling:** Bits are sent over a twisted pair of complementary wires ($D+$ and $D-$). The receiver measures the voltage difference $(V_{D+} - V_{D-})$. External noise hits both wires equally (common-mode noise) and cancels out completely upon subtraction.
2. **Clock recovery:** The clock is embedded directly into the data stream using line encodings (such as 8b/10b or 128b/130b), eliminating the need for a separate clock wire and removing inter-line skew entirely.

Because serial links carry data over a single lane without inter-line clock skew, they can run at tens of gigahertz (such as PCIe 5.0 at 32 GT/s), delivering vastly higher total bandwidth than wide parallel ribbons ever achieved.
</Callout>

### Worked example: comparing transfer durations

Suppose we need to transfer a $4\text{ KB}$ block of data ($4,096\text{ bytes} = 4,096 \times 8 = 32,768\text{ bits}$) between two subsystems.

1. **Over an 8-bit parallel bus running at $25\text{ MHz}$:**
   - Clock period:
     $$T = \frac{1}{25 \times 10^6\text{ Hz}} = 40 \times 10^{-9}\text{ s} = 40\text{ ns}$$
   - The bus transfers $1\text{ byte}$ (8 bits) per clock cycle.
   - Clock cycles needed: $4,096\text{ cycles}$.
   - Total transmission time:
     $$t_{parallel} = 4,096\text{ cycles} \times 40\text{ ns/cycle} = 163,840\text{ ns} = 163.84\ \mu\text{s}$$

2. **Over a 1-bit serial line running at the same $25\text{ MHz}$:**
   - Transfers $1\text{ bit}$ per clock cycle.
   - Clock cycles needed: $32,768\text{ cycles}$.
   - Total transmission time:
     $$t_{serial} = 32,768\text{ cycles} \times 40\text{ ns/cycle} = 1,310,720\text{ ns} = 1,310.72\ \mu\text{s} \approx 1.31\text{ ms}$$
   - At identical clock rates, the serial line is exactly 8 times slower than the 8-bit parallel bus:
     $$\frac{t_{serial}}{t_{parallel}} = \frac{1,310.72\ \mu\text{s}}{163.84\ \mu\text{s}} = 8.0$$

3. **Over a modern high-speed serial link running at $5\text{ Gbps}$ (USB 3.0):**
   - Transmits $5 \times 10^9\text{ bits per second}$:
     $$t_{usb} = \frac{32,768\text{ bits}}{5 \times 10^9\text{ bits/s}} = 6.5536 \times 10^{-6}\text{ s} = 6.5536\ \mu\text{s}$$
   - Speedup comparison:
     $$\frac{t_{parallel}}{t_{usb}} = \frac{163.84\ \mu\text{s}}{6.5536\ \mu\text{s}} \approx 25.0\times$$
   Because the serial link eliminates clock skew, it can run 200 times faster than the parallel bus ($5\text{ GHz}$ compared to $25\text{ MHz}$), completing the transfer 25 times faster despite using only one data lane.

Data moves inside the CPU along internal [buses](/courses/computer-organization/buses#width-is-everything) connecting registers, the ALU, and the cache.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Mistake students make">
**Assuming more parallel wires always means more bandwidth.** Students often think that a 32-bit or 64-bit parallel bus will always transmit data 32 or 64 times faster than a single serial line. In practice, parallel buses hit a frequency wall at roughly $100\text{ MHz}$ to $133\text{ MHz}$ due to clock skew and crosstalk. A differential serial line clocked at $10\text{ GHz}$ or $32\text{ GHz}$ transfers dramatically more data per second than a wide parallel bus ever could.
</Callout>

#### Problem 1: Noise margin calculation and voltage tolerances

An engineer is evaluating an experimental $3.3\text{ V}$ low-voltage CMOS logic family. The component datasheet provides the following threshold voltage specifications:
- $V_{OH(min)} = 2.8\text{ V}$
- $V_{OL(max)} = 0.3\text{ V}$
- $V_{IH(min)} = 2.0\text{ V}$
- $V_{IL(max)} = 0.8\text{ V}$

1. Calculate the high-level noise margin $V_{NH}$, the low-level noise margin $V_{NL}$, and the overall noise margin $V_N$ of this logic family.
2. A driver gate outputs a nominal LOW voltage of $V_{OL} = 0.25\text{ V}$. An inductive transient from a nearby relay induces a positive noise spike of $\Delta V = +0.65\text{ V}$ onto the trace. What voltage arrives at the receiver, and does this cause a logic error?
3. What is the maximum peak noise spike that could be tolerated on this LOW line before the input enters the indeterminate region?

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Noise margin calculations:**
   - High-level noise margin ($V_{NH}$):
     $$V_{NH} = V_{OH(min)} - V_{IH(min)} = 2.8\text{ V} - 2.0\text{ V} = 0.8\text{ V}$$
   - Low-level noise margin ($V_{NL}$):
     $$V_{NL} = V_{IL(max)} - V_{OL(max)} = 0.8\text{ V} - 0.3\text{ V} = 0.5\text{ V}$$
   - Overall noise margin ($V_N$):
     $$V_N = \min(V_{NH}, V_{NL}) = \min(0.8\text{ V}, 0.5\text{ V}) = 0.5\text{ V}$$

2. **Noise evaluation on the LOW line:**
   - Received input voltage:
     $$V_{in} = V_{OL} + \Delta V = 0.25\text{ V} + 0.65\text{ V} = 0.90\text{ V}$$
   - Comparison with threshold: The maximum allowed LOW voltage is $V_{IL(max)} = 0.80\text{ V}$.
   - Because $V_{in} = 0.90\text{ V} > 0.80\text{ V}$, the signal enters the forbidden region ($0.80\text{ V} < V_{in} < 2.00\text{ V}$). The receiver can no longer guarantee recognition of a valid logic 0, resulting in a logic error or unpredictable switching.

3. **Maximum tolerable noise spike:**
   The maximum tolerable noise on this specific line is:
   $$\Delta V_{max} = V_{IL(max)} - V_{OL} = 0.80\text{ V} - 0.25\text{ V} = 0.55\text{ V}$$
   Any noise spike exceeding $+0.55\text{ V}$ pushes the input past $V_{IL(max)}$.
</ModelAnswer>

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
Remember that the general low-level noise margin formula uses the guaranteed worst-case output $V_{OL(max)}$ ($V_{NL} = V_{IL(max)} - V_{OL(max)} = 0.5\text{ V}$). However, when analyzing a specific circuit instance with a measured output voltage ($0.25\text{ V}$), the margin on that specific physical wire is measured from that operating point ($0.80\text{ V} - 0.25\text{ V} = 0.55\text{ V}$). Never subtract $V_{OL} - V_{IL}$ to get a negative value.
</Callout>

#### Problem 2: Oscilloscope waveform analysis

An oscilloscope probe connected to a digital system's clock line displays a periodic square-wave signal. The horizontal time scale shows that one complete repeating cycle occupies $12.5\text{ ns}$. During each cycle, the signal remains at the logic HIGH voltage level for $3.75\text{ ns}$.

1. What is the clock frequency in megahertz ($\text{MHz}$)?
2. What is the duty cycle percentage of this clock signal?
3. If the clock frequency is increased to $200\text{ MHz}$ while maintaining the same $30\%$ duty cycle, what is the new clock period $T_{new}$ and the new pulse width $t_{w,new}$ in nanoseconds?

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Clock frequency calculation:**
   - Period $T = 12.5\text{ ns} = 12.5 \times 10^{-9}\text{ s}$.
   - Frequency formula:
     $$f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{12.5 \times 10^{-9}\text{ s}} = 0.08 \times 10^9\text{ Hz} = 80 \times 10^6\text{ Hz} = 80\text{ MHz}$$

2. **Duty cycle calculation:**
   - Pulse width $t_w = 3.75\text{ ns}$.
   - Duty cycle formula:
     $$\text{Duty Cycle} = \frac{t_w}{T} \times 100\% = \frac{3.75\text{ ns}}{12.5\text{ ns}} \times 100\% = 0.30 \times 100\% = 30\%$$

3. **Calculations at $200\text{ MHz}$:**
   - New period:
     $$T_{new} = \frac{1}{f_{new}} = \frac{1}{200 \times 10^6\text{ Hz}} = 5 \times 10^{-9}\text{ s} = 5.0\text{ ns}$$
   - New pulse width with $30\%$ duty cycle:
     $$t_{w,new} = T_{new} \times \text{Duty Cycle} = 5.0\text{ ns} \times 0.30 = 1.5\text{ ns}$$
</ModelAnswer>

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
Pay careful attention to metric units and powers of 10. A common mistake is writing $1 / (12.5\text{ ns}) = 0.08\text{ MHz}$ instead of $80\text{ MHz}$. Remember that nanoseconds ($10^{-9}\text{ s}$) invert directly to gigahertz ($10^9\text{ Hz}$): $\frac{1}{12.5\text{ ns}} = \frac{1,000}{12.5}\text{ MHz} = 80\text{ MHz}$.
</Callout>

#### Problem 3: Parallel vs serial transmission bottlenecks

Two integrated circuits on a printed circuit board are connected by an 8-bit parallel bus. Electrical signals travel through the board's copper traces at a propagation speed of $v = 15\text{ cm/ns}$ (which corresponds to a propagation delay of $t_{pd} = \frac{1}{v} \approx 6.67\text{ ps/mm}$). Due to routing around other components on the board, trace 7 is $30\text{ mm}$ longer than trace 0.

1. Calculate the clock skew (propagation delay difference $\Delta t$) between trace 0 and trace 7.
2. If the bus operates at a clock frequency of $33\text{ MHz}$ ($T \approx 30.3\text{ ns}$), what fraction of the clock period is consumed by this skew? Can the parallel bus operate reliably?
3. If an engineer attempts to clock the parallel bus at $2.5\text{ GHz}$ ($T = 400\text{ ps}$), what fraction of the clock period is consumed by this skew? Explain why the bus fails completely.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Clock skew calculation:**
   - Length difference $\Delta L = 30\text{ mm}$.
   - Propagation velocity $v = 15\text{ cm/ns} = 150\text{ mm/ns} = 0.15\text{ mm/ps}$.
   - Skew formula:
     $$\Delta t = \frac{\Delta L}{v} = \frac{30\text{ mm}}{0.15\text{ mm/ps}} = 200\text{ ps} = 0.20\text{ ns}$$

2. **Skew impact at $33\text{ MHz}$:**
   - Clock period $T = \frac{1}{33 \times 10^6\text{ Hz}} \approx 30.3\text{ ns} = 30,300\text{ ps}$.
   - Fraction of period consumed by skew:
     $$\text{Fraction} = \frac{\Delta t}{T} = \frac{0.20\text{ ns}}{30.3\text{ ns}} \times 100\% \approx 0.66\%$$
   - Conclusion: The $200\text{ ps}$ skew consumes less than $1\%$ of the clock period. The receiving flip-flops have ample time to satisfy their setup and hold timing constraints. The bus operates reliably.

3. **Skew impact at $2.5\text{ GHz}$:**
   - Clock period $T = \frac{1}{2.5 \times 10^9\text{ Hz}} = 0.40\text{ ns} = 400\text{ ps}$.
   - Fraction of period consumed by skew:
     $$\text{Fraction} = \frac{\Delta t}{T} = \frac{200\text{ ps}}{400\text{ ps}} \times 100\% = 50\%$$
   - Conclusion: Skew consumes half of the entire clock cycle. When you add the flip-flop's required setup time (typically $50\text{ to }100\text{ ps}$), bits launched during clock cycle $N$ on trace 7 arrive during clock cycle $N+1$ relative to trace 0. The receiver captures bits from two different bytes simultaneously, corrupting all data.
</ModelAnswer>

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
Do not confuse clock skew (arrival time discrepancy across different parallel wires) with propagation delay (the time required for a signal to travel from sender to receiver). Propagation delay merely delays when data arrives; clock skew corrupts the alignment between bits of the same word.
</Callout>

#### Problem 4: Active-LOW vs active-HIGH signals

A microcontroller pin labeled $\overline{\text{RESET}}$ (or `nRESET`) connects to a $+3.3\text{ V}$ power supply through a $10\text{ k}\Omega$ pull-up resistor. An external manual push button connects between the pin and ground ($\text{GND}$, $0\text{ V}$).

1. Does this reset circuit employ positive logic or negative logic? Identify the active voltage and the inactive voltage.
2. Explain two engineering advantages of using an active-LOW open-drain connection for reset signals instead of an active-HIGH push-pull connection.
3. Sizing trade-off: Why can the pull-up resistor not be chosen arbitrarily small (such as $20\ \Omega$) or arbitrarily large (such as $1\text{ M}\Omega$) given that the bus trace has a parasitic capacitance of $C_{bus} = 50\text{ pF}$?

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Logic Convention:**
   - The circuit employs **negative logic (active-LOW)**.
   - **Active (asserted) voltage:** $0\text{ V}$ ($\text{GND}$), which triggers the system reset.
   - **Inactive (unasserted) voltage:** $+3.3\text{ V}$ ($V_{DD}$), which allows the microcontroller to execute instructions normally.

2. **Engineering Advantages:**
   - **Wired-OR multi-driver capability:** Multiple reset sources (a manual push button, a power-supply voltage supervisor, a watchdog timer, and an in-circuit debugger) can all connect to the same physical wire in an open-drain configuration. Any single device can safely pull the line to ground to trigger a reset without causing bus contention or short-circuit currents.
   - **Fail-safe power-on behavior:** During power-up, chips take time to initialize and power supply rails ramp gradually from $0\text{ V}$. Because unpowered or uninitialized devices naturally sit near $0\text{ V}$, the active-LOW line holds the processor safely in reset until power stabilizes and the pull-up resistor brings the line HIGH, preventing accidental code execution.

3. **Pull-Up Resistor Sizing Trade-Off:**
   - **If $R$ is too small (such as $20\ \Omega$):** When the button is pressed or an open-drain transistor pulls the line to ground, current flows through the resistor:
     $$I = \frac{V_{DD}}{R} = \frac{3.3\text{ V}}{20\ \Omega} = 165\text{ mA}$$
     Power dissipated: $P = V \times I = 3.3\text{ V} \times 0.165\text{ A} \approx 0.54\text{ W}$. This heavy current wastes power and can overheat the resistor or exceed the maximum current sinking rating of the open-drain transistor.
   - **If $R$ is too large (such as $1\text{ M}\Omega$):** When the button is released, the line must charge the bus parasitic capacitance $C_{bus} = 50\text{ pF}$ through $R$. The $RC$ time constant is:
     $$\tau = R \times C_{bus} = 10^6\ \Omega \times 50 \times 10^{-12}\text{ F} = 50\ \mu\text{s}$$
     The rise time ($10\%$ to $90\%$) takes $t_r \approx 2.2 \tau = 110\ \mu\text{s}$. During this long interval, the voltage lingers inside the forbidden indeterminate region, causing shoot-through current and false re-triggering. A moderate value like $4.7\text{ k}\Omega$ to $10\text{ k}\Omega$ provides the ideal balance between low power dissipation and fast rise time.
</ModelAnswer>

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
Remember that while an open-drain transistor pulls a line LOW very quickly (low transistor ON-resistance $R_{on} \approx 10\ \Omega$), the rising edge depends entirely on the passive pull-up resistor charging the bus capacitance ($t_r \approx 2.2 \times R_{pullup} \times C_{bus}$). Choosing too large a resistor produces slow rising edges that can cause glitches.
</Callout>

#### Problem 5: Interfacing mismatched logic families (TTL to CMOS)

An embedded systems engineer is interfacing a $5\text{ V}$ standard TTL output with a high-speed CMOS ($74\text{HC}$) peripheral input. The component datasheets specify:
- Standard TTL driver: $V_{OH(min)} = 2.4\text{ V}$, $V_{OL(max)} = 0.4\text{ V}$, $I_{OL(max)} = 16\text{ mA}$, off-state HIGH leakage $I_{OH(leakage)} \le 40\ \mu\text{A}$.
- 74HC CMOS input: $V_{IH(min)} = 3.5\text{ V}$ ($0.7 \times V_{CC}$), $V_{IL(max)} = 1.5\text{ V}$ ($0.3 \times V_{CC}$), input leakage $I_{IH} \le 1\ \mu\text{A}$.

1. Explain why a direct connection fails when transmitting a logic 1. Identify which operating region the voltage lands in, and state the physical failure mechanism inside the CMOS gate.
2. The engineer adds an external pull-up resistor $R_{pullup}$ connected to $+5.0\text{ V}$ ($V_{CC}$) to pull the line up to $5\text{ V}$ during logic HIGH. Calculate the absolute minimum allowable resistance $R_{pullup(min)}$ so the TTL driver's output transistor does not exceed its maximum sinking rating ($I_{OL(max)} = 16\text{ mA}$) when pulling LOW ($V_{OL} = 0.4\text{ V}$).
3. When the TTL output is HIGH, the total combined leakage current drawn by the TTL output stage and the CMOS input is $I_{leakage} = 40\ \mu\text{A} + 1\ \mu\text{A} = 41\ \mu\text{A}$. Calculate the maximum allowable pull-up resistance $R_{pullup(max)}$ that guarantees the node voltage does not fall below $V_{IH(min)} = 3.5\text{ V}$.
4. State why replacing the $74\text{HC}$ peripheral with a $74\text{HCT}$ peripheral completely eliminates the need for an external pull-up resistor.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Direct Connection Failure on Logic 1:**
   - **Threshold mismatch:** The standard TTL output guarantees a minimum HIGH voltage of only $V_{OH(min)} = 2.4\text{ V}$. However, the $74\text{HC}$ CMOS input requires at least $V_{IH(min)} = 3.5\text{ V}$ to recognize a valid logic 1.
   - **Indeterminate region:** Because $2.4\text{ V} < 3.5\text{ V}$ (a shortfall of $1.1\text{ V}$), the TTL output voltage of $2.4\text{ V}$ to $2.7\text{ V}$ falls squarely inside the $74\text{HC}$ forbidden indeterminate region ($1.5\text{ V} \text{ to } 3.5\text{ V}$).
   - **Physical failure mechanism:** In this middle region, both the PMOS pull-up and NMOS pull-down transistors in the CMOS input buffer turn partially ON at the same time. This creates a low-resistance path from $V_{CC}$ to $\text{GND}$, inducing continuous shoot-through current, excessive thermal dissipation, and indeterminate or oscillating internal logic states. Note that LOW transmission operates reliably because $V_{OL(max)} = 0.4\text{ V} \le V_{IL(max)} = 1.5\text{ V}$ with a healthy noise margin of $1.5\text{ V} - 0.4\text{ V} = 1.1\text{ V}$.

2. **Minimum Pull-Up Resistance ($R_{pullup(min)}$):**
   - When the TTL driver outputs logic LOW, its internal pull-down transistor connects the line to ground, holding the node at $V_{OL(max)} = 0.4\text{ V}$.
   - Current flows from the $+5.0\text{ V}$ rail through $R_{pullup}$ into the TTL output pin. The TTL driver can sink at most $I_{OL(max)} = 16\text{ mA} = 0.016\text{ A}$.
   - By Ohm's law, to ensure the sink current does not exceed $16\text{ mA}$:
     $$R_{pullup(min)} = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{OL(max)}}{I_{OL(max)}} = \frac{5.0\text{ V} - 0.4\text{ V}}{0.016\text{ A}} = \frac{4.6\text{ V}}{0.016\text{ A}} = 287.5\ \Omega$$
   - Any pull-up resistance smaller than $287.5\ \Omega$ causes the TTL output to sink more than $16\text{ mA}$, elevating $V_{OL}$ above $0.4\text{ V}$ and overheating the driver transistor.

3. **Maximum Pull-Up Resistance ($R_{pullup(max)}$):**
   - When the TTL driver outputs logic HIGH, its internal pull-up turns off, and the line is pulled up toward $+5.0\text{ V}$ by $R_{pullup}$.
   - The total off-state leakage current pulled from the node is:
     $$I_{leakage} = I_{OH(leakage)} + I_{IH} = 40\ \mu\text{A} + 1\ \mu\text{A} = 41\ \mu\text{A} = 41 \times 10^{-6}\text{ A}$$
   - The voltage drop across $R_{pullup}$ must not pull the input node below $V_{IH(min)} = 3.5\text{ V}$:
     $$\Delta V_{drop(max)} = V_{CC} - V_{IH(min)} = 5.0\text{ V} - 3.5\text{ V} = 1.5\text{ V}$$
   - Therefore:
     $$R_{pullup(max)} = \frac{\Delta V_{drop(max)}}{I_{leakage}} = \frac{1.5\text{ V}}{41 \times 10^{-6}\text{ A}} \approx 36,585\ \Omega \approx 36.6\text{ k}\Omega$$
   - In practice, engineers choose a standard value between $1\text{ k}\Omega$ and $4.7\text{ k}\Omega$, safely above $287.5\ \Omega$ (limiting sink current to $1\text{ to }4.6\text{ mA}$) and well below $36.6\text{ k}\Omega$ to provide sharp rising edges.

4. **The 74HCT Alternative:**
   - The $74\text{HCT}$ logic family uses high-speed CMOS internal architecture but incorporates input transistors sized to match bipolar TTL voltage thresholds ($V_{IH(min)} = 2.0\text{ V}$ and $V_{IL(max)} = 0.8\text{ V}$).
   - Since $V_{OH(min)} = 2.4\text{ V} \ge 2.0\text{ V}$, the TTL HIGH output drives the $74\text{HCT}$ input with a guaranteed $0.4\text{ V}$ noise margin ($2.4\text{ V} - 2.0\text{ V} = 0.4\text{ V}$), eliminating the need for external pull-up resistors, saving board space, and eliminating static power draw.
</ModelAnswer>

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
Remember that CMOS inputs draw virtually zero DC current ($1\ \mu\text{A}$), meaning CMOS can never overload a TTL output's current capacity. The incompatibility is purely a voltage threshold mismatch ($V_{OH} < V_{IH}$). Do not state that the TTL output burns out the CMOS gate from excessive current.
</Callout>

#### Problem 6: ADC resolution, Nyquist sampling, and quantization error

An environmental monitoring station uses an analog vibration sensor. The sensor outputs a continuous signal with frequency content from DC ($0\text{ Hz}$) up to a maximum cutoff frequency of $f_{max} = 24\text{ kHz}$. An analog-to-digital converter (ADC) samples this signal over a full-scale voltage span of $0.0\text{ V}$ to $4.096\text{ V}$ ($V_{FS} = 4.096\text{ V}$).

1. According to the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem, what is the absolute minimum sampling frequency $f_s$ required to digitize this signal without aliasing distortion?
2. If the station uses an 8-bit ADC ($N = 8$), calculate:
   - The total number of discrete quantization levels.
   - The quantization step size (voltage resolution $Q$) in millivolts ($\text{mV}$).
   - The maximum quantization error $\Delta V_{error(max)}$ in millivolts ($\text{mV}$), assuming mid-tread rounding to the nearest discrete code.
3. To improve precision, the station replaces the 8-bit digitizer with a 12-bit ADC ($N = 12$) operating across the same $4.096\text{ V}$ input range.
   - Calculate the new quantization step size $Q_{12}$ in millivolts.
   - By what factor is the quantization voltage error reduced?
   - What is the theoretical improvement in Signal-to-Quantization-Noise Ratio ($\Delta\text{SQNR}$) in decibels ($\text{dB}$)?

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Minimum Nyquist Sampling Rate:**
   - The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem requires that the sampling rate $f_s$ be at least twice the highest frequency component present in the band-limited signal ($f_s \ge 2 f_{max}$).
   - With $f_{max} = 24\text{ kHz}$:
     $$f_{s(Nyquist)} = 2 \times 24\text{ kHz} = 48\text{ kS/s} \text{ (or } 48\text{ kHz)}$$
   - Any sampling frequency below $48\text{ kS/s}$ causes high-frequency vibrations to fold back across $f_s/2$ (the Nyquist folding frequency), creating false low-frequency phantom components (aliasing) that cannot be filtered out after sampling.

2. **8-Bit ADC Quantization Metrics ($N = 8$):**
   - **Discrete levels:**
     $$\text{Levels} = 2^N = 2^8 = 256\text{ levels}$$
   - **Quantization step size ($Q$):**
     $$Q = \frac{V_{FS}}{2^N} = \frac{4.096\text{ V}}{256} = 0.016\text{ V} = 16\text{ mV}$$
   - **Maximum quantization error ($\Delta V_{error(max)}$):**
     Assuming the ADC assigns each continuous sample to the nearest discrete quantization code, the maximum error sits exactly halfway between adjacent levels:
     $$\Delta V_{error(max)} = \pm \frac{Q}{2} = \pm \frac{16\text{ mV}}{2} = \pm 8\text{ mV}$$

3. **12-Bit ADC Upgrade Metrics ($N = 12$):**
   - **New quantization step size ($Q_{12}$):**
     $$Q_{12} = \frac{V_{FS}}{2^{12}} = \frac{4.096\text{ V}}{4096} = 0.001\text{ V} = 1.0\text{ mV}$$
   - **Error reduction factor:**
     $$\text{Reduction Factor} = \frac{Q_8}{Q_{12}} = \frac{16\text{ mV}}{1\text{ mV}} = 16\times = 2^{12 - 8} = 2^4$$
     The voltage quantization error drops by a factor of 16.
   - **Theoretical SQNR Improvement ($\Delta\text{SQNR}$):**
     Each additional bit of ADC resolution adds approximately $6.02\text{ dB}$ of dynamic range ($\approx 20 \log_{10}(2) \approx 6.0206\text{ dB}$):
     $$\Delta\text{SQNR} \approx 6.02 \times \Delta N = 6.02 \times (12 - 8) = 6.02 \times 4 = 24.08\text{ dB}$$
     The 12-bit ADC delivers a $24.08\text{ dB}$ cleaner signal, cutting quantization noise power by a factor of $256$ ($16^2$).
</ModelAnswer>

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
Do not confuse sampling rate (how often you measure in time, measured in samples per second or Hz) with bit resolution (how finely you measure voltage, measured in bits or mV). Increasing the sampling rate avoids aliasing in time, but does not reduce quantization noise. Increasing the bit depth reduces quantization noise in voltage, but does not allow capturing higher frequency vibrations.
</Callout>
