# Karnaugh Maps

While Boolean algebra provides a sound foundation for logic minimization, manipulating algebraic expressions by hand becomes error-prone as circuit complexity increases. Spotting which identity to apply, which terms to duplicate, or where a consensus term applies requires careful inspection.

In 1953, telecommunications engineer Maurice Karnaugh published a visual, tabular method building upon Edward Veitch's diagrams. The **Karnaugh map** (or **K-map**) reorganizes the truth table into a multi-dimensional geometric grid where algebraic adjacency translates directly into spatial adjacency.

## The Adjacency Principle and Gray Code

The fundamental theorem underlying all two-level logic minimization is the complement elimination law:
$$
x y + x y' = x(y + y') = x(1) = x
$$
Whenever two product terms differ in exactly one variable, one term containing the variable uncomplemented ($y$) and the other complemented ($y'$), the variable cancels out, leaving a simpler product term with one fewer literal.

In a standard truth table, adjacent rows do not always differ by a single bit. For example, moving from binary $01_2$ ($1$) to $10_2$ ($2$) flips two bits simultaneously.

To measure this difference, switching theory uses the **Hamming distance** ($d_H$): the count of bit positions in which two binary sequences differ. Between $01_2$ and $11_2$, $d_H = 1$ (only the first bit flips). Between $01_2$ and $10_2$, $d_H = 2$ (both bits flip).

Because the complement elimination identity $x y + x y' = x$ applies exclusively to terms with a Hamming distance of exactly 1 ($d_H = 1$), a standard binary-ordered table cannot visually represent adjacent terms.

To restore unit distance, the rows and columns of a K-map are indexed using [Gray code](/courses/logic-design/codes-and-floating-point#gray-code-one-bit-at-a-time):
$$
00 \longrightarrow 01 \longrightarrow 11 \longrightarrow 10
$$

Every step in Gray code ordering, including the circular wrap-around from the last column ($10$) back to the first column ($00$), changes exactly one bit ($d_H = 1$). As a result, physical spatial adjacency anywhere on a K-map corresponds directly to mathematical algebraic reducibility.

## Geometry and coordinate layouts

The physical geometry of a K-map depends on the number of variables:

### 2-variable map ($2 \times 2$, 4 cells)
Variables $A$ (rows) and $B$ (columns):
```text
      B=0   B=1
A=0 [ m0  | m1  ]
A=1 [ m2  | m3  ]
```

### 3-variable map ($2 \times 4$, 8 cells)
Variable $A$ index rows ($0, 1$); variables $BC$ index columns ($00, 01, 11, 10$):
```text
       BC=00   BC=01   BC=11   BC=10
A=0  [  m0   |  m1   |  m3   |  m2   ]
A=1  [  m4   |  m5   |  m7   |  m6   ]
```
Notice that column $11$ ($m_3, m_7$) comes before column $10$ ($m_2, m_6$). The rightmost column ($10$) is adjacent to the leftmost column ($00$) because $10$ and $00$ differ only in the first bit. The 3-variable map wraps around horizontally like a cylinder.

### 4-variable map ($4 \times 4$, 16 cells)
Variables $AB$ index rows ($00, 01, 11, 10$); variables $CD$ index columns ($00, 01, 11, 10$):
```text
        CD=00   CD=01   CD=11   CD=10
AB=00 [  m0   |  m1   |  m3   |  m2   ]
AB=01 [  m4   |  m5   |  m7   |  m6   ]
AB=11 [  m12  |  m13  |  m15  |  m14  ]
AB=10 [  m8   |  m9   |  m11  |  m10  ]
```

A 4-variable map wraps around in **both directions** like a torus (a doughnut):
- The leftmost column is adjacent to the rightmost column.
- The top row is adjacent to the bottom row.
- **The four corners** ($m_0, m_2, m_8, m_{10}$) are mutually adjacent and form a single valid group of 4 cells, which reduces to $B'D'$.

## Grouping rules

On **Karnaugh maps**, grouping adjacent cells simplifies switching functions by eliminating redundant literals to find the minimal sum-of-products (SOP) expression:

1. **Powers of 2:** Group sizes must strictly equal $1, 2, 4, 8, 16, \dots$ ($2^k$) cells. You cannot group 3, 5, or 6 cells.
2. **Rectangular geometry:** Every group must form a rectangle or square on the toroidal grid. Diagonal groupings are illegal.
3. **Variable elimination rate:** Each doubling in group size eliminates one variable:
   - Group of 1 cell: retains all $n$ literals (the original minterm).
   - Group of 2 cells: eliminates 1 variable ($n - 1$ literals).
   - Group of 4 cells: eliminates 2 variables ($n - 2$ literals).
   - Group of 8 cells: eliminates 3 variables ($n - 3$ literals).
   - Group of 16 cells: eliminates 4 variables (evaluates to logic constant 1).
4. **Maximal group size:** Always make each group as large as possible. Larger groups produce shorter product terms with fewer gate inputs.
5. **Complete coverage:** Every logic 1 must be covered by at least one group. Groups may overlap as needed.
6. **Minimal group count:** Select the minimum number of groups necessary to cover all 1s. Fewer groups produce fewer AND gates in the first logic level.

## Formal terminology of logic minimization

Standard switching theory defines strict classifications for K-map groups:

- **Implicant:** Any product term that covers only 1-cells (and don't-cares) of a function without covering any 0-cells. Every valid rectangle on a K-map is an implicant.
- **Prime Implicant (PI):** A rectangular group of $2^k$ cells that cannot be combined with any other group to form a larger group of size $2^{k+1}$. A prime implicant is maximal; no literal can be removed without causing it to cover a 0.
- **Distinguished Minterm:** A minterm (1-cell) that is covered by **exactly one** prime implicant.
- **Essential Prime Implicant (EPI):** A prime implicant that covers at least one distinguished minterm. Because no other prime implicant can cover that specific 1, **every EPI must be included in every minimal sum-of-products expression**.
- **Non-Essential / Redundant Prime Implicants:** Prime implicants whose 1-cells are completely covered by the set of chosen essential prime implicants. Redundant implicants must be discarded.

## Step-by-step minimization algorithm

1. **Step 1:** Plot all minterms (1s) and don't-cares ($X$) onto the K-map grid.
2. **Step 2:** Identify all prime implicants (find all maximal rectangles of sizes $16, 8, 4, 2, 1$).
3. **Step 3:** Inspect each 1-cell. If a 1-cell is covered by only one prime implicant, circle that prime implicant as an **Essential Prime Implicant**.
4. **Step 4:** Add all EPIs to the minimal SOP equation.
5. **Step 5:** If any 1s remain uncovered, select the minimal combination of non-essential prime implicants that covers all remaining 1s.

## Minimizing Product of Sums (POS) with K-maps

To obtain a minimal Product of Sums (POS) circuit:

### Method 1: Grouping the 0s
1. Form maximal rectangular groups of **0s** instead of 1s.
2. Reading these groups yields the minimal Sum of Products for the complement function, $F'$.
3. Apply De Morgan's theorem to invert the result: $F = (F')'$. This directly transforms the AND-OR expression into an OR-AND (POS) expression.

### Method 2: Direct Maxterm Reading
Read the 0-groups directly as sum terms:
- A variable that remains constant at **0** across the group appears **uncomplemented** ($A$).
- A variable that remains constant at **1** across the group appears **complemented** ($A'$).
- A variable that changes cancels out.

## Don't-Care conditions in K-maps

When simplifying functions with don't-care conditions ($d$ or $X$):
- Treat an $X$ as **1** if doing so allows you to form a larger group (reducing literals) or fewer total groups.
- Treat an $X$ as **0** (leave it uncircled) if it does not help expand a group of 1s.
- **Never create a group consisting entirely of don't-cares.** Doing so adds an unnecessary AND gate to the circuit without covering any required 1s.

## Timing hazards in combinational circuits

In ideal switching theory, gates switch instantaneously. In physical hardware, signal transitions incur propagation delay. These unequal delays cause **timing hazards**:

1. **Static-1 Hazard:** An output that is expected to remain steadily at logic 1 experiences a momentary glitch to logic 0 during an input transition. Static-1 hazards occur in Sum-of-Products (AND-OR) networks.
2. **Static-0 Hazard:** An output that is expected to remain steadily at logic 0 experiences a momentary spike to logic 1 during an input transition. Static-0 hazards occur in Product-of-Sums (OR-AND) networks.
3. **Dynamic Hazard:** An output that is supposed to undergo a clean, single transition ($0 \to 1$ or $1 \to 0$) oscillates multiple times ($0 \to 1 \to 0 \to 1$) before reaching steady state. **Dynamic hazards can occur only in multi-level circuits** (circuits with 3 or more logic levels) where a changing signal travels along three or more parallel paths with differing delays. Two-level SOP and POS circuits do not experience dynamic hazards.

### The Static-1 Hazard analyzed

```text
Input A:   0 -----------------> 1
Input B=1: 1 ------------------- 1
Input C=1: 1 ------------------- 1

Ideal Output:   1 ---------------------------------- 1
Actual Output:  1 -------------\____/--------------- 1  <-- Glitch (0 pulse)
```

Consider $F = A'C + AB$. When $B=1$ and $C=1$:
- For $A=0$, term $A'C = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$, so $F = 1$.
- For $A=1$, term $AB = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$, so $F = 1$.
- When $A$ transitions $0 \to 1$, $A'$ must fall to 0 through an inverter while $A$ rises. Because the inverter adds propagation delay, $A'$ may fall to 0 *before* the second gate registers that $A$ is 1. For several nanoseconds, both $A'C$ and $AB$ are 0, producing an unwanted glitch to 0.

### Detecting and eliminating hazards on a K-map

On a K-map, a static-1 hazard occurs whenever two adjacent 1s are covered by **different** prime implicants without being enclosed in a common group.

To make a circuit hazard-free, add a **redundant consensus group** that covers the adjacent minterms. In $F = A'C + AB$, the consensus of $A'C$ and $AB$ is $BC$. Adding $BC$ yields:
$$
F_{\text{hazard-free}} = A'C + AB + BC
$$
When $B=1$ and $C=1$, term $BC$ remains solidly at 1 throughout the transition of $A$, bridging the delay gap and eliminating the glitch. While $BC$ is algebraically redundant, it is physically necessary for glitch-free hardware operation.

## Common exam pitfalls and student mistakes

When minimizing logic on Karnaugh maps, students frequently make these seven errors:

1. **Non-Power-of-Two Grouping:** Attempting to circle groups of 3, 5, or 6 cells. Group sizes must be integer powers of two ($1, 2, 4, 8, 16$) because only powers of two correspond to the complement cancellation $xy + xy' = x$.
2. **Diagonal Grouping:** Grouping cells that touch only at their corners. Adjacency on a K-map is strictly horizontal or vertical (including wraparound edges). Diagonal neighbors differ by two variables ($d_H = 2$) and cannot eliminate any literal.
3. **Binary Axis Numbering:** Labeling coordinate columns as $00, 01, 10, 11$. This destroys spatial adjacency between columns 1 and 2 ($d_H = 2$). The axes must follow Gray code: $00, 01, 11, 10$.
4. **Circling Pure Don't-Care Groups:** Creating a group that contains only $X$s. Don't-cares are only circled if they help enlarge a group of real 1s (or 0s in POS). Circling a group of pure $X$s adds an entirely useless physical gate to the circuit.
5. **Overlooking Four-Corner Adjacency:** Failing to recognize that the four corners of a 4-variable map ($m_0, m_2, m_8, m_{10}$) are geometrically adjacent on a torus, forming a valid 4-cell group that reduces to $B'D'$.
6. **Omitting Essential Prime Implicants:** Greedily circling arbitrary large groups instead of systematically searching for **distinguished minterms** first. If a 1-cell can only be covered by one prime implicant, that prime implicant is essential and must be selected before any other implicants.
7. **Assuming Minimal SOP is Glitch-Free:** Believing that an algebraically minimal circuit is automatically hazard-free. Eliminating hazards requires adding redundant prime implicants (consensus terms) to bridge across boundary cells.

F(A, B, C, D) = \sum m(0, 2, 5, 7, 8, 10, 14, 15)

1. Plot the minterms on a 4-variable K-map.
2. List all Prime Implicants (PIs).
3. Identify all Essential Prime Implicants (EPIs) and their distinguished minterms.
4. Write the minimal sum-of-products expression.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Map Layout ($AB \times CD$):**
   - Row $00$: $m_0=1$, $m_1=0$, $m_3=0$, $m_2=1$
   - Row $01$: $m_4=0$, $m_5=1$, $m_7=1$, $m_6=0$
   - Row $11$: $m_{12}=0$, $m_{13}=0$, $m_{15}=1$, $m_{14}=1$
   - Row $10$: $m_8=1$, $m_9=0$, $m_{11}=0$, $m_{10}=1$

2. **Identify all Prime Implicants:**
   - **Corner Quad ($m_0, m_2, m_8, m_{10}$):** Covers the four corners. $B=0$ and $D=0$ remain constant $\implies \mathbf{B'D'}$.
   - **Quad ($m_5, m_7, m_{13}, m_{15}$? No, $m_{13}=0$):**
     - Pair $(m_5, m_7)$: Row $01$, columns $01, 11 \implies \mathbf{A'BD}$.
     - Pair $(m_7, m_{15})$: Column $11$, rows $01, 11 \implies \mathbf{BCD}$.
     - Pair $(m_{14}, m_{15})$: Row $11$, columns $11, 10 \implies \mathbf{ABC}$.
     - Pair $(m_{10}, m_{14})$: Column $10$, rows $10, 11 \implies \mathbf{ACD'}$.

   All Prime Implicants: $\{B'D', A'BD, BCD, ABC, ACD'\}$.

3. **Determine Essential Prime Implicants:**
   - Inspect $m_0$: covered only by $B'D'$. Thus **$B'D'$ is an EPI** (distinguished minterms $m_0, m_2, m_8$).
   - Inspect $m_5$: covered only by $A'BD$. Thus **$A'BD$ is an EPI** (distinguished minterm $m_5$).
   - Inspect $m_{14}$: covered by $ABC$ and $ACD'$.
   - Inspect $m_7$: covered by $A'BD$ and $BCD$. Since $A'BD$ is already selected, $m_7$ is covered!
   - Now consider remaining uncovered minterms:
     $m_{14}$ and $m_{15}$ remain.
     - Prime implicant $ABC$ covers both $m_{14}$ and $m_{15}$ simultaneously in one 2-cell group!
     - Alternatively, $ACD'$ covers $m_{14}$ and $BCD$ covers $m_{15}$, which would require two terms instead of one.

4. **Minimal SOP:**
   $$
   F = B'D' + A'BD + ABC
   $$

A prime implicant is only *essential* if it covers at least one 1-cell that no other prime implicant covers. Even though $BCD$ is a valid prime implicant, it is not essential because $m_7$ is covered by $A'BD$ and $m_{15}$ is covered by $ABC$. Including $BCD$ would add a redundant gate.
</Callout>

### Problem 2: Minimization with Don't-Cares in SOP and POS

Minimize the incompletely specified function:
$$
F(w, x, y, z) = \sum m(1, 3, 7, 11, 15) + \sum d(0, 2, 5)
$$
in both minimal SOP and minimal POS forms.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Plot the function:**
   - Row $wx = 00$: $m_0 = X$, $m_1 = 1$, $m_3 = 1$, $m_2 = X$.
   - Row $wx = 01$: $m_4 = 0$, $m_5 = X$, $m_7 = 1$, $m_6 = 0$.
   - Row $wx = 11$: $m_{12} = 0$, $m_{13} = 0$, $m_{15} = 1$, $m_{14} = 0$.
   - Row $wx = 10$: $m_8 = 0$, $m_9 = 0$, $m_{11} = 1$, $m_{10} = 0$.

2. **Minimal SOP (Grouping 1s and useful Xs):**
   - Column $yz = 11$ contains $m_3=1, m_7=1, m_{15}=1, m_{11}=1$. This is an entire column of four 1s!
     Group: $(m_3, m_7, m_{11}, m_{15}) \implies \mathbf{yz}$.
   - Row $wx = 00$ contains $m_0=X, m_1=1, m_3=1, m_2=X$. Grouping all 4 cells:
     Group: $(m_0, m_1, m_2, m_3) \implies \mathbf{w'x'}$.
   All 1s ($m_1, m_3, m_7, m_{11}, m_{15}$) are now completely covered! Don't-care $m_5$ is left as 0.
   $$
   F_{\text{SOP}} = w'x' + yz
   $$

3. **Minimal POS (Grouping 0s and useful Xs):**
   The 0-cells of the function are $m_4, m_6, m_8, m_9, m_{10}, m_{12}, m_{13}, m_{14}$, with available don't-cares $m_0, m_2, m_5$.
   
   Identify prime implicants of 0s on the map:
   - **Octet (8 cells):** Columns $yz = 00$ and $yz = 10$.
     By assigning don't-cares $m_0$ and $m_2$ as 0, all 8 cells in columns $00$ and $10$ form a single group:
     $$
     \{m_0, m_4, m_{12}, m_8, m_2, m_6, m_{14}, m_{10}\}
     $$
     Variables $w, x$, and $y$ cycle through both 0 and 1 across this octet; only variable $z = 0$ remains constant.
     This octet yields product term $\mathbf{z'}$.
   - **Quad (4 cells):** Rows $wx = 11, 10$ in columns $yz = 00, 01$.
     The remaining uncovered 0s are $m_9$ and $m_{13}$ (in column $yz = 01$). Group them with adjacent 0s $m_8$ and $m_{12}$:
     $$
     \{m_8, m_9, m_{12}, m_{13}\}
     $$
     Across these four cells, $w = 1$ and $y = 0$ remain constant.
     This quad yields product term $\mathbf{w y'}$.

   All eight 0-cells are now covered. The minimal sum-of-products for the complement $F'$ is:
   $$
   F' = z' + wy'
   $$

   Apply De Morgan's theorem to obtain the minimal Product of Sums (POS) for $F$:
   $$
   F = (F')' = (z' + wy')' = (z')' \cdot (wy')' = z(w' + y)
   $$

   The minimal POS expression $F = z(w' + y)$ requires only 3 literals and 2 logic gates, offering a much cheaper hardware implementation than the minimal SOP form ($w'x' + yz$, 4 literals).

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
Notice that don't-cares $m_0$ and $m_2$ were treated as $1$ in the SOP minimization, but treated as $0$ in the POS minimization! Don't-cares can take whichever value minimizes the specific hardware form under design.
</Callout>
</ModelAnswer>

### Problem 3: Static-1 Hazard detection and elimination

A combinational logic network implements $F(A, B, C) = A'C + AB$.

1. Construct the K-map and determine the input transition that exposes a static-1 hazard.
2. Explain the physical cause of the glitch in terms of gate propagation delays.
3. Modify the expression to guarantee hazard-free operation.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **K-Map Analysis:**
   - Term $A'C$ covers minterms $m_1 (001)$ and $m_3 (011)$.
   - Term $AB$ covers minterms $m_6 (110)$ and $m_7 (111)$.
   - Notice minterms $m_3 (011)$ and $m_7 (111)$ are geometrically adjacent ($d_H = 1$, differing only in $A$).
   - However, $m_3$ is in group $A'C$ and $m_7$ is in group $AB$. There is no single group containing both $m_3$ and $m_7$.
   - **Hazardous transition:** With $B=1$ and $C=1$, when input $A$ changes from $0 \to 1$ (or $1 \to 0$).

2. **Physical Cause of Glitch:**
   When $A$ changes $0 \to 1$, signal $A'$ must pass through an inverter. The falling edge of $A'$ arrives at gate $A'C$ before the rising edge of $A$ reaches gate $AB$. For a brief duration equal to the inverter propagation delay ($t_{pd}$), both AND gate outputs are 0, causing the output OR gate to produce an unintended 0-spike.

3. **Hazard-Free Circuit Design:**
   Add the consensus term between $A'C$ and $AB$:
   $$
   \text{Consensus}(A'C, AB) = BC
   $$
   Term $BC$ covers $(m_3, m_7)$. 
   The hazard-free equation is:
   $$
   F_{\text{hazard-free}} = A'C + AB + BC
   $$
   When $B=1$ and $C=1$, $BC$ remains continuously at 1, holding the output steady at 1 regardless of switching transients on $A$ and $A'$.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
Static hazards can only occur when a single input variable transitions between adjacent 1-cells. Transitions changing two or more variables simultaneously (e.g. from $001 \to 110$) are dynamic multiple-input transitions where race conditions are unpredictable.
</Callout>
</ModelAnswer>

### Problem 4: 5-variable K-map minimization

Minimize $F(A, B, C, D, E) = \sum m(0, 2, 4, 6, 9, 11, 13, 15, 16, 18, 20, 22, 25, 27, 29, 31)$ using two 4-variable maps.

<ModelAnswer>
A 5-variable function uses two 4-variable maps: Map 0 for $A=0$ (minterms 0 to 15) and Map 1 for $A=1$ (minterms 16 to 31). Minterms with the same $(B,C,D,E)$ coordinates in both maps are adjacent along the third dimension ($A$).

1. **Analyze Map 0 ($A=0$):**
   - 1s are at: $m_0, m_2, m_4, m_6$ (row $BC=00, 01$ in columns $00, 10 \implies B'E'$).
   - 1s are at: $m_9, m_{11}, m_{13}, m_{15}$ (row $BC=10, 11$ in columns $01, 11 \implies BE$).

2. **Analyze Map 1 ($A=1$):**
   - 1s are at: $m_{16}, m_{18}, m_{20}, m_{22}$ (identical coordinates as $m_0, m_2, m_4, m_6$).
   - 1s are at: $m_{25}, m_{27}, m_{29}, m_{31}$ (identical coordinates as $m_9, m_{11}, m_{13}, m_{15}$).

3. **3D Superimposition:**
   Because the exact same patterns appear in both Map 0 ($A=0$) and Map 1 ($A=1$), each 4-cell group merges into an 8-cell group across the $A$ dimension, eliminating variable $A$:
   - Group 1 (8 cells): $\{m_0, m_2, m_4, m_6, m_{16}, m_{18}, m_{20}, m_{22}\} \implies \mathbf{B'E'}$.
   - Group 2 (8 cells): $\{m_9, m_{11}, m_{13}, m_{15}, m_{25}, m_{27}, m_{29}, m_{31}\} \implies \mathbf{BE}$.

4. **Minimal Sum of Products:**
   $$
   F(A, B, C, D, E) = B'E' + BE = (B \oplus E)'
   $$
   The function reduces to an equivalence (XNOR) relationship between $B$ and $E$, completely independent of variables $A$, $C$, and $D$.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
In 5-variable K-maps, minterms that occupy the identical row and column on both maps are geometrically adjacent along the 3D axis. Forgetting to merge across maps doubles the term count and leaves literal $A$ un-eliminated.
</Callout>
</ModelAnswer>

### Problem 5: Larger prime implicant trap and redundant groups

A 4-variable function is defined as:
$$
F(A, B, C, D) = \sum m(1, 5, 6, 7, 11, 12, 13, 15)
$$

1. Plot the function on a 4-variable K-map and find all Prime Implicants.
2. Identify which Prime Implicants are Essential Prime Implicants (EPIs).
3. Demonstrate why the 4-cell group (quad) covering minterms $\{m_5, m_7, m_{13}, m_{15}\}$ is NOT part of the minimal sum of products, despite being larger than all other groups.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Identify all Prime Implicants:**
   - 4-cell group (Quad): $\{m_5, m_7, m_{13}, m_{15}\} \implies \mathbf{BD}$.
   - 2-cell groups (Pairs):
     - $\{m_1, m_5\} \implies \mathbf{A'C'D}$.
     - $\{m_6, m_7\} \implies \mathbf{A'BC}$.
     - $\{m_{12}, m_{13}\} \implies \mathbf{ABC'}$.
     - $\{m_{11}, m_{15}\} \implies \mathbf{ACD}$.
   All Prime Implicants: $\{BD, A'C'D, A'BC, ABC', ACD\}$.

2. **Evaluate Essential Prime Implicants:**
   - Minterm $m_1 (0001)$ is covered only by $A'C'D$. $\implies \mathbf{A'C'D}$ is an EPI.
   - Minterm $m_6 (0110)$ is covered only by $A'BC$. $\implies \mathbf{A'BC}$ is an EPI.
   - Minterm $m_{12} (1100)$ is covered only by $ABC'$. $\implies \mathbf{ABC'}$ is an EPI.
   - Minterm $m_{11} (1011)$ is covered only by $ACD$. $\implies \mathbf{ACD}$ is an EPI.

3. **Check coverage of minterms by the four EPIs:**
   - $A'C'D$ covers: $\{m_1, m_5\}$
   - $A'BC$ covers: $\{m_6, m_7\}$
   - $ABC'$ covers: $\{m_{12}, m_{13}\}$
   - $ACD$ covers: $\{m_{11}, m_{15}\}$
   Together, these four EPIs cover every single minterm in the function:
   $$
   \{m_1, m_5, m_6, m_7, m_{11}, m_{12}, m_{13}, m_{15}\}
   $$
   Notice that all four minterms of the quad $BD$ ($\{m_5, m_7, m_{13}, m_{15}\}$) are already covered!
   Including $BD$ in the expression would produce a 5-term expression ($BD + A'C'D + A'BC + ABC' + ACD$).
   Therefore, the 4-cell prime implicant $BD$ is completely redundant. The unique minimal SOP is:
   $$
   F_{\text{minimal}} = A'C'D + A'BC + ABC' + ACD
   $$

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
A common exam heuristic is "always circle the biggest group first." As this problem proves, a large group can have zero distinguished minterms. If you blindly include the 4-cell group, you still need all four pairs to cover $m_1, m_6, m_{11}, m_{12}$, leaving you with a non-minimal 5-term solution. Always find the Essential Prime Implicants first!
</Callout>
</ModelAnswer>

### Problem 6: Static-0 hazard in Product of Sums (POS) circuits

A combinational logic network implements the Product of Sums function:
$$
F(A, B, C) = (A + B)(A' + C)
$$

1. Plot the function's 0-cells on a 3-variable K-map.
2. Identify the single-variable input transition that exposes a static-0 hazard.
3. Determine the consensus sum term needed to prevent the glitch and state the hazard-free POS equation.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **K-Map Analysis (0-cells):**
   - Factor $(A + B) = 0$ when $A=0, B=0$, covering maxterms $M_0 (000)$ and $M_1 (001)$.
   - Factor $(A' + C) = 0$ when $A=1, C=0$, covering maxterms $M_4 (100)$ and $M_6 (110)$.
   - Notice maxterm $M_0 (000)$ and maxterm $M_4 (100)$ are geometrically adjacent ($d_H = 1$, differing only in $A$).
   - However, $M_0$ belongs to group $(A + B)$ and $M_4$ belongs to group $(A' + C)$. They are not covered by a common sum term.
   - **Hazardous condition:** When $B = 0$ and $C = 0$, while input $A$ switches from $0 \to 1$ (or $1 \to 0$).

2. **Physical cause of the static-0 glitch:**
   With $B=0$ and $C=0$, $F = (A + 0)(A' + 0) = A \cdot A' = 0$.
   During an input transition $A: 1 \to 0$:
   - Gate 1 computes $A + B = 1 \to 0$ (falling edge).
   - Gate 2 computes $A' + C = 0 \to 1$ (rising edge).
   - Because $A'$ is generated through an inverter, the rising edge at Gate 2 may arrive before the falling edge at Gate 1 completes.
   - For a brief interval $\Delta t$, both OR gates output logic 1 simultaneously.
   - The output AND gate receives $(1 \cdot 1) = 1$, producing an unintended momentary 1-spike (glitch) on an output that was supposed to remain stably at 0.

3. **Hazard-free circuit:**
   Apply the dual consensus theorem to sum terms $(A + B)$ and $(A' + C)$:
   $$
   \text{Dual Consensus}\big((A + B), (A' + C)\big) = (B + C)
   $$
   The consensus term $(B + C)$ bridges adjacent 0-cells $M_0$ and $M_4$.
   The hazard-free POS equation is:
   $$
   F_{\text{hazard-free}} = (A + B)(A' + C)(B + C)
   $$
   When $B=0$ and $C=0$, the factor $(B + C) = 0 + 0 = 0$, holding the output AND gate firmly at 0 during any switching on $A$.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
A static-1 hazard in SOP produces an unwanted glitch to 0; a static-0 hazard in POS produces an unwanted glitch to 1. In both cases, the glitch is eliminated by adding the consensus term bridging the uncovered adjacent cells.
</Callout>
</ModelAnswer>
