# Karnaugh Maps

While Boolean algebra provides a sound foundation for logic minimization, manipulating algebraic expressions by hand becomes error-prone as circuit complexity increases. Spotting which identity to apply, which terms to duplicate, or where a consensus term applies requires ingenuity.

In 1953, telecommunications engineer Maurice Karnaugh published a visual, tabular method building upon Edward Veitch's diagrams. The **Karnaugh map** (or **K-map**) reorganizes the truth table into a multi-dimensional geometric grid where algebraic adjacency translates directly into spatial adjacency.

## The Adjacency Principle and Gray Code

The fundamental theorem underlying all two-level logic minimization is the complement elimination law:
$$
x y + x y' = x(y + y') = x(1) = x
$$
Whenever two product terms differ in exactly one variable—one term containing the variable uncomplemented ($y$) and the other complemented ($y'$)—the variable cancels out, leaving a simpler product term with one fewer literal.

In a standard truth table, adjacent rows do not always differ by a single bit. For example, moving from binary $01_2$ ($1$) to $10_2$ ($2$) flips two bits simultaneously.

To restore unit distance, the rows and columns of a K-map are indexed using [Gray code](/courses/logic-design/codes-and-floating-point#gray-code-one-bit-at-a-time):
$$
00 \longrightarrow 01 \longrightarrow 11 \longrightarrow 10
$$

Because any two neighboring cells in Gray code ordering have a **Hamming distance of 1** ($d_H = 1$), any two physically adjacent cells on a K-map can be combined to eliminate the differing variable.

## Geometry and coordinate layouts

The physical geometry of a K-map depends on the number of variables:

### 2-variable map ($2 \times 2$, 4 cells)
Variables $A$ (rows) and $B$ (columns):
```text
      B=0   B=1
A=0 [ m0  | m1  ]
A=1 [ m2  | m3  ]
```

### 3-variable map ($2 \times 4$, 8 cells)
Variable $A$ index rows ($0, 1$); variables $BC$ index columns ($00, 01, 11, 10$):
```text
       BC=00   BC=01   BC=11   BC=10
A=0  [  m0   |  m1   |  m3   |  m2   ]
A=1  [  m4   |  m5   |  m7   |  m6   ]
```
Notice that column $11$ ($m_3, m_7$) comes before column $10$ ($m_2, m_6$). The rightmost column ($10$) is adjacent to the leftmost column ($00$) because $10$ and $00$ differ only in the first bit. The 3-variable map wraps around horizontally like a cylinder.

### 4-variable map ($4 \times 4$, 16 cells)
Variables $AB$ index rows ($00, 01, 11, 10$); variables $CD$ index columns ($00, 01, 11, 10$):
```text
        CD=00   CD=01   CD=11   CD=10
AB=00 [  m0   |  m1   |  m3   |  m2   ]
AB=01 [  m4   |  m5   |  m7   |  m6   ]
AB=11 [  m12  |  m13  |  m15  |  m14  ]
AB=10 [  m8   |  m9   |  m11  |  m10  ]
```

A 4-variable map wraps around in **both directions** like a torus (a doughnut):
- The leftmost column is adjacent to the rightmost column.
- The top row is adjacent to the bottom row.
- **The four corners** ($m_0, m_2, m_8, m_{10}$) are mutually adjacent and form a single valid group of 4 cells, which reduces to $B'D'$.

## Grouping rules

On **Karnaugh maps**, grouping adjacent cells simplifies switching functions by eliminating redundant literals to find the minimal sum-of-products (SOP) expression:

1. **Powers of 2:** Group sizes must strictly equal $1, 2, 4, 8, 16, \dots$ ($2^k$) cells. You cannot group 3, 5, or 6 cells.
2. **Rectangular geometry:** Every group must form a rectangle or square on the toroidal grid. Diagonal groupings are illegal.
3. **Variable elimination rate:** Each doubling in group size eliminates one variable:
   - Group of 1 cell: retains all $n$ literals (the original minterm).
   - Group of 2 cells: eliminates 1 variable ($n - 1$ literals).
   - Group of 4 cells: eliminates 2 variables ($n - 2$ literals).
   - Group of 8 cells: eliminates 3 variables ($n - 3$ literals).
   - Group of 16 cells: eliminates 4 variables (evaluates to logic constant 1).
4. **Maximal group size:** Always make each group as large as possible. Larger groups produce shorter product terms with fewer gate inputs.
5. **Complete coverage:** Every logic 1 must be covered by at least one group. Groups may overlap as needed.
6. **Minimal group count:** Select the minimum number of groups necessary to cover all 1s. Fewer groups produce fewer AND gates in the first logic level.

## Formal terminology of logic minimization

Standard switching theory defines strict classifications for K-map groups:

- **Implicant:** Any product term that covers only 1-cells (and don't-cares) of a function without covering any 0-cells. Every valid rectangle on a K-map is an implicant.
- **Prime Implicant (PI):** A rectangular group of $2^k$ cells that cannot be combined with any other group to form a larger group of size $2^{k+1}$. A prime implicant is maximal; no literal can be removed without causing it to cover a 0.
- **Distinguished Minterm:** A minterm (1-cell) that is covered by **exactly one** prime implicant.
- **Essential Prime Implicant (EPI):** A prime implicant that covers at least one distinguished minterm. Because no other prime implicant can cover that specific 1, **every EPI must be included in every minimal sum-of-products expression**.
- **Non-Essential / Redundant Prime Implicants:** Prime implicants whose 1-cells are completely covered by the set of chosen essential prime implicants. Redundant implicants must be discarded.

## Step-by-step minimization algorithm

1. **Step 1:** Plot all minterms (1s) and don't-cares ($X$) onto the K-map grid.
2. **Step 2:** Identify all prime implicants (find all maximal rectangles of sizes $16, 8, 4, 2, 1$).
3. **Step 3:** Inspect each 1-cell. If a 1-cell is covered by only one prime implicant, circle that prime implicant as an **Essential Prime Implicant**.
4. **Step 4:** Add all EPIs to the minimal SOP equation.
5. **Step 5:** If any 1s remain uncovered, select the minimal combination of non-essential prime implicants that covers all remaining 1s.

## Minimizing Product of Sums (POS) with K-maps

To obtain a minimal Product of Sums (POS) circuit:

### Method 1: Grouping the 0s
1. Form maximal rectangular groups of **0s** instead of 1s.
2. Reading these groups yields the minimal Sum of Products for the complement function, $F'$.
3. Apply De Morgan's theorem to invert the result: $F = (F')'$. This directly transforms the AND-OR expression into an OR-AND (POS) expression.

### Method 2: Direct Maxterm Reading
Read the 0-groups directly as sum terms:
- A variable that remains constant at **0** across the group appears **uncomplemented** ($A$).
- A variable that remains constant at **1** across the group appears **complemented** ($A'$).
- A variable that changes cancels out.

## Don't-Care conditions in K-maps

When simplifying functions with don't-care conditions ($d$ or $X$):
- Treat an $X$ as **1** if doing so allows you to form a larger group (reducing literals) or fewer total groups.
- Treat an $X$ as **0** (leave it uncircled) if it does not help expand a group of 1s.
- **Never create a group consisting entirely of don't-cares.** Doing so adds an unnecessary AND gate to the circuit without covering any required 1s.

## Timing hazards in combinational circuits

In ideal switching theory, gates switch instantaneously. In physical hardware, signal transitions incur propagation delay. These unequal delays cause **timing hazards**:

### The Static-1 Hazard
A **static-1 hazard** is an unwanted momentary glitch to 0 on an output that is theoretically expected to remain steadily at 1 during an input transition:

```text
Input A:   0 -----------------> 1
Input B=1: 1 ------------------- 1
Input C=1: 1 ------------------- 1

Ideal Output:   1 ---------------------------------- 1
Actual Output:  1 -------------\____/--------------- 1  <-- Glitch (0 pulse)
```

Consider $F = A'C + AB$. When $B=1$ and $C=1$:
- For $A=0$, term $A'C = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$, so $F = 1$.
- For $A=1$, term $AB = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$, so $F = 1$.
- When $A$ transitions $0 \to 1$, $A'$ must fall to 0 through an inverter while $A$ rises. Because the inverter adds propagation delay, $A'$ may fall to 0 *before* the second gate registers that $A$ is 1. For several nanoseconds, both $A'C$ and $AB$ are 0, producing an unwanted glitch to 0.

### Detecting and eliminating hazards on a K-map
On a K-map, a static-1 hazard occurs whenever two adjacent 1s are covered by **different** prime implicants without being enclosed in a common group.

To make a circuit hazard-free, add a **redundant consensus group** that covers the adjacent minterms. In $F = A'C + AB$, the consensus of $A'C$ and $AB$ is $BC$. Adding $BC$ yields:
$$
F_{\text{hazard-free}} = A'C + AB + BC
$$
When $B=1$ and $C=1$, term $BC$ remains solidly at 1 throughout the transition of $A$, completely bridging the delay gap and eliminating the glitch.

### Problem 3: Static-1 Hazard detection and elimination

A combinational logic network implements $F(A, B, C) = A'C + AB$.

1. Construct the K-map and determine the input transition that exposes a static-1 hazard.
2. Explain the physical cause of the glitch in terms of gate propagation delays.
3. Modify the expression to guarantee hazard-free operation.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **K-Map Analysis:**
   - Term $A'C$ covers minterms $m_1 (001)$ and $m_3 (011)$.
   - Term $AB$ covers minterms $m_6 (110)$ and $m_7 (111)$.
   - Notice minterms $m_3 (011)$ and $m_7 (111)$ are geometrically adjacent ($d_H = 1$, differing only in $A$).
   - However, $m_3$ is in group $A'C$ and $m_7$ is in group $AB$. There is no single group containing both $m_3$ and $m_7$.
   - **Hazardous transition:** With $B=1$ and $C=1$, when input $A$ changes from $0 \to 1$ (or $1 \to 0$).

2. **Physical Cause of Glitch:**
   When $A$ changes $0 \to 1$, signal $A'$ must pass through an inverter. The falling edge of $A'$ arrives at gate $A'C$ before the rising edge of $A$ reaches gate $AB$. For a brief duration equal to the inverter propagation delay ($t_{pd}$), both AND gate outputs are 0, causing the output OR gate to produce an unintended 0-spike.

3. **Hazard-Free Circuit Design:**
   Add the consensus term between $A'C$ and $AB$:
   $$
   \text{Consensus}(A'C, AB) = BC
   $$
   Term $BC$ covers $(m_3, m_7)$. 
   The hazard-free equation is:
   $$
   F_{\text{hazard-free}} = A'C + AB + BC
   $$
   When $B=1$ and $C=1$, $BC$ remains continuously at 1, holding the output steady at 1 regardless of switching transients on $A$ and $A'$.
</ModelAnswer>

### Problem 4: 5-variable K-map minimization

Minimize $F(A, B, C, D, E) = \sum m(0, 2, 4, 6, 9, 11, 13, 15, 16, 18, 20, 22, 25, 27, 29, 31)$ using two 4-variable maps.

<ModelAnswer>
A 5-variable function uses two 4-variable maps: Map 0 for $A=0$ (minterms $0-15$) and Map 1 for $A=1$ (minterms $16-31$). Minterms with the same $(B,C,D,E)$ coordinates in both maps are adjacent along the third dimension ($A$).

1. **Analyze Map 0 ($A=0$):**
   - 1s are at: $m_0, m_2, m_4, m_6$ (row $BC=00, 01$ in columns $00, 10 \implies B'E'$).
   - 1s are at: $m_9, m_{11}, m_{13}, m_{15}$ (row $BC=10, 11$ in columns $01, 11 \implies BE$).

2. **Analyze Map 1 ($A=1$):**
   - 1s are at: $m_{16}, m_{18}, m_{20}, m_{22}$ (identical coordinates as $m_0, m_2, m_4, m_6$).
   - 1s are at: $m_{25}, m_{27}, m_{29}, m_{31}$ (identical coordinates as $m_9, m_{11}, m_{13}, m_{15}$).

3. **3D Superimposition:**
   Because the exact same patterns appear in both Map 0 ($A=0$) and Map 1 ($A=1$), each 4-cell group merges into an 8-cell group across the $A$ dimension, eliminating variable $A$:
   - Group 1 (8 cells): $\{m_0, m_2, m_4, m_6, m_{16}, m_{18}, m_{20}, m_{22}\} \implies \mathbf{B'E'}$.
   - Group 2 (8 cells): $\{m_9, m_{11}, m_{13}, m_{15}, m_{25}, m_{27}, m_{29}, m_{31}\} \implies \mathbf{BE}$.

4. **Minimal Sum of Products:**
   $$
   F(A, B, C, D, E) = B'E' + BE = (B \oplus E)'
   $$
   The function reduces to an equivalence (XNOR) relationship between $B$ and $E$, completely independent of variables $A$, $C$, and $D$.
</ModelAnswer>
