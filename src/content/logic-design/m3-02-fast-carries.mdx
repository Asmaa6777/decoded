# Ripple Carry and Look-Ahead

Digital logic gates are physical electronic devices made of transistors. When gate inputs switch between logic levels, the output does not respond instantaneously. The finite time required for transistors to charge and discharge parasitic capacitances is the [propagation delay](/courses/logic-design/logic-gates#propagation-delay-and-transition-times) ($t_{pd}$).

A single gate delay is small, often under 50 picoseconds in modern silicon, but delays accumulate along signal paths. In arithmetic circuits, this creates a performance bottleneck known as the **carry propagation problem**.

## The Propagation Delay Bottleneck in Ripple-Carry Adders

Recall the [ripple-carry adder](/courses/logic-design/adders#parallel-binary-adders-ripple-carry-adder) from the previous lesson. In an $n$-bit parallel adder composed of cascaded [full adders](/courses/logic-design/adders#the-full-adder):
- Each stage $i$ computes its sum bit as $S_i = A_i \oplus B_i \oplus C_i$.
- Each stage $i$ computes its outgoing carry as $C_{i+1} = A_i B_i + (A_i \oplus B_i)C_i$.

Although the operand bits $A_i$ and $B_i$ are available simultaneously across all bit positions at time $t = 0$, the carry signals cannot be evaluated in parallel. Stage 1 cannot finalize $C_2$ until it receives $C_1$ from Stage 0. Stage 2 cannot finalize $C_3$ until it receives $C_2$, and so forth:

$$
C_0 \longrightarrow \text{FA}_0 \xrightarrow{C_1} \text{FA}_1 \xrightarrow{C_2} \text{FA}_2 \xrightarrow{C_3} \text{FA}_3 \xrightarrow{C_4} \cdots \xrightarrow{C_n}
$$

Like a row of falling dominoes, the carry bit must "ripple" through every single stage before the most significant sum bit and final carry are valid.

### The critical timing path

Assuming each standard gate (AND, OR) contributes a propagation delay of $\tau$, and an XOR gate constructed from two gate levels contributes $2\tau$:
- In each full adder, the carry-out is produced through one AND gate and one OR gate: a delay of $2\tau$ per stage.
- The least significant stage generates $C_1$ in $2\tau$.
- Each subsequent stage $i$ adds $2\tau$ to propagate the carry.
- The final carry $C_n$ emerges after $2n\tau$.
- The final sum bit $S_{n-1}$ requires one additional XOR delay ($2\tau$) after $C_{n-1}$ arrives.

For an $n$-bit ripple-carry adder, the worst-case propagation delay scales linearly:
$$
t_{\text{RCA}} \approx 2n\tau = O(n)
$$

| Word Width ($n$) | Logic Levels in Carry Path | Delay Relative to Gate Delay ($\tau$) | Max Frequency ($\tau = 1\text{ ns}$) |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| 4 bits | 8 levels | $8\tau$ | $125\text{ MHz}$ |
| 16 bits | 32 levels | $32\tau$ | $31.25\text{ MHz}$ |
| 32 bits | 64 levels | $64\tau$ | $15.6\text{ MHz}$ |
| 64 bits | 128 levels | $128\tau$ | $7.8\text{ MHz}$ |

Because a CPU's clock frequency depends on the longest path in the machine ($f_{\text{clk}} \le \frac{1}{t_{\text{critical}}}$), a 64-bit ripple-carry adder would limit the processor to very low clock speeds.

## The Carry-Lookahead Principle

To avoid linear delay $O(n)$, stages must not wait for their neighbors. The **carry-lookahead adder** (CLA) achieves this by calculating all carry bits in parallel directly from the primary inputs $A$, $B$, and $C_0$.

Consider what causes stage $i$ to produce an outgoing carry $C_{i+1}$:
1. **Carry Generate ($G_i$):** If both input bits are 1 ($A_i = 1$ and $B_i = 1$), the stage produces a carry unconditionally, regardless of the incoming carry $C_i$:
   $$
   G_i = A_i B_i
   $$
2. **Carry Propagate ($P_i$):** If at least one input bit is 1, an incoming carry $C_i = 1$ will pass through the stage to emerge as $C_{i+1} = 1$:
   $$
   P_i = A_i \oplus B_i \qquad (\text{or } P_i = A_i + B_i)
   $$

Using these definitions, the carry-out from any stage $i$ is expressed as:
$$
C_{i+1} = G_i + P_i C_i
$$

> [!NOTE]
> **Why $A_i \oplus B_i$ is used for Propagate instead of $A_i + B_i$:**
> In pure Boolean algebra, either $P_i = A_i + B_i$ or $P_i = A_i \oplus B_i$ produces identical carry equations because when $A_i = B_i = 1$, the generate term $G_i = 1$ dominates ($1 + 1\cdot C_i = 1$).
> 
> However, hardware designers prefer $P_i = A_i \oplus B_i$ because the XOR output is already needed to compute the sum bit:
> $$
> S_i = A_i \oplus B_i \oplus C_i = P_i \oplus C_i
> $$
> Reusing $P_i = A_i \oplus B_i$ for both carry and sum eliminates duplicate XOR gates, saving silicon area and routing congestion.

## Unrolling the Recursive Carry Equations

Carry-lookahead logic recursively substitutes each carry expression into the next:

For stage 0:
$$
C_1 = G_0 + P_0 C_0
$$

For stage 1:
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_2 &= G_1 + P_1 C_1 \\
    &= G_1 + P_1(G_0 + P_0 C_0) \\
    &= G_1 + P_1 G_0 + P_1 P_0 C_0
\end{aligned}
$$

For stage 2:
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_3 &= G_2 + P_2 C_2 \\
    &= G_2 + P_2(G_1 + P_1 G_0 + P_1 P_0 C_0) \\
    &= G_2 + P_2 G_1 + P_2 P_1 G_0 + P_2 P_1 P_0 C_0
\end{aligned}
$$

For stage 3:
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_4 &= G_3 + P_3 C_3 \\
    &= G_3 + P_3(G_2 + P_2 G_1 + P_2 P_1 G_0 + P_2 P_1 P_0 C_0) \\
    &= G_3 + P_3 G_2 + P_3 P_2 G_1 + P_3 P_2 P_1 G_0 + P_3 P_2 P_1 P_0 C_0
\end{aligned}
$$

### The critical structural insight

Examine the equation for $C_4$:
- $C_4$ does **not** depend on intermediate carries $C_1$, $C_2$, or $C_3$.
- $C_4$ is expressed entirely in terms of the initial carry $C_0$ and the bit generate and propagate terms ($G_0\dots G_3$ and $P_0\dots P_3$).
- Because all $P_i$ and $G_i$ terms are computed simultaneously from primary inputs $A$ and $B$, every carry signal is a standard **two-level AND-OR circuit**!

Each product term in $C_4$ corresponds to a distinct physical origin:
- **$G_3$:** A carry generated directly in stage 3 ($A_3 = B_3 = 1$).
- **$P_3 G_2$:** A carry generated in stage 2 that propagates through stage 3.
- **$P_3 P_2 G_1$:** A carry generated in stage 1 that propagates through stages 2 and 3.
- **$P_3 P_2 P_1 G_0$:** A carry generated in stage 0 that propagates through stages 1, 2, and 3.
- **$P_3 P_2 P_1 P_0 C_0$:** An external carry-in $C_0$ that propagates through all four stages without stopping.

Notice the gate fan-in progression:
- $C_1$ requires a 2-input AND gate and a 2-input OR gate.
- $C_2$ requires up to a 3-input AND gate and a 3-input OR gate.
- $C_3$ requires up to a 4-input AND gate and a 4-input OR gate.
- $C_4$ requires up to a 5-input AND gate and a 5-input OR gate.

A 5th carry bit ($C_5$) would require a 6-input AND gate and 6-input OR gate. Because standard logic gates are limited to 4 or 5 inputs, 4 bits is the natural modular boundary for carry-lookahead blocks.

## Architecture of a Carry-Lookahead Adder

A 4-bit carry-lookahead adder operates in three distinct, highly parallel stages:

1. **Stage 1 (Bit Generate and Propagate):**
   All $P_i = A_i \oplus B_i$ and $G_i = A_i B_i$ are generated simultaneously for all $i \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$.
   *Latency: $1\tau_{\text{gate}}$ for $G_i$, $1\tau_{\text{XOR}}$ (or $2\tau$) for $P_i$.*

2. **Stage 2 (Carry-Lookahead Generator, CLAG):**
   The specialized CLAG logic block evaluates the expanded equations for $C_1, C_2, C_3, C_4$ in parallel using two levels of gates (one level of multi-input AND gates feeding one multi-input OR gate per carry).
   *Latency: $2\tau_{\text{gate}}$ (1 AND level + 1 OR level).*

3. **Stage 3 (Sum Generation):**
   All sum bits are computed simultaneously once the carries emerge:
   $$
   S_0 = P_0 \oplus C_0, \quad S_1 = P_1 \oplus C_1, \quad S_2 = P_2 \oplus C_2, \quad S_3 = P_3 \oplus C_3
   $$
   *Latency: $1\tau_{\text{XOR}}$ (or $2\tau$).*

Total propagation delay through the entire 4-bit CLA:
$$
t_{\text{CLA}} = t_{P,G} + t_{\text{CLAG}} + t_{\text{Sum}} \approx 2\tau + 2\tau + 2\tau = 6\tau
$$
This delay is constant: $C_4$ arrives at the same instant as $C_1$.

## The Physical Limit: Fan-in and Silicon Complexity

If carry-lookahead evaluates in constant time, why not build a 64-bit single-level CLA?

Inspect the algebraic equation for carry $C_{n}$:
- The final term of $C_{64}$ is $P_{63} P_{62} \cdots P_1 P_0 C_0$, requiring a **65-input AND gate**.
- The equation for $C_{64}$ contains 65 product terms, requiring a **65-input OR gate**.

In real CMOS silicon, multi-input gates cannot scale indefinitely:
1. **Fan-In Degradation:** An $N$-input CMOS NAND or NOR gate places $N$ transistors in series. Series resistance increases linearly with $N$, while parasitic junction capacitances add up, causing propagation delay to scale quadratically ($\tau \propto N^2$).
2. **Noise Margins and Voltage Drops:** High fan-in severely degrades logic threshold voltages and noise margins.
3. **Physical Limits:** In standard semiconductor libraries, gate [fan-in](/courses/logic-design/logic-gates#fan-in-and-fan-out) is strictly limited to 4 or 5 inputs.

A single-level 64-bit CLA is physically impossible to fabricate.

## Hierarchical (Block) Carry-Lookahead Adders

To scale lookahead logic to 16, 32, and 64 bits without violating gate fan-in constraints, systems use **hierarchical (multilevel) carry-lookahead adders**.

The bits are divided into 4-bit blocks. Each 4-bit block computes its own internal carries, and also outputs two summary signals to a higher-level lookahead generator:
- **Block Propagate ($P_G$ or $P_{\text{group}}$):** The block will pass an incoming carry $C_0$ entirely through all 4 stages if every stage propagates:
  $$
  P_G = P_3 P_2 P_1 P_0
  $$
- **Block Generate ($G_G$ or $G_{\text{group}}$):** The block will generate an outgoing carry internally, regardless of $C_0$:
  $$
  G_G = G_3 + P_3 G_2 + P_3 P_2 G_1 + P_3 P_2 P_1 G_0
  $$

The carry-out of the entire 4-bit block is then simply:
$$
C_4 = G_G + P_G C_0
$$

```
   ┌─────────────── 16-bit Multilevel CLA ───────────────┐
   │                                                     │
   │  ┌──────────┐  ┌──────────┐  ┌──────────┐  ┌──────────┐
   │  │Block 3   │  │Block 2   │  │Block 1   │  │Block 0   │
   │  │Bits 15-12│  │Bits 11-8 │  │Bits 7-4  │  │Bits 3-0  │
   │  └──┬────┬──┘  └──┬────┬──┘  └──┬────┬──┘  └──┬────┬──┘
   │     │P3  │G3      │P2  │G2      │P1  │G1      │P0  │G0
   │     ▼    ▼        ▼    ▼        ▼    ▼        ▼    ▼
   │  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  │   Second-Level Lookahead Carry Generator (74182)│
   │  └──┬──────────────┬──────────────┬────────────────┘
   │     │ C12          │ C8           │ C4
   │     └──────────────┼──────────────┘
   └────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┘
```

A second-level lookahead unit, such as the classic **74182 Look-Ahead Carry Generator** IC, takes the $(P_G, G_G)$ pairs from four 4-bit adder blocks (such as 74181 or 74283 adders) and computes the block carries $C_4, C_8, C_{12}, C_{16}$ simultaneously:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_4 &= G_{G0} + P_{G0} C_0 \\
C_8 &= G_{G1} + P_{G1} G_{G0} + P_{G1} P_{G0} C_0 \\
C_{12} &= G_{G2} + P_{G2} G_{G1} + P_{G2} P_{G1} G_{G0} + P_{G2} P_{G1} P_{G0} C_0 \\
C_{16} &= G_{G3} + P_{G3} G_{G2} + P_{G3} P_{G2} G_{G1} + P_{G3} P_{G2} P_{G1} G_{G0} + P_{G3} P_{G2} P_{G1} P_{G0} C_0
\end{aligned}
$$

In a hierarchical CLA, propagation delay scales logarithmically:
$$
t_{\text{Hierarchical CLA}} = O(\log n)
$$
For a 64-bit adder, delay drops from 128 gate delays in ripple carry to approximately 10 gate delays in a 3-level CLA tree.

## Alternative Fast Adder Topologies

Hardware designers use several other fast adder architectures depending on trade-offs between area, power, and speed:

### 1. Carry-Skip Adder (Carry-Bypass Adder)
A carry-skip adder divides an $n$-bit adder into blocks of $k$ bits. Inside each block, standard ripple carry is used. However, an AND gate computes $P_{\text{block}} = P_0 P_1 \cdots P_{k-1}$.
- If $P_{\text{block}} = 1$, a 2-to-1 multiplexer skips the carry directly around the block to the next stage, bypassing the $k$ ripple stages.
- Delay scales with $O(\sqrt{n})$ while requiring very little additional hardware.

### 2. Carry-Select Adder (CSA)
A carry-select adder speculatively duplicates hardware:
- For each $k$-bit block, two separate additions are performed simultaneously: one assuming $C_{\text{in}} = 0$, and one assuming $C_{\text{in}} = 1$.
- When the actual carry arrives from the preceding block, a 2-to-1 multiplexer simply selects the correct pre-computed sum.
- Eliminates the carry calculation delay in exchange for approximately 100% more silicon area.

### 3. Parallel Prefix Adders (Tree Adders)
Modern high-frequency 64-bit processors (such as x86 and ARM Cortex-A) use parallel prefix architectures:
- **Kogge-Stone Adder:** Minimum logic depth ($O(\log_2 n)$), uniform fan-out, but high wiring congestion.
- **Brent-Kung Adder:** Minimal wiring and area with slightly higher logic depth ($2\log_2 n - 1$).

<Callout tone="neon" label="The Cost of Speed">
Faster adders always consume more silicon area and more dynamic power. Ripple-carry adders require $O(n)$ gates and minimum wiring; Carry-Lookahead adders require $O(n \log n)$ gates; Kogge-Stone adders require substantial interconnect area. In low-power IoT microcontrollers, ripple adders are often chosen specifically to save energy and chip area.
</Callout>

## Common exam pitfalls and student mistakes

When working with fast arithmetic circuits, students often make these six mistakes:

1. **Confusing Generate ($G_i$) and Propagate ($P_i$):** Mistaking $G_i = A_i B_i$ with $P_i = A_i \oplus B_i$. Generate is an unconditional AND: if $A_i = B_i = 1$, a carry is produced regardless of incoming carries. Propagate is an XOR (or OR): if $A_i \oplus B_i = 1$, an incoming carry will pass through to the next stage.
2. **Dropping the Initial Carry ($C_0$) from the Carry-Lookahead Equations:** Forgetting the trailing product term ($P_3 P_2 P_1 P_0 C_0$) in $C_4$. Students often mistakenly assume $C_0 = 0$ is fixed, which causes the lookahead equations to fail completely when adders are cascaded or used in subtraction mode where $C_0 = 1$.
3. **Underestimating Ripple-Carry Propagation Delay:** Calculating the delay of an $n$-bit ripple-carry adder as $n\tau$ instead of $2n\tau$. In each standard full adder, the carry-out passes through an AND gate and an OR gate (2 gate levels: $2\tau$ per stage). In addition, the final sum bit $S_{n-1}$ requires an extra XOR delay after carry $C_{n-1}$ arrives.
4. **Assuming CLA Sum Bits Appear Simultaneously with Carry Bits:** While the lookahead generator produces carries $C_1, C_2, C_3, C_4$ in parallel in 2 gate delays ($t_{\text{CLAG}} = 2\tau$), the sum bits require an additional XOR operation ($S_i = P_i \oplus C_i$). The sum outputs stabilize one XOR delay ($2\tau$) *after* the carries are valid.
5. **Group Generate ($G_G$) Notation and Indexing Errors:** Forgetting that the most significant stage's generate signal ($G_3$) in a 4-bit block has no propagate signal multiplying it. The block generate is $G_G = G_3 + P_3 G_2 + P_3 P_2 G_1 + P_3 P_2 P_1 G_0$. A frequent exam mistake is writing $P_3 G_3$ or omitting the standalone $G_3$.
6. **The "Infinite Single-Level CLA" Fallacy:** Believing that carry-lookahead adders can be built to arbitrary word lengths (e.g. 64 bits) in two gate levels. In real silicon, gate fan-in is physically bounded to 4 or 5 inputs due to quadratic RC delay degradation ($\tau \propto N^2$). Wide adders must use hierarchical multilevel lookahead trees or tree adders, which scale as $O(\log n)$ rather than $O(1)$.

## Practice problems

### Problem 1: Delay calculation: 32-bit Ripple vs 32-bit Multilevel CLA

Assume elementary gates (AND, OR, NAND, NOR) have a propagation delay of $\tau = 1\text{ ns}$, and 2-input XOR gates have a delay of $2\tau = 2\text{ ns}$.
1. Calculate the worst-case propagation delay of a 32-bit Ripple-Carry Adder.
2. Calculate the worst-case propagation delay of a 32-bit Hierarchical Carry-Lookahead Adder constructed from eight 4-bit CLA blocks and two 4-bit 74182 lookahead carry generators.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **32-bit Ripple-Carry Adder Delay:**
   - Generation of $C_1$ in Stage 0: $A_0, B_0$ feed an AND gate and OR gate: $2\tau = 2\text{ ns}$.
   - Propagation through stages 1 to 30: each full adder carry path adds $2\tau = 2\text{ ns}$.
     $$\text{Delay to } C_{31} = 2\tau + 30 \times 2\tau = 62\tau = 62\text{ ns}$$
   - Final sum bit $S_{31} = P_{31} \oplus C_{31}$: adds one XOR delay ($2\tau = 2\text{ ns}$).
   - Total critical path delay:
     $$t_{\text{RCA}} = 62\tau + 2\tau = 64\tau = 64\text{ ns}$$

2. **32-bit Hierarchical CLA Delay:**
   - **Step 1:** Generate all individual $P_i$ and $G_i$ in parallel:
     $$t_{PG} = \max(t_{AND}, t_{XOR}) = 2\tau = 2\text{ ns}$$
   - **Step 2:** Generate group propagate $P_G$ and group generate $G_G$ within each 4-bit block:
     $$P_G = P_3 P_2 P_1 P_0 \implies 1\text{ level of AND} = 1\tau$$
     $$G_G = G_3 + P_3 G_2 + \dots \implies 2\text{ levels (AND-OR)} = 2\tau$$
     Accumulated time: $2\tau + 2\tau = 4\tau$.
   - **Step 3:** The second-level 74182 lookahead generator receives all $(P_G, G_G)$ and computes block carries ($C_4, C_8, \dots, C_{28}$):
     $$t_{\text{CLAG2}} = 2\text{ gate levels} = 2\tau$$
     Accumulated time to block carry $C_{28}$: $4\tau + 2\tau = 6\tau$.
   - **Step 4:** The internal CLAG of the final 4-bit block (bits 28 to 31) generates internal carry $C_{31}$ using $C_{28}$:
     $$t_{\text{CLAG1}} = 2\text{ gate levels} = 2\tau$$
     Accumulated time to $C_{31}$: $6\tau + 2\tau = 8\tau$.
   - **Step 5:** Final sum bit $S_{31} = P_{31} \oplus C_{31}$:
     $$t_{\text{sum}} = t_{XOR} = 2\tau$$
     Total delay:
     $$t_{\text{H-CLA}} = 8\tau + 2\tau = 10\tau = 10\text{ ns}$$

The hierarchical lookahead adder is **6.4 times faster** than the ripple-carry adder ($10\text{ ns}$ vs $64\text{ ns}$).
</ModelAnswer>

### Problem 2: Numerical trace of a 4-bit Carry-Lookahead Adder

Given the 4-bit inputs $A = 1011_2$ ($A_3=1, A_2=0, A_1=1, A_0=1$), $B = 0111_2$ ($B_3=0, B_2=1, B_1=1, B_0=1$), and incoming carry $C_0 = 1$:
1. Compute all individual generate ($G_i$) and propagate ($P_i$) terms.
2. Evaluate $C_1, C_2, C_3, C_4$ using the unrolled carry-lookahead equations.
3. Compute the sum vector $S_3 S_2 S_1 S_0$ and verify the arithmetic result.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Generate and Propagate terms ($G_i = A_i B_i, P_i = A_i \oplus B_i$):**
   - Bit 0: $A_0=1, B_0=1 \implies G_0 = 1\cdot 1 = 1, \quad P_0 = 1 \oplus 1 = 0$.
   - Bit 1: $A_1=1, B_1=1 \implies G_1 = 1\cdot 1 = 1, \quad P_1 = 1 \oplus 1 = 0$.
   - Bit 2: $A_2=0, B_2=1 \implies G_2 = 0\cdot 1 = 0, \quad P_2 = 0 \oplus 1 = 1$.
   - Bit 3: $A_3=1, B_3=0 \implies G_3 = 1\cdot 0 = 0, \quad P_3 = 1 \oplus 0 = 1$.

2. **Evaluating Carries:**
   - $C_1 = G_0 + P_0 C_0 = 1 + (0 \cdot 1) = 1$.
   - $C_2 = G_1 + P_1 G_0 + P_1 P_0 C_0 = 1 + (0 \cdot 1) + (0 \cdot 0 \cdot 1) = 1$.
   - $C_3 = G_2 + P_2 G_1 + P_2 P_1 G_0 + P_2 P_1 P_0 C_0 = 0 + (1 \cdot 1) + (1 \cdot 0 \cdot 1) + (1 \cdot 0 \cdot 0 \cdot 1) = 1$.
   - $C_4 = G_3 + P_3 G_2 + P_3 P_2 G_1 + P_3 P_2 P_1 G_0 + P_3 P_2 P_1 P_0 C_0$:
     $$C_4 = 0 + (1 \cdot 0) + (1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1) + (1 \cdot 1 \cdot 0 \cdot 1) + (1 \cdot 1 \cdot 0 \cdot 0 \cdot 1) = 0 + 0 + 1 + 0 + 0 = 1$$.

3. **Sum Bits ($S_i = P_i \oplus C_i$):**
   - $S_0 = P_0 \oplus C_0 = 0 \oplus 1 = 1$.
   - $S_1 = P_1 \oplus C_1 = 0 \oplus 1 = 1$.
   - $S_2 = P_2 \oplus C_2 = 1 \oplus 1 = 0$.
   - $S_3 = P_3 \oplus C_3 = 1 \oplus 1 = 0$.
   - Result: $S = 0011_2$ with $C_4 = 1 \implies 10011_2 = 19_{10}$.
   - Verification: $A = 11_{10}, B = 7_{10}, C_0 = 1 \implies 11 + 7 + 1 = 19_{10}$. Exact match!
</ModelAnswer>

### Problem 3: Group Generate and Group Propagate Derivations

Derive the formal expressions for Block Propagate ($P_G$) and Block Generate ($G_G$) for a 4-bit adder block (bits $0$ to $3$). Prove that $C_4 = G_G + P_G C_0$.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Starting from the expanded equation for $C_4$:**
   $$C_4 = G_3 + P_3 G_2 + P_3 P_2 G_1 + P_3 P_2 P_1 G_0 + P_3 P_2 P_1 P_0 C_0$$

2. **Factoring the initial carry $C_0$:**
   Notice that $C_0$ appears only in the final product term:
   $$C_4 = [G_3 + P_3 G_2 + P_3 P_2 G_1 + P_3 P_2 P_1 G_0] + [P_3 P_2 P_1 P_0] C_0$$

3. **Defining the block terms:**
   - Define **Block Generate ($G_G$):**
     $$G_G = G_3 + P_3 G_2 + P_3 P_2 G_1 + P_3 P_2 P_1 G_0$$
     This term is true if a carry is generated at stage 3, or generated at stage 2 and propagated through stage 3, or generated at stage 1 and propagated through stages 2 and 3, or generated at stage 0 and propagated through stages 1, 2, and 3. In all these cases, a carry exits the block without requiring $C_0$.
   - Define **Block Propagate ($P_G$):**
     $$P_G = P_3 P_2 P_1 P_0$$
     This term is 1 only when all four stages propagate an incoming carry.

4. **Conclusion:**
   Substituting $G_G$ and $P_G$ directly into the equation gives:
   $$C_4 = G_G + P_G C_0$$
   This proves that a 4-bit block behaves like a single larger adder stage with generate signal $G_G$ and propagate signal $P_G$.
</ModelAnswer>

### Problem 4: Carry-Select vs Carry-Lookahead architectural comparison

Compare the Carry-Select Adder (CSA) and Carry-Lookahead Adder (CLA) across three engineering criteria: silicon area, propagation delay scaling, and design layout modularity.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Silicon Area (Transistor Count):**
   - **CLA:** Moderate area overhead. Requires dedicated multi-input AND/OR lookahead carry generator circuits, but does not duplicate the underlying full adders.
   - **CSA:** High area overhead. Duplicates the ripple adder hardware for each block (one adder for $C_{in}=0$ and one for $C_{in}=1$), plus a bank of 2-to-1 multiplexers for sum and carry selection. Consumes roughly 80% to 100% more area than a ripple adder.

2. **Propagation Delay Scaling:**
   - **CLA:** Logarithmic scaling $O(\log n)$ when using multilevel hierarchical lookahead trees. Offers the highest speed for wide words (32-bit and 64-bit).
   - **CSA:** Sub-linear scaling $O(\sqrt{n})$ when block sizes are tuned uniformly or progressively. Slightly slower than CLA for large word sizes, but significantly faster than ripple carry.

3. **Design Layout Modularity and Routing:**
   - **CLA:** Irregular interconnect topology. The carry equations have irregular numbers of inputs ($1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ inputs), causing non-uniform routing density and routing congestion in VLSI standard-cell layouts.
   - **CSA:** Highly regular and modular layout. Composed of repetitive standard ripple-adder slices and uniform multiplexer banks. Much easier to place and route automatically in VLSI design tools.
</ModelAnswer>

### Problem 5: Carry-Skip (Bypass) Adder timing analysis and optimal block sizing

A 16-bit adder is implemented as a carry-skip adder (CSA) partitioned into $k = 4$ uniform blocks of $m = 4$ bits each (Block 0: bits 0-3; Block 1: bits 4-7; Block 2: bits 8-11; Block 3: bits 12-15).
The circuit specifications are:
- Full adder ripple carry delay per bit: $t_{carry} = 1.0\text{ ns}$
- 4-input AND gate delay to compute block propagate $P_{G,j} = P_{4j+3} P_{4j+2} P_{4j+1} P_{4j}$: $t_{AND} = 1.5\text{ ns}$
- 2-to-1 skip multiplexer delay per block: $t_{\text{skip}} = 1.2\text{ ns}$
- XOR delay to produce final sum bits from incoming carry: $t_{XOR} = 1.8\text{ ns}$
- Primary inputs $A$ and $B$ arrive at $t = 0\text{ ns}$, with $C_0 = 0$.

1. Formulate the worst-case propagation delay path through the uniform 16-bit carry-skip adder.
2. Calculate the worst-case delay $t_{\text{worst}}$ and compare the speedup achieved relative to a 16-bit ripple-carry adder ($16 \times 1.0\text{ ns} = 16.0\text{ ns}$ carry ripple).
3. Explain why variable (non-uniform) block sizing (such as 2, 3, 4, 4, 3) reduces the worst-case delay compared to uniform block sizing.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Critical Path Formulation:**
   The worst-case delay in a carry-skip adder occurs when:
   - A carry is generated at the least-significant bit of the first block (bit 0: $A_0=B_0=1$).
   - The carry ripples through the remaining $(m - 1) = 3$ bits of Block 0 to produce carry-out $C_4$.
   - All intermediate blocks (Block 1 and Block 2) have all bit propagates equal to 1 ($P_i = 1$), allowing the carry to skip through their 2-to-1 multiplexers without rippling through their internal adders.
   - The carry enters the final block (Block 3), where it cannot skip (at least one $P_i = 0$), forcing the carry to ripple through all $m$ bits of the final block to compute the final sum bit $S_{15}$.

   The total delay formula is:
   $$t_{\text{worst}} = t_{\text{gen, block 0}} + (m_0 - 1)t_{carry} + (k - 2)t_{\text{skip}} + (m_{k-1})t_{carry} + t_{XOR}$$
   Here $t_{\text{gen, block 0}} = t_{carry} = 1.0\text{ ns}$ (or $m_0 t_{carry} = 4.0\text{ ns}$ through Block 0).

2. **Numerical Calculation:**
   - Time for Block 0 to generate and ripple carry $C_4$:
     $$t_{\text{Block 0}} = 4 \times t_{carry} = 4 \times 1.0\text{ ns} = 4.0\text{ ns}$$
     *(Note: $P_{G,0}$ is computed in $t_{AND} = 1.5\text{ ns} < 4.0\text{ ns}$, so the skip MUX control line is already stable).*
   - Skipping intermediate blocks (Block 1 and Block 2, $k - 2 = 2$ skips):
     $$t_{\text{skips}} = 2 \times t_{\text{skip}} = 2 \times 1.2\text{ ns} = 2.4\text{ ns}$$
   - Carry arrives at Block 3 at time:
     $$t_{\text{arrive, Block 3}} = 4.0\text{ ns} + 2.4\text{ ns} = 6.4\text{ ns}$$
   - In Block 3, carry ripples through bits 12, 13, 14, and 15 ($4 \times t_{carry} = 4.0\text{ ns}$), then generates $S_{15}$ via XOR ($t_{XOR} = 1.8\text{ ns}$, or $(m - 1)t_{carry} + t_{XOR} = 3 \times 1.0 + 1.8 = 4.8\text{ ns}$):
     $$t_{\text{Block 3}} = (4 - 1) \times 1.0\text{ ns} + 1.8\text{ ns} = 4.8\text{ ns}$$
   - Total worst-case propagation delay:
     $$t_{\text{worst}} = 4.0\text{ ns} + 2.4\text{ ns} + 4.8\text{ ns} = 11.2\text{ ns}$$

   **Speedup vs Ripple-Carry:**
   In a 16-bit ripple-carry adder:
   $$t_{\text{RCA}} = 15 \times 1.0\text{ ns} + 1.8\text{ ns} = 16.8\text{ ns}$$
   $$\text{Speedup} = \frac{16.8\text{ ns}}{11.2\text{ ns}} \approx 1.50\times \quad (\approx 33.3\%\text{ delay reduction})$$

3. **Why Variable Block Sizing Outperforms Uniform Sizing:**
   In uniform sizing, the carry arrives at later blocks after significant skip delay. While the carry is skipping through middle blocks, the early bits of later blocks are sitting idle.
   By making the middle blocks larger and the edge blocks smaller (e.g., sizes $2, 3, 4, 4, 3$):
   - First block has fewer bits (e.g., 2 bits), so it generates $C_2$ very quickly ($2.0\text{ ns}$ instead of $4.0\text{ ns}$).
   - Middle blocks have more bits (e.g., 4 bits). Since they are bypassed via a single skip MUX ($1.2\text{ ns}$), making them larger allows more bits to be skipped per unit delay.
   - Final block has fewer bits (e.g., 3 bits), so the rippling required to finalize the most significant sums is shortened.
   Variable block sizing balances the arrival times across all paths, achieving $O(\sqrt{n})$ delay with minimal hardware overhead.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
A common misconception is assuming that the skip path is taken for every block simultaneously. If a block generates its own carry internally ($P_{G,j} = 0$), the skip MUX selects the internal ripple path, not the bypass path. The worst case is a hybrid path: rippling in the first block, skipping intermediate blocks, and rippling in the final block.
</Callout>
</ModelAnswer>

### Problem 6: Parallel Prefix Adders and Prefix Operator associativity

Parallel prefix adders (including Kogge-Stone and Brent-Kung) reformulate carry computation as a prefix problem using the carry generate ($G$) and carry propagate ($P$) pairs.
The fundamental prefix associative operator $\circ$ combines two adjacent blocks $(G_a, P_a)$ and $(G_b, P_b)$ (where block $b$ is more significant than block $a$):
$$(G_{b:a}, P_{b:a}) = (G_b, P_b) \circ (G_a, P_a) = (G_b + P_b G_a, \, P_b P_a)$$

1. Formally prove that the prefix operator $\circ$ is associative:
   $$(G_c, P_c) \circ \big[(G_b, P_b) \circ (G_a, P_a)\big] = \big[(G_c, P_c) \circ (G_b, P_b)\big] \circ (G_a, P_a)$$
2. Explain the fundamental architectural consequence of associativity in parallel computer arithmetic.
3. Compare the 32-bit Kogge-Stone and 32-bit Brent-Kung adders across three design parameters: logic tree depth (number of prefix levels), total prefix cell count, and maximum wire fan-out/routing congestion.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Proof of Associativity:**
   Let:
   - Left-hand association: evaluate $(G_{ba}, P_{ba}) = (G_b, P_b) \circ (G_a, P_a) = (G_b + P_b G_a, P_b P_a)$.
     Then combine with $(G_c, P_c)$:
     $$
     \begin{aligned}
     (G_c, P_c) \circ (G_{ba}, P_{ba}) &= \big(G_c + P_c G_{ba}, \, P_c P_{ba}\big) \\
     &= \big(G_c + P_c (G_b + P_b G_a), \, P_c (P_b P_a)\big) \\
     &= \big(G_c + P_c G_b + P_c P_b G_a, \, P_c P_b P_a\big)
     \end{aligned}
     $$

   - Right-hand association: evaluate $(G_{cb}, P_{cb}) = (G_c, P_c) \circ (G_b, P_b) = (G_c + P_c G_b, P_c P_b)$.
     Then combine with $(G_a, P_a)$:
     $$
     \begin{aligned}
     (G_{cb}, P_{cb}) \circ (G_a, P_a) &= \big(G_{cb} + P_{cb} G_a, \, P_{cb} P_a\big) \\
     &= \big((G_c + P_c G_b) + (P_c P_b) G_a, \, (P_c P_b) P_a\big) \\
     &= \big(G_c + P_c G_b + P_c P_b G_a, \, P_c P_b P_a\big)
     \end{aligned}
     $$
   Both groupings produce the identical result $(G_c + P_c G_b + P_c P_b G_a, P_c P_b P_a)$. Therefore, the prefix operator $\circ$ is strictly associative.

2. **Architectural Consequence of Associativity:**
   Because $\circ$ is associative, prefix operations can be grouped in any order without altering the mathematical result. This allows the sequential linear recurrence $C_{i} = G_{i-1} + P_{i-1} C_{i-1}$ (which has $O(n)$ chain depth) to be restructured into a balanced binary tree of depth $O(\log_2 n)$.
   All $n$ prefix carries can be evaluated concurrently in $\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ gate levels, breaking the sequential carry chain bottleneck.

3. **Comparison of 32-bit Kogge-Stone vs Brent-Kung Adders:**

| Metric | 32-bit Kogge-Stone Adder | 32-bit Brent-Kung Adder |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Logic Levels (Tree Depth)** | $\lceil \log_2(32) \rceil = 5$ levels (fastest possible prefix tree) | $2\log_2(32) - 2 = 8$ levels (forward tree + inverse distribution tree) |
| **Prefix Cell Count** | High: $n \log_2 n - n + 1 = 32 \times 5 - 32 + 1 = 129$ cells | Low: $2n - 2 - \log_2 n = 64 - 2 - 5 = 57$ cells |
| **Wiring Fan-out and Congestion** | High fan-out (2 at each level) and dense overlapping lateral tracks | Low fan-out (maximum fan-out = 2) and sparse, tree-like non-overlapping routing |
| **Silicon Area & Power** | Large area, high dynamic routing power | Minimal area, low switching power |
| **Primary Industrial Use** | High-performance CPU execution units where raw clock cycle time dominates | Low-power mobile and embedded processors where silicon footprint and power are critical |

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
Students often claim that Brent-Kung is faster because it uses fewer cells. Fewer cells reduce power and layout area, but the trade-off is a deeper tree ($2\log_2 n - 2$ levels vs $\log_2 n$ levels for Kogge-Stone). Kogge-Stone is strictly faster because it minimizes the number of logic levels on the critical path.
</Callout>
</ModelAnswer>
