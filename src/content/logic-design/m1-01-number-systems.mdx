# Number Systems

Computers are built from electronic switches that settle into two stable voltage states: HIGH and LOW. In [signals](/courses/logic-design/signals#logic-levels-and-threshold-voltages), we label these two states **1** and **0**. Because hardware relies on two physical states, digital circuits work in the binary number system.

A single binary digit is called a **bit**, the smallest unit of information in digital computing. To represent larger quantities, hardware groups bits into standard sizes:
- A **nibble** is 4 bits, which can hold $2^4 = 16$ distinct values (the exact capacity of one hexadecimal digit).
- A **byte** is 8 bits, which can hold $2^8 = 256$ distinct values. It is the standard addressable unit of computer memory.
- A **word** is the natural data width a processor handles in one cycle (such as 16 bits on older chips, 32 bits on microcontrollers, and 64 bits on modern processors).

People think in decimal (base 10). Engineers write byte patterns, register values, and memory addresses in octal (base 8) and hexadecimal (base 16). Working with digital hardware means moving comfortably between these number systems.

## Positional number representation and the radix

In a **positional number system**, the value of a digit depends on both the symbol and its position relative to the **radix point** (the decimal point in base 10, or binary point in base 2). The base, or **radix** ($r$), specifies how many digit symbols exist in that system:

$$
\text{Digit set } \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, r-1\}
$$

A number $N$ with $n$ integer digits and $m$ fractional digits is written as a sequence of digit coefficients:

$$
N = (a_{n-1} a_{n-2} \dots a_1 a_0 . a_{-1} a_{-2} \dots a_{-m})_r
$$

The numerical value of this sequence comes from its **polynomial expansion**:

$$
N = \sum_{i=-m}^{n-1} a_i r^i = a_{n-1} r^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 r^1 + a_0 r^0 + a_{-1} r^{-1} + \dots + a_{-m} r^{-m}
$$

In this expansion, each position $i$ has a **column weight** equal to $r^i$:
- The digit immediately to the left of the radix point ($a_0$) has weight $r^0 = 1$.
- Moving left into higher integer positions, column weights multiply by $r$ at each step ($r^1, r^2, r^3, \dots, r^{n-1}$).
- Moving right past the radix point into fractional positions, column weights divide by $r$ at each step ($r^{-1}, r^{-2}, r^{-3}, \dots, r^{-m}$).

The leftmost digit ($a_{n-1}$) has the highest column weight and is called the **most significant digit** (MSD, or **most significant bit**, MSB, in base 2). The rightmost integer digit ($a_0$) has the lowest integer weight and is called the **least significant digit** (LSD, or **least significant bit**, LSB, in base 2).

| System | Radix ($r$) | Allowed Digits | Integer Column Weights ($r^3, r^2, r^1, r^0$) | Fractional Weights ($r^{-1}, r^{-2}, r^{-3}$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Binary** | 2 | 0, 1 | 8, 4, 2, 1 | 0.5, 0.25, 0.125 |
| **Octal** | 8 | 0 to 7 | 512, 64, 8, 1 | 0.125, 0.015625, 0.001953 |
| **Decimal** | 10 | 0 to 9 | 1000, 100, 10, 1 | 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 |
| **Hexadecimal** | 16 | 0 to 9, A to F | 4096, 256, 16, 1 | 0.0625, 0.003906, 0.000244 |

In hexadecimal, we need 16 distinct symbols. After the digit $9$, we use the letters $\text{A}$ through $\text{F}$ to represent the values $10$ through $15$:

$$
\text{A}=10, \quad \text{B}=11, \quad \text{C}=12, \quad \text{D}=13, \quad \text{E}=14, \quad \text{F}=15
$$

<Callout tone="orange" label="Mistake students make">
**Using digit symbols that equal or exceed the radix.** In any base $r$, the maximum allowable digit symbol is $r - 1$. Writing $(1021)_2$ is invalid because digit 2 does not exist in binary. Similarly, $(784)_8$ is invalid because digit 8 does not exist in octal. When an addition or count reaches $r$, it carries into the next column to the left as $(10)_r$.
</Callout>

## Binary to decimal

To convert **binary to decimal**, or to convert from any base $r$ to decimal, multiply each digit by its column weight $r^i$ and add the products. A **binary** number sums powers of 2.

### Worked conversions to decimal

**Example 1: Binary to decimal**

Convert $(1101101.101)_2$ to decimal:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(1101101.101)_2 &= 1(2^6) + 1(2^5) + 0(2^4) + 1(2^3) + 1(2^2) + 0(2^1) + 1(2^0) + 1(2^{-1}) + 0(2^{-2}) + 1(2^{-3}) \\
&= 64 + 32 + 0 + 8 + 4 + 0 + 1 + 0.5 + 0 + 0.125 \\
&= 109.625_{10}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Example 2: Octal to decimal**

Convert $(2374.25)_8$ to decimal:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2374.25)_8 &= 2(8^3) + 3(8^2) + 7(8^1) + 4(8^0) + 2(8^{-1}) + 5(8^{-2}) \\
&= 2(512) + 3(64) + 7(8) + 4(1) + 2(0.125) + 5(0.015625) \\
&= 1024 + 192 + 56 + 4 + 0.25 + 0.078125 \\
&= 1276.328125_{10}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Example 3: Hexadecimal to decimal**

Convert $(\text{E5A.4})_{16}$ to decimal:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\text{E5A.4})_{16} &= 14(16^2) + 5(16^1) + 10(16^0) + 4(16^{-1}) \\
&= 14(256) + 5(16) + 10(1) + 4(0.0625) \\
&= 3584 + 80 + 10 + 0.25 \\
&= 3674.25_{10}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Example 4: Arbitrary base to decimal**

Convert $(432.1)_5$ to decimal:

$$
(432.1)_5 = 4(5^2) + 3(5^1) + 2(5^0) + 1(5^{-1}) = 4(25) + 3(5) + 2(1) + 0.2 = 100 + 15 + 2 + 0.2 = 117.2_{10}
$$

<Callout tone="orange" label="Mistake students make">
**Confusing hexadecimal letter values with 1-based alphabetic indices.** Students often write $\text{A} = 1, \text{B} = 2, \dots$ instead of $\text{A} = 10, \text{B} = 11, \text{C} = 12, \text{D} = 13, \text{E} = 14, \text{F} = 15$. Another common slip is treating $r^0$ as $0$ rather than $1$, which loses the units column entirely.
</Callout>

## Decimal to another base

To convert a decimal integer to **binary** or to any other base $r$, keep dividing by the radix $r$ using **successive division**. 

### Why repeated division works

Consider the polynomial expansion of an integer $N$ in base $r$:

$$
N = a_{n-1} r^{n-1} + a_{n-2} r^{n-2} + \dots + a_1 r^1 + a_0
$$

Factor out the base $r$ from all terms except the last:

$$
N = r \cdot \left( a_{n-1} r^{n-2} + \dots + a_1 \right) + a_0 = r \cdot Q_0 + a_0
$$

When we divide $N$ by $r$, the quotient is $Q_0$ and the integer remainder is $a_0 = N \bmod r$. The remainder is the least significant digit (LSD, weight $r^0$).

Next, dividing the quotient $Q_0$ by $r$ yields:

$$
Q_0 = r \cdot Q_1 + a_1
$$

The remainder of this second division is $a_1$ (weight $r^1$). Repeating this division until the quotient becomes 0 generates all digit coefficients:

- The **first remainder** generated is the **least significant digit** (LSD or LSB, rightmost position, weight $r^0$).
- The **last remainder** generated is the **most significant digit** (MSD or MSB, leftmost position, weight $r^{n-1}$).
- Write the converted number by reading the remainders from **bottom to top** (last generated to first generated).

### Step-by-step conversion of decimal 157

**To Binary ($r = 2$):**

$$
\begin{aligned}
157 \div 2 &= 78 \quad \text{remainder } 1 \quad (\text{LSB, } a_0) \\
78 \div 2 &= 39 \quad \text{remainder } 0 \quad (a_1) \\
39 \div 2 &= 19 \quad \text{remainder } 1 \quad (a_2) \\
19 \div 2 &= 9 \quad \text{remainder } 1 \quad (a_3) \\
9 \div 2 &= 4 \quad \text{remainder } 1 \quad (a_4) \\
4 \div 2 &= 2 \quad \text{remainder } 0 \quad (a_5) \\
2 \div 2 &= 1 \quad \text{remainder } 0 \quad (a_6) \\
1 \div 2 &= 0 \quad \text{remainder } 1 \quad (\text{MSB, } a_7)
\end{aligned}
$$

Reading remainders from bottom to top yields: $157_{10} = 10011101_2$.

**To Octal ($r = 8$):**

$$
\begin{aligned}
157 \div 8 &= 19 \quad \text{remainder } 5 \quad (\text{LSD, } a_0) \\
19 \div 8 &= 2 \quad \text{remainder } 3 \quad (a_1) \\
2 \div 8 &= 0 \quad \text{remainder } 2 \quad (\text{MSD, } a_2)
\end{aligned}
$$

Reading from bottom to top yields: $157_{10} = 235_8$.

**To Hexadecimal ($r = 16$):**

$$
\begin{aligned}
157 \div 16 &= 9 \quad \text{remainder } 13 = \text{D} \quad (\text{LSD, } a_0) \\
9 \div 16 &= 0 \quad \text{remainder } 9 \quad (\text{MSD, } a_1)
\end{aligned}
$$

Reading from bottom to top yields: $157_{10} = 9\text{D}_{16}$.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Mistake students make">
**Reading integer division remainders from top to bottom.** Students often write down remainders in the order they compute them. That reverses the number, placing the least significant bit ($a_0$) into the highest position. The first remainder from dividing by $r$ is always the units place ($a_0$); the last remainder is the highest power ($a_{n-1}$). Read remainders from bottom to top.
</Callout>

## Why programmers love hex

Programmers use **hexadecimal** because $16 = 2^4$: each hex digit corresponds to exactly 4 bits (one nibble). That lets you write long binary strings compactly without doing arithmetic. Octal works the same way with groups of 3 bits, because $8 = 2^3$.

Mathematically, any binary number can be partitioned into blocks of $k$ bits (where $r = 2^k$):

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{k \cdot m - 1} b_i 2^i = \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} \left( \sum_{p=0}^{k-1} b_{j \cdot k + p} 2^p \right) (2^k)^j = \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} c_j r^j
$$

Each group of $k$ bits evaluates independently to a single digit $c_j$ in base $r$.

```
Octal Digits (3 bits, 2^3 = 8):
  0: 000    1: 001    2: 010    3: 011
  4: 100    5: 101    6: 110    7: 111

Hexadecimal Digits (4 bits, 2^4 = 16):
  0: 0000   1: 0001   2: 0010   3: 0011
  4: 0100   5: 0101   6: 0110   7: 0111
  8: 1000   9: 1001   A: 1010   B: 1011
  C: 1100   D: 1101   E: 1110   F: 1111
```

### The grouping rules

To convert a binary number to octal or hex:

1. **For the integer part:** Group bits starting from the **radix point moving left**. If the leftmost group has fewer than 3 or 4 bits, pad it on the left with leading zeros.
2. **For the fractional part:** Group bits starting from the **radix point moving right**. If the rightmost group has fewer than 3 or 4 bits, pad it on the right with trailing zeros.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Mistake students make">
**Grouping bits across the radix point or padding fractional bits on the wrong side.** Grouping must start *at* the radix point and move outward in both directions. Never group across the radix point. Padding $0.1_2$ on the left into $0.0001_2$ corrupts the number from $0.5_{10}$ to $0.0625_{10}$. For fractional parts, pad with trailing zeros on the right: $0.1_2 \to 0.1000_2 = 0.8_{16} = 0.5_{10}$.
</Callout>

### Worked grouping examples

Convert the binary number $(10110110101.101011)_2$ to hexadecimal and octal:

**To Hexadecimal (groups of 4):**

- Integer part: $\underbrace{0101}_{5}\ \underbrace{1011}_{\text{B}}\ \underbrace{0101}_{5}$ (padded with one leading 0 on the left).
- Fractional part: $\underbrace{1010}_{\text{A}}\ \underbrace{1100}_{\text{C}}$ (padded with two trailing 0s on the right).
- Result: $(10110110101.101011)_2 = (5\text{B}5.\text{AC})_{16}$.

**To Octal (groups of 3):**

- Integer part: $\underbrace{010}_{2}\ \underbrace{110}_{6}\ \underbrace{110}_{6}\ \underbrace{101}_{5}$ (padded with one leading 0 on the left).
- Fractional part: $\underbrace{101}_{5}\ \underbrace{011}_{3}$.
- Result: $(10110110101.101011)_2 = (2665.53)_8$.

### Converting between octal and hexadecimal

To convert directly between octal and hex, use binary as the intermediate stepping stone:

$$
\text{Octal} \longleftrightarrow \text{Binary (3-bit groups)} \longleftrightarrow \text{Binary (4-bit groups)} \longleftrightarrow \text{Hexadecimal}
$$

For example, convert $(\text{7B.4})_{16}$ to octal:

1. Expand each hex digit into 4 bits: $\text{7} = 0111$, $\text{B} = 1011$, $.4 = .0100 \implies 01111011.0100_2$.
2. Regroup into 3 bits starting outward from the radix point:
   - Integer: $\underbrace{001}_{1}\ \underbrace{111}_{7}\ \underbrace{011}_{3}$
   - Fractional: $\underbrace{010}_{2}\ \underbrace{000}_{0}$
3. Read in octal: $(173.2)_8$.

<Callout tone="sky" label="Where you see this in hardware">
Hexadecimal is standard shorthand in assembly language, circuit schematics, and debuggers. A 32-bit register holds 8 hex digits, a 64-bit address holds 16 hex digits, and color codes like `#FF8800` represent red, green, and blue intensities as three 8-bit bytes ($255, 136, 0$). You also compute addresses by hand when you [calculate 8086 physical addresses](/courses/microprocessors/segments-and-addresses).
</Callout>

## Fractions

A decimal fraction cannot be converted by division because dividing a fraction by $r$ would make it smaller. Instead, we convert fractional parts using **successive multiplication** by the target base $r$.

### Why repeated multiplication works

Consider the fractional expansion of a number $F$ in base $r$:

$$
F = a_{-1} r^{-1} + a_{-2} r^{-2} + a_{-3} r^{-3} + \dots
$$

Multiplying $F$ by $r$ scales the first fractional digit into the integer units position:

$$
F \times r = a_{-1} + \left( a_{-2} r^{-1} + a_{-3} r^{-2} + \dots \right) = a_{-1} + F_1
$$

The extracted integer portion $a_{-1}$ is the most significant fractional digit (weight $r^{-1}$). The remaining fractional part $F_1$ is multiplied by $r$ again to extract $a_{-2}$, and so on:

- The **first integer** extracted is the **most significant fractional digit** ($a_{-1}$, immediately to the right of the radix point).
- Successive integers form subsequent fractional positions moving to the right.
- Read the digits from **top to bottom** (in the exact order they were generated).
- Stop when the fractional part becomes exactly zero (terminating fraction) or when you reach the required number of bits or detect a repeating cycle.

**Reading fractional multiplication results from bottom to top.** Because integer division reads bottom to top, students often read fractional multiplication results bottom to top as well. Integer division produces the least significant bit first. Fractional multiplication produces the most significant fractional bit ($a_{-1}$) first. Record fractional digits from top to bottom.

### Terminating vs repeating fractions

A rational fraction $\frac{p}{q}$ (expressed in lowest terms) terminates in base $r$ if and only if every prime factor of the denominator $q$ also divides the radix $r$.

- In decimal (base 10), the prime factors of 10 are **2 and 5**. Fractions such as $\frac{1}{2} = 0.5$, $\frac{1}{4} = 0.25$, $\frac{1}{5} = 0.2$, and $\frac{1}{10} = 0.1$ terminate.
- In binary (base 2), the only prime factor of 2 is **2**.
- Any fraction whose reduced denominator has a prime factor other than 2 (such as 3, 5, or 7) will never terminate in binary.

Even familiar decimal fractions like $0.1_{10} = \frac{1}{10}$ and $0.7_{10} = \frac{7}{10}$ produce infinitely repeating bit streams in binary:

**Example 1: Terminating binary fraction ($0.6875_{10}$)**

$$
\begin{aligned}
0.6875 \times 2 &= \mathbf{1}.375 \quad \to \text{integer } 1 \quad (a_{-1}) \\
0.375 \times 2 &= \mathbf{0}.75 \quad \to \text{integer } 0 \quad (a_{-2}) \\
0.75 \times 2 &= \mathbf{1}.50 \quad \to \text{integer } 1 \quad (a_{-3}) \\
0.50 \times 2 &= \mathbf{1}.00 \quad \to \text{integer } 1 \quad (a_{-4}, \text{fraction is } 0)
\end{aligned}
$$

Reading top to bottom gives: $0.6875_{10} = 0.1011_2$.

**Example 2: Infinitely repeating binary fraction ($0.7_{10}$)**

$$
\begin{aligned}
0.7 \times 2 &= \mathbf{1}.4 \quad \to \text{integer } 1 \quad (a_{-1}) \\
0.4 \times 2 &= \mathbf{0}.8 \quad \to \text{integer } 0 \quad (a_{-2}) \\
0.8 \times 2 &= \mathbf{1}.6 \quad \to \text{integer } 1 \quad (a_{-3}) \\
0.6 \times 2 &= \mathbf{1}.2 \quad \to \text{integer } 1 \quad (a_{-4}) \\
0.2 \times 2 &= \mathbf{0}.4 \quad \to \text{integer } 0 \quad (\text{repeats from line 2!})
\end{aligned}
$$

The cycle `0110` repeats forever: $0.7_{10} = 0.1\,0110\,0110\,0110\ldots_2 = 0.1\overline{0110}_2$.

Similarly, decimal $0.1_{10} = 0.0001100110011\dots_2 = 0.0\overline{0011}_2$.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Mistake students make">
**Assuming finite decimal numbers can always be represented exactly in computer memory.** Many programmers assume numbers like $0.1$ or $0.2$ are stored with exact precision. Because 10 has prime factor 5, $0.1_{10}$ never terminates in base 2. In IEEE 754 binary floating point, it rounds to a finite register width (23 or 52 fraction bits). This truncation causes quirks like `0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3` evaluating to `false`.
</Callout>

## Practice problems

### Problem 1: Mixed base conversions

Convert the decimal number $73.3125_{10}$ into:
1. Binary
2. Octal
3. Hexadecimal

<ModelAnswer>
First separate the integer part ($73$) and fractional part ($0.3125$):

**1. Integer part ($73_{10}$):**
Use successive division by 2:
- $73 \div 2 = 36$ remainder 1 (LSB, $a_0$)
- $36 \div 2 = 18$ remainder 0 ($a_1$)
- $18 \div 2 = 9$ remainder 0 ($a_2$)
- $9 \div 2 = 4$ remainder 1 ($a_3$)
- $4 \div 2 = 2$ remainder 0 ($a_4$)
- $2 \div 2 = 1$ remainder 0 ($a_5$)
- $1 \div 2 = 0$ remainder 1 (MSB, $a_6$)

Reading remainders bottom-to-top gives the integer binary: $1001001_2$.

**2. Fractional part ($0.3125_{10}$):**
Use successive multiplication by 2:
- $0.3125 \times 2 = \mathbf{0}.625 \implies 0$ ($a_{-1}$)
- $0.625 \times 2 = \mathbf{1}.25 \implies 1$ ($a_{-2}$)
- $0.25 \times 2 = \mathbf{0}.5 \implies 0$ ($a_{-3}$)
- $0.5 \times 2 = \mathbf{1}.0 \implies 1$ ($a_{-4}$, fraction reaches 0)

Reading integers top-to-bottom gives the fractional binary: $0.0101_2$.

Combine both parts:
1. **Binary:** $1001001.0101_2$
2. **Octal:** Group by 3 bits starting outward from the radix point:
   - Integer: $\underbrace{001}_{1}\ \underbrace{001}_{1}\ \underbrace{001}_{1}$ (padded with two leading 0s)
   - Fractional: $\underbrace{010}_{2}\ \underbrace{100}_{4}$ (padded with one trailing 0)
   - Result: $111.24_8$.
3. **Hexadecimal:** Group by 4 bits starting outward from the radix point:
   - Integer: $\underbrace{0100}_{4}\ \underbrace{1001}_{9}$ (padded with one leading 0)
   - Fractional: $\underbrace{0101}_{5}$
   - Result: $49.5_{16}$.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
When converting fractional binary to octal, students frequently group $.0101_2$ as $\underbrace{010}_{2}\ \underbrace{1}_{1} = .21_8$. Incomplete fractional groups must be padded on the *right* with zeros: $.0101_2 \to .010\ 100_2 = .24_8$. Padding on the left or failing to pad gives the wrong value.
</Callout>
</ModelAnswer>

### Problem 2: Arbitrary base evaluation

Determine the decimal equivalent of the number $(342.13)_5$, and state the minimum radix required for the number $(7\text{B}2)_r$ to be valid.

<ModelAnswer>
1. **Decimal equivalent of $(342.13)_5$:**
   Apply the polynomial expansion with radix $r = 5$:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   (342.13)_5 &= 3(5^2) + 4(5^1) + 2(5^0) + 1(5^{-1}) + 3(5^{-2}) \\
   &= 3(25) + 4(5) + 2(1) + 1\left(\frac{1}{5}\right) + 3\left(\frac{1}{25}\right) \\
   &= 75 + 20 + 2 + 0.2 + 0.12 \\
   &= 97.32_{10}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. **Minimum radix for $(7\text{B}2)_r$:**
   In any base $r$, all digit symbols must be strictly less than $r$:
   $$\text{Digit set} \in \{0, 1, \dots, r-1\} \implies r > \max(\text{digits})$$
   The digit symbol $\text{B}$ represents decimal value $11$. Therefore, the radix must satisfy $r > 11$, which means the minimum integer radix is $r = 12$ (duodecimal).

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
Students often answer $r = 11$ because $\text{B} = 11$. Remember that in base 11, the allowed digits are only $0$ through $10$ ($\text{A}$). Digit $\text{B}$ ($11$) requires a radix of at least 12.
</Callout>
</ModelAnswer>

Convert $(653.42)_8$ directly to hexadecimal without converting to decimal first.

<ModelAnswer>
**Step 1: Convert each octal digit to a 3-bit binary group:**

### Problem 4: Truncation error analysis

Convert decimal $0.2_{10}$ to an 8-bit fractional binary number, and calculate the absolute truncation error between the true decimal value and its 8-bit binary representation.

<ModelAnswer>
Multiply by 2 successively to extract 8 binary digits:
1. $0.2 \times 2 = \mathbf{0}.4 \implies 0$ ($a_{-1}$)
2. $0.4 \times 2 = \mathbf{0}.8 \implies 0$ ($a_{-2}$)
3. $0.8 \times 2 = \mathbf{1}.6 \implies 1$ ($a_{-3}$)
4. $0.6 \times 2 = \mathbf{1}.2 \implies 1$ ($a_{-4}$)
5. $0.2 \times 2 = \mathbf{0}.4 \implies 0$ ($a_{-5}$, cycle repeats!)
6. $0.4 \times 2 = \mathbf{0}.8 \implies 0$ ($a_{-6}$)
7. $0.8 \times 2 = \mathbf{1}.6 \implies 1$ ($a_{-7}$)
8. $0.6 \times 2 = \mathbf{1}.2 \implies 1$ ($a_{-8}$)

The 8-bit truncated binary representation is:
$$
0.00110011_2
$$

**Evaluating this 8-bit binary fraction in decimal:**
$$
\begin{aligned}
0.00110011_2 &= 2^{-3} + 2^{-4} + 2^{-7} + 2^{-8} \\
&= \frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{16} + \frac{1}{128} + \frac{1}{256} \\
&= 0.125 + 0.0625 + 0.0078125 + 0.00390625 \\
&= 0.19921875_{10}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Absolute truncation error:**
$$
|0.2 - 0.19921875| = 0.00078125_{10} = \frac{1}{1280}_{10}
$$

Relative error:
$$
\frac{0.00078125}{0.2} \times 100\% = 0.390625\%
$$

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common exam pitfall">
Students sometimes confuse truncation (chopping off bits after the eighth bit) with rounding. Rounding examines the ninth bit ($a_{-9} = 0$), so round-to-nearest gives the same bit pattern here. When the next bit is 1, round-to-nearest adds 1 to the least significant bit, while truncation discards the rest.
</Callout>
</ModelAnswer>
