# The Physical Layer

Computing is interaction. You do something, the machine responds, and the loop continues. The machine has no intention and no awareness; it only reacts to structured input, so the quality of your input decides the quality of what you get back.

Most people never take this in. They treat computers as tools that sometimes work and sometimes don't, without realizing that fluency matters. Each layer of fluency removes friction between what you intend and what happens. The first layer is physical: where your hands meet the machine.

## Typing

Typing sits at the center of this layer, and it matters more than it seems. When you type slowly, the mechanics of getting words out keep interrupting your thoughts, and that gets frustrating. At around 60 words per minute you start to think and write at almost the same time, and at around ninety the keyboard stops being a barrier at all.

Speed is only part of it. What you really gain is that you no longer spend attention hunting for keys, so your brain stays with the idea instead of the tool.

<Callout tone="orange" label="The test">
You are fluent enough when you can finish typing a sentence whose end you had not yet planned when you started.
</Callout>

### Building the muscle

The path is mechanical. Learn the home row (index fingers on F and J; feel for the bumps), train accuracy first, and let speed follow. Stop looking at the keyboard. It will feel slow for about two weeks, and then the slowness goes away.

## Shortcuts

I remember a stretch when my mouse kept lagging and I was forced onto the keyboard. That taught me that almost everything on a computer can be done without leaving the keyboard, and once you take that in, the way you work changes.

You stop breaking off your thinking to navigate, and you move through tasks without pausing. People who live in shortcuts are faster, and they also think more coherently while they work, because their tools stop interrupting the thought.
