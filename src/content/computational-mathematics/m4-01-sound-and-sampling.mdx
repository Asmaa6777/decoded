## What a sound is

When you speak, your vocal cords push the air back and forth. At the listener's ear, the air pressure goes up, then down, then back up, over and over. **A sound is one number, air pressure, changing over time.**

One complete trip, up, down and back to the start, is a **cycle**. A **hertz (Hz)** is a count: cycles per second. A 3 Hz wave completes 3 cycles every second. Press play and count:

<WaveCycle />

The time one cycle takes is the **period**:

$$
T = \frac{1}{f}
$$

At 3 Hz, each cycle takes $1/3 \approx 0.33$ seconds.

You can't hear 3 Hz; it's far too slow. People hear from about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. A speaking voice vibrates at roughly 85 to 255 Hz, plus higher **overtones**, whole-number multiples of that frequency, which make your voice sound like you. Those are too fast to see but easy to hear:

<ToneButtons />

440 Hz means 440 cycles every second, so one cycle takes $1/440 \approx 2.3$ milliseconds.

## Sampling: what "44,100 per second" means

A microphone produces a smooth, continuous wave. A computer can't store a smooth curve, only a list of numbers. So it checks the wave at regular moments and writes down its value each time. Each written-down value is a **sample**, and the number of samples taken per second is the **sample rate**, written $F_s$.

The sample rate is also measured in hertz, but here hertz counts *measurements* per second, not cycles. A sample rate of 10 Hz means 10 measurements every second. Change how fast the wave moves and how often it's measured:

<SamplingDemo />

Now set the wave to 5 Hz and the sample rate to 7 Hz. The dots trace out a slow 2 Hz wave that isn't there at all. Sampling too slowly makes a fast wave look like a slower one; this is **aliasing**, the same word, and the same idea, as the [jagged edges in computer graphics](/courses/computer-graphics/aliasing). The rule that prevents it is the **Nyquist rule**: take more than 2 samples per cycle of the fastest wave you want to keep.

That rule explains the number 44,100. Hearing tops out near 20,000 Hz, so you need more than $2 \times 20{,}000 = 40{,}000$ samples per second. CDs use 44,100, which leaves some margin, and most audio files still use it, or 48,000.

| | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Gap between two samples | 22.7 µs | $1/44{,}100$ s, about 23 millionths of a second |
| Samples in a blink (about 0.1 s) | 4,410 | $0.1 \times 44{,}100$ |
| A 3-second recording | 132,300 numbers | $3 \times 44{,}100$ |

## A recording is a vector

Once a sound is a list of numbers, it's a **vector**, and everything in this course applies to it: adding vectors, scaling them, measuring their length with a norm, and `conv`. That's the bridge between audio and computational mathematics, and the rest of the project walks across it.

In the project you'll load a real recording with `audioread` (MATLAB) or `soundfile.read` (Python). Here, a 440 Hz tone built from scratch stands in for it, so the code runs anywhere:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
Fs = 44100;                      % samples per second
t = (0:3*Fs-1)' / Fs;            % the time of each sample, 3 seconds
x = sin(2*pi*440*t);             % a 440 Hz tone, one column vector
size(x)

ans =
       132300           1

% with a real file instead:
% [x, Fs] = audioread('voice.wav');   % stereo gives 2 columns
plot(t(1:400), x(1:400))         % the first 9 ms
```

```python
import numpy as np

Fs = 44100                       # samples per second
t = np.arange(3 * Fs) / Fs       # the time of each sample, 3 seconds
x = np.sin(2 * np.pi * 440 * t)  # a 440 Hz tone, one vector
print(x.shape)  # (132300,)
print(round(t[1] * 1e6, 1))  # 22.7
```

Speech models such as Whisper start exactly here: a recording becomes a vector of samples, usually resampled to 16,000 per second, since speech carries little above 8,000 Hz. Everything the machine learning model learns about your voice, it learns from that list of numbers.
