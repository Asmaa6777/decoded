## What a sound is

When you speak, your vocal cords push the air back and forth. At the listener's ear, the air pressure goes up, then down, then back up, over and over. **A sound is one number, air pressure, changing over time.**

One complete trip, up, down and back to the start, is a **cycle**. A **hertz (Hz)** is a count: cycles per second. The **frequency** $f$ of a wave is how many cycles it completes in one second, measured in hertz. A 3 Hz wave has $f = 3$, so it completes 3 cycles every second. Press play and count:

<WaveCycle />

The time one cycle takes is the **period** $T$, in seconds:

$$
T = \frac{1}{f}
$$

Three cycles fit into one second, so each cycle gets one third of that second: $T = 1/3 \approx 0.33$ seconds. In general, $f$ cycles share 1 second equally, so each one takes $1/f$ seconds.

You can't hear 3 Hz; it's far too slow. People hear from about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. A speaking voice vibrates at about 85 to 255 Hz. It also produces higher **overtones**, which are whole-number multiples of that starting frequency and give your voice its character. For example, a 100 Hz voice also carries energy at $2 \times 100 = 200$ Hz, $3 \times 100 = 300$ Hz, and so on. Those waves are too fast to see on a one-second graph, but easy to hear:

<ToneButtons />

The buttons above play 110, 220, 440 and 880 Hz tones. A 440 Hz tone completes 440 cycles every second, so one cycle takes $1/440 \approx 0.00227$ seconds, or about $2.3$ milliseconds. A **millisecond** (ms) is a thousandth of a second ($10^{-3}$ s). Doubling the frequency from 220 Hz to 440 Hz makes the wave vibrate twice as fast. Your ear hears each frequency doubling as one **octave** higher, the musical step from one note to the next note with the same name.

## Sampling: what "44,100 per second" means

A microphone turns air pressure into a smooth, continuous voltage, an [analog](/courses/logic-design/signals) wave. A computer cannot store a continuous curve; it stores a finite list of numbers. An analog-to-digital converter checks the wave at regular moments and writes down its value each time. Each recorded value is a **sample**, and the number of samples taken per second is the **sample rate**, written $F_s$.

The sample rate is also measured in hertz, because hertz simply means "per second". For the wave, $f$ counts cycles per second; for sampling, $F_s$ counts *measurements* per second. If a wave has $f = 2$ Hz and we measure it at $F_s = 10$ Hz, we take $10 / 2 = 5$ samples per cycle. Those samples sit $1 / F_s = 0.1$ seconds apart. Change how fast the wave moves and how often it is measured:

<SamplingDemo />

Now set the wave to $f = 5$ Hz and the sample rate to $F_s = 7$ Hz. Every $1/7$ of a second the wave advances by $5/7$ of a cycle. Advancing by $5/7$ of a full turn reaches the same point as stepping *backward* by $2/7$ of a turn ($1 - 5/7 = 2/7$). For sine, $\sin(2\pi \cdot \tfrac{5}{7} n) = \sin(2\pi n - 2\pi \cdot \tfrac{2}{7} n) = -\sin(2\pi \cdot \tfrac{2}{7} n)$, which is an inverted 2 Hz wave ($7 - 5 = 2$ Hz). The computer keeps only the dots, so after recording it cannot tell the real 5 Hz wave from a 2 Hz wave. Sampling too slowly makes a fast wave look like a slower one. This mistake is called **aliasing**, the same idea behind [jagged edges in computer graphics](/courses/computer-graphics/aliasing).

The rule that prevents it is the **Nyquist rule**:

$$
F_s > 2f
$$

You must take *more* than 2 samples per cycle of the fastest wave you want to keep. Each cycle rises to a high point (a peak) and falls to a low point (a trough). Catching that up-and-down motion takes at least one sample near the top and one near the bottom. Even $F_s = 2f$ is too slow: if you sample a sine wave at the exact moments it crosses zero, every sample is 0 and the wave disappears.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common mistake">
Confusing the wave's frequency $f$ with the sample rate $F_s$, because both are written in Hz. $f$ belongs to the sound in the air and tells how fast the wave vibrates. $F_s$ is set by the recorder and tells how often it measures. A 440 Hz note recorded at $F_s = 44{,}100$ Hz gives $44{,}100 / 440 \approx 100.2$ samples per cycle. Also watch the Nyquist factor of 2: keeping frequencies up to 20,000 Hz requires $F_s > 40{,}000$ Hz, not 20,000 Hz.
</Callout>

That rule explains the number 44,100. Human hearing stops near 20,000 Hz, so recording it takes more than $2 \times 20{,}000 = 40{,}000$ samples per second. The value $F_s = 44{,}100$ Hz leaves a transition band for the analog filter that blocks frequencies above 20 kHz before sampling, and historically matched early digital tape mastering formats (which stored 3 samples per video line on 25 Hz PAL and 30 Hz NTSC gear). Most audio files still use 44,100 or 48,000 Hz.

| | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Gap between two samples | 22.7 µs | $1/44{,}100 \text{ s} \approx 0.0000227 \text{ s}$; a **microsecond** (µs) is a millionth of a second ($10^{-6}$ s) |
| Samples in a blink (about 0.1 s) | 4,410 | $0.1 \text{ s} \times 44{,}100 \text{ samples/s}$ |
| A 3-second recording | 132,300 numbers | $3 \text{ s} \times 44{,}100 \text{ samples/s}$ |

## A recording is a vector

Once a sound is a list of numbers, it is a [vector](/courses/computational-mathematics/matrices-and-matlab#writing-a-matrix-down)—a column vector in MATLAB, or a 1D array of shape `(N,)` in NumPy, which behaves as a vector without needing a column dimension. Every vector tool in this course now works on audio: [adding vectors](/courses/computational-mathematics/matrices-and-matlab#adding-and-subtracting), [scaling them](/courses/computational-mathematics/matrices-and-matlab#multiplying-by-a-scalar), measuring their length with a norm, and `conv`.

In the project you will load a real recording with `audioread` (MATLAB) or `soundfile.read` (Python). A **mono** recording has one microphone channel and loads as a single column of samples. A **stereo** recording has left and right channels and loads as a 2-column matrix. Below, a 440 Hz sine wave replaces the audio file so the code runs anywhere.

Why `sin(2*pi*440*t)`? Trigonometric functions like $\sin(\theta)$ evaluate angles in radians, where a full $360^\circ$ circle is $2\pi$ radians (about $6.283$). Multiplying the time $t$ in seconds by $2\pi \cdot 440$ converts $440$ cycles per second into radians per second, making the wave complete 440 cycles each second. Sample $n$ (indexed from $n = 0$ at time $t = 0$) happens at time $t = n / F_s$ seconds. In MATLAB, array indices start at 1, so sample $n$ is stored in `x(n + 1)`. In Python, indices start at 0, so sample $n$ is `x[n]`. For $n = 0, 1, 2$, those times are $0$, $1/44{,}100 \approx 22.7\ \mu\text{s}$ and $2/44{,}100 \approx 45.4\ \mu\text{s}$. Plotting the first 400 samples shows $400 / 44{,}100 \approx 0.00907$ seconds (about 9 ms), which holds $440 \times 0.00907 \approx 4$ cycles of the tone:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
Fs = 44100;                      % samples per second
t = (0:3*Fs-1)' / Fs;            % 132300 sample times from 0 to ~3 s, as a column
x = sin(2*pi*440*t);             % a 440 Hz tone, one column vector
size(x)

ans =
       132300           1

% first three sample values, at t = 0, 22.7 us, 45.4 us:
round(x(1:3)', 4)

ans =
         0    0.0626    0.1251

% with a real file instead:
% [x, Fs] = audioread('voice.wav');   % returns samples x and file sample rate Fs
plot(t(1:400), x(1:400))         % the first 9 ms (~4 cycles)
```

```python
import numpy as np

Fs = 44100                       # samples per second
t = np.arange(3 * Fs) / Fs       # 132300 sample times from 0 to ~3 s
x = np.sin(2 * np.pi * 440 * t)  # a 440 Hz tone, one vector
print(x.shape)  # (132300,)
print(round(t[1] * 1e6, 1))  # 22.7
print(np.round(x[:3], 4).tolist())  # [0.0, 0.0626, 0.1251]

# with a real file instead (pip install soundfile):
# import soundfile as sf
# x, Fs = sf.read("voice.wav")   # returns samples x and file sample rate Fs
```

</CodeTabs>

Check the second sample ($n = 1$, stored in `x(2)` in MATLAB and `x[1]` in Python) by hand: at $t = 1/44{,}100$ s, the angle is $2\pi \times 440 / 44{,}100 \approx 0.06269$ radians, and $\sin(0.06269) \approx 0.0626$.

<Callout tone="sky" label="Where this goes in AI">
Speech models such as Whisper start the same way: a recording becomes a vector of samples, usually resampled to $F_s = 16{,}000$ Hz. By the Nyquist rule, 16,000 samples per second keeps frequencies up to $16{,}000 / 2 = 8{,}000$ Hz. That range covers human speech while cutting the vector to about a third of its CD length. The machine learning model learns everything about your voice from that list of numbers.
</Callout>
