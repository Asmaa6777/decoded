# Modular Arithmetic

You already do modular arithmetic when you work out that 5 hours after 10 o'clock is 3 o'clock. Numbers wrap around once they reach a limit, and that wrapping is what makes cryptography possible.

## The mod operation

$a \bmod n$ is the remainder when $a$ is divided by $n$, and the positive integer $n$ is called the **modulus**:

$$
a = \left\lfloor \frac{a}{n} \right\rfloor \cdot n + (a \bmod n)
$$

So $11 \bmod 7 = 4$ and $-11 \bmod 7 = 3$. Picture a clock with $n$ positions: positive numbers walk clockwise and negative numbers walk the other way.

<ModClock />

```matlab
mod(-28, 81)              % 53
mod(7653, 915)            % 333
mod([-4 -1 7 9], 3)       % 2  2  1  0
mod(1:10, 5)              % 1 2 3 4 0 1 2 3 4 0
```

<Callout tone="orange" label="A trap in other languages">
In C, Java and JavaScript, the `%` operator keeps the sign of the first number, so `-11 % 7` gives `-4`. MATLAB's `mod` and Python's `%` give `3`. This difference is a common source of bugs.
</Callout>

## Congruence

Two integers are **congruent modulo $n$** if they leave the same remainder, written $a \equiv b \pmod{n}$. They land on the same spot on the clock. For example, $73 \equiv 4 \pmod{23}$ and $21 \equiv -9 \pmod{10}$.

Congruence behaves much like equality:

1. $a \equiv b \pmod n$ exactly when $n \mid (a - b)$.
2. $a \equiv a$, for any $n$.
3. If $a \equiv b$ then $b \equiv a$.
4. If $a \equiv b$ and $b \equiv c$, then $a \equiv c$.

## You can reduce at any time

Addition, subtraction and multiplication all respect the modulus:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[(a \bmod n) + (b \bmod n)] \bmod n &= (a + b) \bmod n \\
[(a \bmod n) - (b \bmod n)] \bmod n &= (a - b) \bmod n \\
[(a \bmod n) \cdot (b \bmod n)] \bmod n &= (a \cdot b) \bmod n
\end{aligned}
$$

So you can shrink the numbers partway through and still get the right answer. Check it with $11 \bmod 8 = 3$ and $15 \bmod 8 = 7$. Then $(3 \cdot 7) \bmod 8 = 21 \bmod 8 = 5$, and directly $(11 \cdot 15) \bmod 8 = 165 \bmod 8 = 5$.

That also makes big powers easy. To find $10^7 \bmod 17$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
10^2 &= 100 \equiv 15 \\
10^4 &= 15^2 \equiv 4 \\
10^7 &= 10 \cdot 10^2 \cdot 10^4 \equiv 10 \cdot 15 \cdot 4 = 600 \equiv 5
\end{aligned}
$$

## Be careful when cancelling

If $(a + b) \equiv (a + c) \pmod n$, you can always cancel to get $b \equiv c$. For multiplication, you can only cancel when $a$ is coprime to $n$. For instance, $2 \cdot 3 \equiv 2 \cdot 7 \pmod 8$, since both are 6, yet $3 \not\equiv 7 \pmod 8$.
