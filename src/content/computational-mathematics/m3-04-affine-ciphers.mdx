# Affine Ciphers

A shift cipher only adds. If we multiply as well, we get a larger key space, and the lesson on [modular inverses](/courses/computational-mathematics/modular-inverses) suddenly matters: multiplying only works as a cipher when you can undo it.

## The cipher

An **affine transformation** uses a pair of keys, $k_1$ and $k_2$:

$$
E_{(k_1, k_2)}(m) = k_1 m + k_2 \pmod{26}
$$

To decrypt, subtract $k_2$ and then multiply by the inverse of $k_1$:

$$
D_{(k_1, k_2)}(c) = k_1^{-1}(c - k_2) \pmod{26}
$$

The inverse only exists when $\gcd(k_1, 26) = 1$ ([here's why](/courses/computational-mathematics/modular-inverses#when-an-inverse-exists)), and this is a hard requirement. ## Worked example: encrypting

Encrypt SECURITY with $k_1 = 7$ and $k_2 = 21$:

| Letter | $m$ | $7m + 21$ | mod 26 | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S | 18 | 147 | 17 | R |
| E | 4 | 49 | 23 | X |
| C | 2 | 35 | 9 | J |
| U | 20 | 161 | 5 | F |
| R | 17 | 140 | 10 | K |
| I | 8 | 77 | 25 | Z |
| T | 19 | 154 | 24 | Y |
| Y | 24 | 189 | 7 | H |

So SECURITY becomes RXJFKZYH.

## Worked example: decrypting

To decrypt VLXIJH, first find the inverse of 7 modulo 26 with the [extended Euclidean algorithm](/courses/computational-mathematics/modular-inverses#the-extended-euclidean-algorithm). It's 15, since $7 \cdot 15 = 105 = 4 \cdot 26 + 1$:

| Letter | $c$ | $15(c - 21)$ | mod 26 | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| V | 21 | 0 | 0 | A |
| L | 11 | -150 | 6 | G |
| X | 23 | 30 | 4 | E |
| I | 8 | -195 | 13 | N |
| J | 9 | -180 | 2 | C |
| H | 7 | -210 | 24 | Y |

The message is AGENCY.

## How strong is it?

There are 12 valid values for $k_1$, because [Euler's totient](/courses/computational-mathematics/primality-and-the-totient#eulers-totient-function) gives $\phi(26) = 12$, and 26 for $k_2$. That makes $12 \times 26 = 312$ keys, which a computer tries instantly. It also maps each letter to one fixed letter, so letter frequencies still give it away.

<Callout tone="sky" label="Try it yourself">
Use $k_1 = 11$ and $k_2 = 23$ to encrypt THE NATIONAL SECURITY AGENCY. The inverse of 11 modulo 26 is 19, so you can check your answer by decrypting with $19(c - 23)$.
</Callout>

## In Python

NumPy applies the formula to a whole message at once, and `pow` supplies the inverse:

```python
import numpy as np

def to_nums(text):
    return np.frombuffer(text.encode(), dtype=np.uint8).astype(int) - 65

def to_text(nums):
    return "".join(chr(n + 65) for n in nums)

print(to_text((7 * to_nums("SECURITY") + 21) % 26))  # RXJFKZYH
print(to_text(pow(7, -1, 26) * (to_nums("VLXIJH") - 21) % 26))  # AGENCY
print(len(set((13 * np.arange(26) + 5) % 26)))  # 2
```

The last line shows the bad key: 26 letters collapse onto 2 outputs.

## In machine learning

The formula $k_1 m + k_2$ has the same shape as a neural network layer, $wx + b$. That's where the word "affine" comes from in both fields: a linear map followed by a shift. In ML, it's why `b` is called the bias, and why a layer without it is linear but not affine.

The collapse you saw with a bad $k_1$ is also the same idea as a singular matrix from the determinants lesson. Both lose information, so neither can be undone. Most network layers are happy to discard information like this. Normalizing flows can't, because they must be able to run backwards, so they're built from layers that are guaranteed to be invertible.
