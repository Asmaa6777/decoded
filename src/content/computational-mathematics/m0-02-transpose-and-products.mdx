# Transpose and Vector Products

Flipping a matrix over its diagonal looks like a small change. But it lets you multiply two vectors in both possible orders, and the two orders give very different answers.

## The transpose

The **transpose** of an $m$-by-$n$ matrix $A$ is the $n$-by-$m$ matrix $A'$ whose rows are the columns of $A$:

$$
(A')_{ij} = a_{ji}
$$

Books usually write it $A^T$. MATLAB writes $A'$, and this course uses both. Notice the size flips too: a 2-by-3 matrix becomes 3-by-2. Row 1 of $A$ becomes column 1 of $A^T$, row 2 becomes column 2, and so on:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 & 3 \\ 0 & 5 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \\
A^T &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ -2 & 5 \\ 3 & -2 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

A square matrix stays square, and a row vector becomes a column vector:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B &= \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 & -4 \\ 3 & -1 & 2 \\ 0 & 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \\
B^T &= \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 3 & 0 \\ 2 & -1 & 4 \\ -4 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
D = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\quad
D^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Press the button and watch where each number goes. Entries on the main diagonal have $i = j$, so they stay where they are. Everything else swaps with its mirror image:

MATLAB writes the transpose with an apostrophe:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
B = magic(3);
B'

ans =

     1     5     9

```

```python
import numpy as np

B = np.array([[8, 1, 6], [3, 5, 7], [4, 9, 2]])
print(B.T.tolist())  # [[8, 3, 4], [1, 5, 9], [6, 7, 2]]

# Plain Python: zip(*rows) pairs up the columns
A = [[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]]
print([list(col) for col in zip(*A)])  # [[1, 4], [2, 5], [3, 6]]
```

</CodeTabs>

A matrix is **symmetric** when $A' = A$. `pascal(3)` is symmetric and `magic(3)` isn't. Transposing also turns a [row vector](/courses/computational-mathematics/matrices-and-matlab#writing-a-matrix-down) into a [column vector](/courses/computational-mathematics/matrices-and-matlab#writing-a-matrix-down), which you'll need next.

<Callout tone="sky" label="For complex numbers">
In MATLAB, `'` also takes the complex conjugate of each entry: it flips the sign of the imaginary part. Use `.'` when you want the transpose alone. For real matrices the two are identical. NumPy's `.T` never conjugates; write `.conj().T` when you want both.
</Callout>

## The dot product

For two vectors of the same length, the **dot product** (or inner product) multiplies matching entries and adds the results:

$$
a \cdot b = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \cdots + a_n b_n
$$

With the sum sign this is $a \cdot b = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i$, read "add up $a_i b_i$ for $i = 1$ to $n$". Books often write "$a, b \in \mathbb{R}^n$" for this, meaning both are lists of $n$ real numbers. For example, with $u = (1, -2, 3, 4)$ and $v = (2, 3, -2, 1)$:

$$
\begin{aligned} u \cdot v &= (1)(2) + (-2)(3) \\ &\quad + (3)(-2) + (4)(1) \\ &= 2 - 6 - 6 + 4 = -6 \end{aligned}
$$

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
u = [1 -2 3 4];
v = [2 3 -2 1];
dot(u, v)

ans =
    -6
```

```python
import numpy as np

u = np.array([1, -2, 3, 4])
v = np.array([2, 3, -2, 1])
print(u @ v)  # -6

# Without NumPy, it's the definition: multiply pairs, then add
print(sum(a * b for a, b in zip([1, -2, 3, 4], [2, 3, -2, 1])))  # -6
```

</CodeTabs>

The sign of the answer has a meaning. To see it, draw each vector as an arrow from the origin $(0, 0)$ to the [coordinates](/courses/computer-graphics/pixel-coordinates) $(3, 1)$ it names: an arrow pointing right and a little up. A second formula for the same number then shows why the sign matters. If $\theta$ is the angle between the two arrows and $\|a\|$, $\|b\|$ are their lengths, then

$$
a \cdot b = \|a\|\,\|b\|\cos\theta
$$

Lengths are never negative, so the sign comes from $\cos\theta$ alone. A large positive dot product means the two vectors point in roughly the same direction ($\theta$ near 0, cosine near 1). Zero means they're at right angles ($\cos 90° = 0$). Negative means they point apart ($\theta$ over 90°). For example, $(1, 0) \cdot (0, 1) = 0$: one points right, the other up.

Turn and stretch the two arrows. Both ways of computing the dot product always agree, and the sign changes exactly when the angle crosses 90°:

<DotProductAngle />

Divide by both lengths, which are [Euclidean norms](/courses/computational-mathematics/norms-and-trace#three-norms-to-know) ($\|a\| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + \cdots + a_n^2}$), and only the cosine is left. That's the **cosine similarity**, a score from $-1$ to $1$ that ignores length and keeps only direction:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{a \cdot b}{\|a\|\,\|b\|}
$$

This is how AI systems decide that two things are alike. A language model turns each word, sentence or image into a long vector called an **embedding**. A search engine or chatbot then finds related documents by ranking their cosine similarity to your question. Two vectors pointing the same way score 1, however long they are:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
a = [1 2 0];
b = [2 4 0];
dot(a, b) / (norm(a) * norm(b))

ans =
     1
```

```python
import numpy as np

a = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 0.0])
b = np.array([2.0, 4.0, 0.0])

print(round(a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)), 4))  # 1.0
```

</CodeTabs>

Transformers, the networks behind chatbots, use the same idea inside **attention**. Each word gives every other word a relevance score. The score is a dot product between two vectors the model has learned, called the query and the key.

## One pair of vectors, two products

MATLAB also multiplies vectors with `*`, as matrices. The full rule is in [the next lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/multiplying-matrices#the-rule), but for vectors it's short. Write the two sizes side by side, like (1-by-3)(3-by-1). The two inside numbers must be equal, and the two outside numbers give the size of the answer.

Take a column vector $u$ and a row vector $v$ of the same length. Both orders pass that check, because the inside numbers match either way. But the results are completely different:

- `v * u` is 1-by-3 times 3-by-1, so the result is 1-by-1. This is the **inner product**.
- `u * v` is 3-by-1 times 1-by-3, so the result is 3-by-3. This is the **outer product**. Entry $(i, j)$ is simply $u_i v_j$, so each row is a copy of $v$ scaled by one entry of $u$. Nothing gets added up.

Switch between the two and compare:

<VectorProductVisualizer />

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
u = [3; 1; 4];
v = [2 0 -1];

v * u          % inner product, 1-by-1

ans =
     2

u * v          % outer product, 3-by-3

ans =
     6     0    -3
     2     0    -1
     8     0    -4
```

```python
import numpy as np

u = np.array([3, 1, 4])
v = np.array([2, 0, -1])

# NumPy's 1-D arrays have no row or column orientation, so there's no
# transpose to get right: @ gives the inner product, np.outer the outer one.
print(u @ v)  # 2
print(np.outer(u, v).tolist())  # [[6, 0, -3], [2, 0, -1], [8, 0, -4]]
```

</CodeTabs>

If `x` and `y` are both column vectors, then `x * y` is an error because the inner sizes are n and 1. Transpose one of them and it works: `x' * y` is the inner product and `x * y'` is the outer product.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common mistake">
Putting the transpose on the wrong vector. `x' * y` and `x * y'` look almost the same, but one gives a single number and the other a full $n$-by-$n$ matrix. Before running either, write the sizes down: (1-by-n)(n-by-1) is 1-by-1, and (n-by-1)(1-by-n) is n-by-n. The outer sizes are the answer's size.
</Callout>

Recommendation systems use both. A classic film recommender learns a short vector for every user and every film. Each entry measures one hidden taste, such as "likes action". A predicted rating is the **dot product** of a user's vector and a film's vector, so it's high when the user's tastes match what the film offers. The **outer product** builds a whole table at once: a column of every user's action score times a row of every film's action score gives the "action" part of every prediction.

The transpose is useful in machine learning too. If $X$ holds one example per row, then $X'X$ takes the dot product of every feature column with every other. Suppose the data is centred: each column's [mean](/courses/discrete-mathematics/probability-and-expectation#random-variables-and-expectation) has already been subtracted, so every feature averages 0. Then $X'X$ divided by $n$ (or by $n - 1$, depending on the convention) is the **covariance matrix**. It records which features go up and down together, and you'll meet it again in [the trace](/courses/computational-mathematics/norms-and-trace#the-trace).

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
X = [1 2; 3 4; 5 6];
X' * X

ans =
    35    44
    44    56
```

```python
import numpy as np

X = np.array([[1, 2], [3, 4], [5, 6]])
print((X.T @ X).tolist())  # [[35, 44], [44, 56]]
```

</CodeTabs>
