# Transpose and Vector Products

Flipping a matrix over its diagonal looks like a small trick, but it's what lets you multiply two vectors in both possible orders, and those two orders give very different answers.

## The transpose

The **transpose** of an $m$-by-$n$ matrix $A$ is the $n$-by-$m$ matrix $A'$ whose rows are the columns of $A$:

$$
(A')_{ij} = a_{ji}
$$

Books usually write it $A^T$. MATLAB writes $A'$, and this course uses both. Notice the size flips too: a 2-by-3 matrix becomes 3-by-2. Row 1 of $A$ becomes column 1 of $A^T$, row 2 becomes column 2, and so on:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 & 3 \\ 0 & 5 & -2 \end{bmatrix}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ -2 & 5 \\ 3 & -2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

A square matrix stays square, and a row vector becomes a column vector:

$$
B = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 & -4 \\ 3 & -1 & 2 \\ 0 & 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
\ \Rightarrow\ 
B^T = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 3 & 0 \\ 2 & -1 & 4 \\ -4 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
D = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\ \Rightarrow\ 
D^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Press the button and watch where each number goes. Entries on the main diagonal have $i = j$, so they stay where they are. Everything else swaps with its mirror image:

MATLAB writes the transpose with an apostrophe:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
B = magic(3);
B'

ans =
     8     3     4
     1     5     9
     6     7     2
```

```python
import numpy as np

B = np.array([[8, 1, 6], [3, 5, 7], [4, 9, 2]])
print(B.T.tolist())  # [[8, 3, 4], [1, 5, 9], [6, 7, 2]]

# Plain Python: zip(*rows) pairs up the columns
A = [[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]]
print([list(col) for col in zip(*A)])  # [[1, 4], [2, 5], [3, 6]]
```

</CodeTabs>

A matrix is **symmetric** when $A' = A$. `pascal(3)` is symmetric and `magic(3)` isn't. Transposing also turns a [row vector](/courses/computational-mathematics/matrices-and-matlab#writing-a-matrix-down) into a [column vector](/courses/computational-mathematics/matrices-and-matlab#writing-a-matrix-down), which is the move you need next.

<Callout tone="sky" label="For complex numbers">
In MATLAB, `'` also conjugates complex entries. Use `.'` when you want the transpose alone. For real matrices the two are identical. NumPy's `.T` never conjugates; write `.conj().T` when you want both.
</Callout>

## The dot product

For two vectors of the same length, the **dot product** (or inner product) multiplies matching entries and adds the results:

$$
a \cdot b = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \cdots + a_n b_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i
$$

Books often write "$a, b \in \mathbb{R}^n$" for this, meaning both are lists of $n$ real numbers. For example, with $u = (1, -2, 3, 4)$ and $v = (2, 3, -2, 1)$:

$$
u \cdot v = (1)(2) + (-2)(3) + (3)(-2) + (4)(1) = -6
$$

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
u = [1 -2 3 4];
v = [2 3 -2 1];
dot(u, v)

ans =
    -6
```

```python
import numpy as np

u = np.array([1, -2, 3, 4])
v = np.array([2, 3, -2, 1])
print(u @ v)  # -6

# Without NumPy, it's the definition: multiply pairs, then add
print(sum(a * b for a, b in zip([1, -2, 3, 4], [2, 3, -2, 1])))  # -6
```

</CodeTabs>

The sign tells you something. A large positive dot product means the two vectors point roughly the same way, zero means they're at right angles, and negative means they point apart. Divide by both lengths, which are [Euclidean norms](/courses/computational-mathematics/norms-and-trace#three-norms-to-know), and you get the **cosine similarity**, a score from $-1$ to $1$ that ignores length and keeps only direction.

This is how AI systems decide that two things are alike. A language model turns each word, sentence or image into a long vector called an **embedding**, and a search engine or chatbot finds related documents by ranking their cosine similarity to your question. Two vectors pointing the same way score 1, however long they are:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
a = [1 2 0];
b = [2 4 0];
dot(a, b) / (norm(a) * norm(b))

ans =
     1
```

```python
import numpy as np

a = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 0.0])
b = np.array([2.0, 4.0, 0.0])

print(round(a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)), 4))  # 1.0
```

</CodeTabs>

Transformers, the networks behind chatbots, use the same idea inside **attention**: each word scores how relevant every other word is with a dot product between two learned vectors, called the query and the key.

## One pair of vectors, two products

Take a column vector $u$ and a row vector $v$ of the same length. Both orders are legal [matrix products](/courses/computational-mathematics/multiplying-matrices#the-rule), because the inner sizes match either way, yet the results have nothing in common:

- `v * u` is 1-by-3 times 3-by-1, so the result is 1-by-1. This is the **inner product**.
- `u * v` is 3-by-1 times 1-by-3, so the result is 3-by-3. This is the **outer product**.

Switch between the two and compare:

<VectorProductVisualizer />

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
u = [3; 1; 4];
v = [2 0 -1];

v * u          % inner product, 1-by-1

ans =
     2

u * v          % outer product, 3-by-3

ans =
     6     0    -3
     2     0    -1
     8     0    -4
```

```python
import numpy as np

u = np.array([3, 1, 4])
v = np.array([2, 0, -1])

# NumPy's 1-D arrays have no row or column orientation, so there's no
# transpose to get right: @ gives the inner product, np.outer the outer one.
print(u @ v)  # 2
print(np.outer(u, v).tolist())  # [[6, 0, -3], [2, 0, -1], [8, 0, -4]]
```

</CodeTabs>

If `x` and `y` are both column vectors, then `x * y` is an error because the inner sizes are n and 1. Transpose one of them and it works: `x' * y` is the inner product and `x * y'` is the outer product.

Recommendation systems use both. A classic film recommender learns a short vector for every user and every film, and predicts a rating as the **dot product** of the two: high when the user's tastes line up with what the film offers. The **outer product** of the same vectors fills in a whole table of predictions at once.

The transpose earns its place in machine learning too. If $X$ holds one example per row, then $X'X$ takes the dot product of every feature column with every other. For centred data, it's the **covariance matrix** up to a scaling factor, which records which features rise and fall together, and it's where PCA starts when it compresses data to its most important directions.

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
X = [1 2; 3 4; 5 6];
X' * X

ans =
    35    44
    44    56
```

```python
import numpy as np

X = np.array([[1, 2], [3, 4], [5, 6]])
print((X.T @ X).tolist())  # [[35, 44], [44, 56]]
```

</CodeTabs>
