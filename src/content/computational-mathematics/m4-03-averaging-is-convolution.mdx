# Project: Averaging Is Convolution

"Convolution" sounds like a big idea, but it's three small ones stacked on top of each other. Take them one at a time.

## Step 1: multiply and add

You already do this when you work out a course grade. Say the exam counts 50%, the project 30% and quizzes 20%, and you scored 80, 90 and 70:

$$
\begin{aligned}
0.5 \cdot 80 &+ 0.3 \cdot 90 + 0.2 \cdot 70 \\
&= 40 + 27 + 14 = 81
\end{aligned}
$$

Each number gets a **weight** saying how much it counts. Multiply each number by its weight, then add everything up. This is a **weighted sum**, and it is the [dot product](/courses/computational-mathematics/transpose-and-products#the-dot-product) of the scores with the weights. A [linear model](/courses/computational-mathematics/multiplying-matrices#the-rule)'s prediction, $w \cdot x$, is the same calculation. Notice that the weights add up to $0.5 + 0.3 + 0.2 = 1$. When the weights sum to $1$, the weighted sum keeps the scale of the inputs. Scoring 80 on every part gives $0.5 \cdot 80 + 0.3 \cdot 80 + 0.2 \cdot 80 = 80$.

The list of weights, here $[0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2]$, is what convolution calls the **kernel**.

## Step 2: an average is a weighted sum

The average of 3, 1 and 3 is $(3 + 1 + 3)/3 = 7/3 \approx 2.33$. Dividing by 3 at the end is the same as multiplying each number by $\tfrac13$ before adding:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{3 + 1 + 3}{3} &= \tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 \\
&= 1 + \tfrac13 + 1 = \frac{7}{3} \approx 2.33
\end{aligned}
$$

Averaging 3 numbers is a weighted sum with the kernel $[\tfrac13,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac13]$. Every number gets the same weight, and the three weights add up to $\tfrac13 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac13 = 1$.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common mistake">
Forgetting to divide the kernel by $M$, so the weights do not add up to 1. Convolving with $[1,\ 1,\ 1]$ adds three neighbours without dividing by 3, which triples the volume ($\text{sum} = 3$). A sum of weights greater than 1 amplifies the signal; a sum less than 1 attenuates it. To keep the volume of a steady signal unchanged ($1.0 \times$), the weights in `h` must add up to 1, as in `ones(1, M) / M` or $[\tfrac14,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14]$.
</Callout>

## Step 3: a window that slides

A signal is a long list of numbers, while the kernel here has only 3 weights. Look at 3 numbers at a time through a **window** centered on the current sample $n$, reaching one sample to the left ($n-1$) and one to the right ($n+1$). Take the weighted sum of what is inside, write down the answer, then move the window one step to the right and repeat.

Each window position gives one output number. The full list of outputs is the **convolution** of the signal with the kernel. At the first and last positions, the window extends past the ends of the signal. Because a kernel of odd length $M$ reaches $(M-1)/2$ samples to each side (here $(3-1)/2 = 1$ sample), we pad the signal with one zero at each end (the dashed cells) so every window holds 3 numbers.

For the noisy signal $[3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3]$, padding with a zero on the left and right gives $[0\ 3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3\ 0]$. Sliding the 3-weight average kernel $[\tfrac13,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac13]$ across all five window positions produces:

1. **Position 1** (window $[0,\ 3,\ 1]$): $\tfrac13 \cdot 0 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1 = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.33$.
2. **Position 2** (window $[3,\ 1,\ 3]$): $\tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 = \frac{7}{3} \approx 2.33$.
3. **Position 3** (window $[1,\ 3,\ 1]$): $\tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1 = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67$.
4. **Position 4** (window $[3,\ 1,\ 3]$): $\tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 = \frac{7}{3} \approx 2.33$.
5. **Position 5** (window $[1,\ 3,\ 0]$): $\tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 0 = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.33$.

Step through it on the recording from the [last lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/noise-and-averaging#a-five-sample-example), then try the other kernels and the other signal:

<ConvolutionStepper />

**Convolution slides a window along the signal and takes a weighted sum at every stop using the kernel's weights.** The kernel decides what the convolution does:

- **Average $[\tfrac13,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac13]$** gives all three neighbours equal weight, smoothing the middle of $[3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3]$ to $[2.33\ 1.67\ 2.33]$.
- **Centre-heavy $[\tfrac14,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14]$** gives half the weight to the middle sample and a quarter to each neighbour, still adding to $1$. At position 2, the window $[3,\ 1,\ 3]$ gives $\tfrac14 \cdot 3 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot 3 = 2$. Across all five positions, the output is $[1.75\ 2\ 2\ 2\ 1.75]$.
- **Copy $[0,\ 1,\ 0]$** gives weight 1 to the middle sample and 0 to its neighbours. Every window returns its middle number, leaving $[3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3]$ unchanged.

## What just happened

**Noisy constant.** The clean value under $[3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3]$ is 2. Before averaging, each middle number was 1 away from 2. After averaging with $[\tfrac13,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac13]$, the middle outputs are 2.33, 1.67 and 2.33, only 0.33 away from 2. The noise in the middle shrank to a third of its size. The two end outputs are 1.33, which is $2 - 1.33 = 0.67$ away from 2. They improved less because the end windows include a padded zero outside the signal, which pulls the average toward 0. Signal processing calls this distortion at the boundaries an **edge effect**.

**Sharp spike.** Now switch the stepper to $[0\ 0\ 6\ 0\ 0]$. Padded with zeros, it is $[0\ 0\ 0\ 6\ 0\ 0\ 0]$. At position 1, the window $[0,\ 0,\ 0]$ averages to $0$. At positions 2, 3 and 4, the windows are $[0,\ 0,\ 6]$, $[0,\ 6,\ 0]$ and $[6,\ 0,\ 0]$. Each window holds one 6 and two zeros, so each averages to $\tfrac13 \cdot 6 = 2$. At position 5, the window $[0,\ 0,\ 0]$ gives $0$ again. The single spike of height 6 has spread across three samples of height 2: $[0\ 2\ 2\ 2\ 0]$. That spike was part of the clean signal, yet averaging flattened it.

<Callout tone="orange" label="The catch">
Averaging cannot tell noise from a sudden change in the signal. It smooths both: in signal processing, a filter that lets slow changes pass and blocks fast ones is called a **low-pass filter**. In speech, the fast changes are consonants like "s", "t" and "k", so too much averaging makes a voice sound muffled. The [last lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/measuring-the-trade-off) measures this trade-off.
</Callout>

## The formula

Write $y(n)$ for the noisy signal going into the filter, and $\hat{x}(n)$ (read "x-hat") for the smoothed estimate coming out. For the 3-number average, $\hat{x}(n)$ averages $y$ at $n$ and its two neighbours, indexed $k = -1, 0, 1$:

$$
\hat{x}(n) = \tfrac13 y(n-1) + \tfrac13 y(n) + \tfrac13 y(n+1)
$$

At $n = 2$ on $y = [3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3]$, this gives $\hat{x}(2) = \tfrac13(3 + 1 + 3) \approx 2.33$, matching position 2 of the stepper.

Textbooks write one formula that works for any kernel $h$:

$$
\hat{x}(n) = \sum_k h(k)\, y(n - k)
$$

Here $\sum_k$ means "add up over every index $k$ in the kernel". Multiply each weight $h(k)$ by the signal value $y(n-k)$ it lines up with, then add the products. Why $n - k$ with a minus sign? It comes from [multiplying polynomials](/courses/computational-mathematics/multiplying-and-dividing#convolution), where the coefficient at position $n$ collects every pair of indices $k$ and $n - k$ that add up to $n$. As $k$ moves left to right across the kernel, $n - k$ moves right to left across the signal. That minus sign means the kernel is **flipped** (read backward) before sliding. Our kernels ($[\tfrac13,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac13]$, $[\tfrac14,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14]$ and $[0,\ 1,\ 0]$) are symmetric, so flipping them changes nothing.

An uneven kernel shows the flip clearly. Take $h = [1,\ 2,\ 3]$ with weights indexed $k = -1, 0, 1$, so $h(-1) = 1$, $h(0) = 2$ and $h(1) = 3$. In the formula, $h(1)$ multiplies $y(n - 1)$ and $h(-1)$ multiplies $y(n + 1)$. Viewed left to right across the window $[y(n-1), y(n), y(n+1)]$, the weights applied are $[h(1), h(0), h(-1)] = [3, 2, 1]$—the kernel reversed. On the spike $[0\ 0\ 6\ 0\ 0]$ where the 6 sits at index 3, at $n = 2$ the window $[0, 0, 6]$ meets weights $[3, 2, 1]$, giving $3(0) + 2(0) + 1(6) = 6$. At $n = 3$, $[0, 6, 0]$ gives $2(6) = 12$. At $n = 4$, $[6, 0, 0]$ gives $3(6) = 18$. The output is $[0\ 6\ 12\ 18\ 0]$. On polynomial coefficients, `conv([1 2], [1 3])` returns `[1 5 6]` because $(s + 2)(s + 3) = s^2 + 5s + 6$. Smoothing a recording and multiplying polynomials use the same operation.

## In code

MATLAB's `conv(y, h)` computes the **full** convolution, which includes every position where the kernel touches even one sample of `y`. For a signal of length $N$ and a kernel of length $M$, the full convolution has length $N + M - 1$ (here $5 + 3 - 1 = 7$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{conv}([3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3],\ [\tfrac13\ \tfrac13\ \tfrac13]) \\
&\quad = [1,\ 1.3333,\ 2.3333,\ 1.6667,\ 2.3333,\ 1.3333,\ 1]
\end{aligned}
$$

The extra $1$ at each end is where the kernel touches only the first or last $3$ ($\tfrac13 \cdot 3 = 1$). Passing `'same'` drops those outer ends and keeps the middle $N$ numbers. The output then has the **same length** as `y`, matching the 5 positions of the stepper:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
h = ones(1, 3) / 3;              % the kernel [1/3 1/3 1/3]
conv([3 1 3 1 3], h, 'same')

ans =
    1.3333    2.3333    1.6667    2.3333    1.3333

conv([1 2], [1 3])               % full convolution = polynomial multiplication

ans =
     1     5     6

% on the whole recording, with an M-sample average:
% x_hat = conv(y, ones(1, M) / M, 'same');
```

```python
import numpy as np

def conv_same(x, h):
    """MATLAB's conv(x, h, 'same'): the centre of the full result."""
    start = len(h) // 2          # skips (M-1)//2 boundary samples to keep the middle N
    return np.convolve(x, h)[start:start + len(x)]

h = np.ones(3) / 3               # the kernel [1/3 1/3 1/3]
print(np.round(conv_same([3, 1, 3, 1, 3], h), 4).tolist())  # [1.3333, 2.3333, 1.6667, 2.3333, 1.3333]
print(np.convolve([1, 2], [1, 3]).tolist())  # [1, 5, 6]

# on the whole recording, with an M-sample average:
# x_hat = conv_same(y, np.ones(M) / M)
```

</CodeTabs>

<Callout tone="orange" label="NumPy's 'same' isn't MATLAB's">
Leaving out `'same'` returns $N + M - 1$ samples instead of $N$. Subtracting `x - x_hat` in the next lesson will then fail with a size error. Also, NumPy's `np.convolve(x, h, mode="same")` differs from MATLAB when the kernel length $M$ is even. On `x = [1, 2, 3, 4]` and `h = [1, 1]`, the full convolution is `[1, 3, 5, 7, 4]`. MATLAB's `'same'` takes `[3, 5, 7, 4]` (starting at index `len(h) // 2 = 1`), while NumPy's `mode="same"` takes `[1, 3, 5, 7]`. The helper `conv_same` above matches MATLAB for every kernel length.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="sky" label="The same operation, learned">
A [convolutional neural network](/courses/computational-mathematics/multiplying-and-dividing#convolution) does the same operation, except nobody chooses the kernel by hand. The network learns the weights from data. A 1D convolution layer in a speech model slides learned kernels along the audio samples just as `conv` does here. Its first layers often learn smoothing and edge-finding kernels like the ones in the stepper.
</Callout>
