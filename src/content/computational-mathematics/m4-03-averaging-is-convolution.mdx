# Project: Averaging Is Convolution

"Convolution" sounds like a big idea, but it's three small ones stacked on top of each other. Take them one at a time.

## Step 1: multiply and add

You already do this when you work out a course grade. Say the exam counts 50%, the project 30% and quizzes 20%, and you scored 80, 90 and 70:

$$
0.5 \cdot 80 + 0.3 \cdot 90 + 0.2 \cdot 70 = 40 + 27 + 14 = 81
$$

Each number gets a **weight** saying how much it counts. Multiply each number by its weight, then add everything up. This is a **weighted sum**, and it's the [dot product](/courses/computational-mathematics/transpose-and-products#the-dot-product) of the scores with the weights. A [linear model](/courses/computational-mathematics/multiplying-matrices#the-rule)'s prediction, $w \cdot x$, is exactly this. Notice that the weights $0.5 + 0.3 + 0.2 = 1$: when the weights add up to $1$, a weighted sum keeps the overall scale of the numbers (if you scored 80 on everything, your grade is $0.5 \cdot 80 + 0.3 \cdot 80 + 0.2 \cdot 80 = 80$).

The list of weights, here $[0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2]$, is what convolution calls the **kernel**.

## Step 2: an average is a weighted sum

The average of 3, 1 and 3 is $(3 + 1 + 3)/3 = 7/3 \approx 2.33$. Dividing by 3 at the end is the same as multiplying each number by $\tfrac13$ before adding:

$$
\frac{3 + 1 + 3}{3} = \tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 = 1 + \tfrac13 + 1 = \frac{7}{3} \approx 2.33
$$

So "take the average of 3 numbers" is a weighted sum with the kernel $[\tfrac13,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac13]$, where every number counts equally and the three weights add up to $\tfrac13 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac13 = 1$.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common mistake">
Forgetting to divide the kernel by $M$, so the weights don't add up to 1. Convolving with $[1,\ 1,\ 1]$ adds three neighbours without dividing by 3, which makes the signal 3 times louder ($\text{sum} = 3$). For a smoothing filter that preserves the volume of a steady signal, the weights in `h` must sum to 1, such as `ones(1, M) / M` or $[\tfrac14,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14]$.
</Callout>

## Step 3: a window that slides

A signal is a long list of numbers, but the kernel has only 3 weights. So look at 3 numbers at a time, through a **window**. Take the weighted sum of what's inside, write down the answer, move the window one step to the right and do it again, all the way along.

Each window position gives one output number. The full list of outputs is the **convolution** of the signal with the kernel. At the two ends, the window hangs off the edge of the signal, so we pad the signal with a zero at each end (the dashed cells) so the window always has 3 numbers to multiply.

For the noisy signal $[3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3]$, padding with a zero on the left and right gives $[0\ 3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3\ 0]$. Sliding the 3-weight average kernel $[\tfrac13,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac13]$ across all five window positions produces:

1. **Position 1** (window $[0,\ 3,\ 1]$): $\tfrac13 \cdot 0 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1 = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.33$.
2. **Position 2** (window $[3,\ 1,\ 3]$): $\tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 = \frac{7}{3} \approx 2.33$.
3. **Position 3** (window $[1,\ 3,\ 1]$): $\tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1 = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67$.
4. **Position 4** (window $[3,\ 1,\ 3]$): $\tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 = \frac{7}{3} \approx 2.33$.
5. **Position 5** (window $[1,\ 3,\ 0]$): $\tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 0 = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.33$.

Step through it on the recording from the [last lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/noise-and-averaging#a-five-sample-example), then try the other kernels and the other signal:

<ConvolutionStepper />

That's all of it: **convolution slides a window along the signal and, at every stop, takes a weighted sum using the kernel's weights**. The kernel decides what the convolution does:

- **Average $[\tfrac13,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac13]$** gives all three neighbours equal weight, smoothing the middle of $[3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3]$ to $[2.33\ 1.67\ 2.33]$.
- **Centre-heavy $[\tfrac14,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14]$** gives half the weight to the middle sample and a quarter to each neighbour (still adding up to $\tfrac14 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 = 1$). At position 2 its window is $[3,\ 1,\ 3]$, giving $\tfrac14 \cdot 3 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot 3 = 0.75 + 0.5 + 0.75 = 2$, and across all five positions it gives $[1.75\ 2\ 2\ 2\ 1.75]$.
- **Copy $[0,\ 1,\ 0]$** gives weight 1 to the middle sample and 0 to its neighbours, so every window returns its middle number and $[3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3]$ comes back unchanged.

## What just happened

**Noisy constant.** The truth hiding under $[3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3]$ is 2. Before averaging, each middle number was 1 away from 2. After averaging with $[\tfrac13,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac13]$, the middle outputs are 2.33, 1.67 and 2.33, only 0.33 away: the noise there got three times smaller, which is what we want. The two end outputs, 1.33, improved less (from 1 away to $2 - 1.33 = 0.67$ away), because at the ends the window includes a padded zero outside the signal, pulling the average toward 0. Signal processing calls this distortion at the boundaries an **edge effect**.

**Sharp spike.** Now switch the stepper to $[0\ 0\ 6\ 0\ 0]$. Padded with zeros, it is $[0\ 0\ 0\ 6\ 0\ 0\ 0]$. At position 1 the window $[0,\ 0,\ 0]$ averages to $0$. At positions 2, 3 and 4 the windows are $[0,\ 0,\ 6]$, $[0,\ 6,\ 0]$ and $[6,\ 0,\ 0]$: each one contains the 6 once and two zeros, so each averages to $\tfrac13 \cdot 6 = 2$. At position 5 the window $[0,\ 0,\ 0]$ gives $0$ again. The single spike of height 6 has been spread across three samples of height 2: $[0\ 2\ 2\ 2\ 0]$. That spike was real, not noise, but averaging flattened it anyway, which is what we *don't* want.

<Callout tone="orange" label="The catch">
Averaging can't tell noise from a real, sudden change. It smooths both: in signal processing, a filter that lets slow changes pass and blocks fast ones is called a **low-pass filter**. In speech, the fast changes are consonants like "s", "t" and "k", so too much averaging makes a voice sound muffled. The [last lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/measuring-the-trade-off) measures this trade-off.
</Callout>

## The formula

To distinguish the input of the filter from its output, write $y(n)$ for the noisy signal going in and $\hat{x}(n)$ (read "x-hat", our estimate of the clean signal) for the smoothed signal coming out. For the 3-number average, the output at position $n$ is the average of $y$ at $n$ and its two neighbours, indexed $k = -1, 0, 1$:

$$
\hat{x}(n) = \tfrac13 y(n-1) + \tfrac13 y(n) + \tfrac13 y(n+1)
$$

Check it at $n = 2$ on $y = [3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3]$: $\hat{x}(2) = \tfrac13 y(1) + \tfrac13 y(2) + \tfrac13 y(3) = \tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 \approx 2.33$, matching position 2 of the stepper.

Textbooks write one formula that works for any kernel $h$:

$$
\hat{x}(n) = \sum_k h(k)\, y(n - k)
$$

Here $\sum_k$ means "add up over every index $k$ in the kernel". For each weight $h(k)$, multiply it by the signal value it lines up with, $y(n-k)$, and add them all up. Why $n - k$ with a minus sign? It comes from [multiplying polynomials](/courses/computational-mathematics/multiplying-and-dividing#convolution), where the coefficient at position $n$ collects every pair of indices $k$ and $n - k$ that add up to $n$. As $k$ goes from left to right across the kernel, $n - k$ goes from right to left across the signal, which means the kernel is **flipped** (read backwards) before sliding. Our kernels ($[\tfrac13,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac13]$, $[\tfrac14,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14]$ and $[0,\ 1,\ 0]$) are symmetric, so flipping them changes nothing.

Try a lopsided kernel to see the flip: if $h = [1,\ 2,\ 3]$ (indexed $k = -1, 0, 1$) and the signal is the spike $[0\ 0\ 6\ 0\ 0]$, the non-zero terms happen when $n - k = 3$, so at $n = 2, 3, 4$ the outputs are $6 \cdot h(-1) = 6$, $6 \cdot h(0) = 12$ and $6 \cdot h(1) = 18$, giving $[0\ 6\ 12\ 18\ 0]$. And on polynomial coefficients, `conv([1 2], [1 3])` gives `[1 5 6]`, because $(s + 2)(s + 3) = s^2 + (1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 1)s + 2 \cdot 3 = s^2 + 5s + 6$. Smoothing a recording and multiplying polynomials are one operation.

## In code

MATLAB's `conv(y, h)` computes the **full** convolution, which includes every position where the kernel touches even one sample of `y`. For a signal of length $N$ and a kernel of length $M$, the full convolution has length $N + M - 1$ (here $5 + 3 - 1 = 7$):

$$
\text{conv}([3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3],\ [\tfrac13\ \tfrac13\ \tfrac13]) = [1,\ 1.3333,\ 2.3333,\ 1.6667,\ 2.3333,\ 1.3333,\ 1]
$$

The extra $1$ at each end is where the kernel touches only the first or last $3$ ($\tfrac13 \cdot 3 = 1$). Passing `'same'` drops those partial-touch tails and keeps the middle $N$ numbers, so the output is the **same length** as `y`, matching the 5 positions of the stepper:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
h = ones(1, 3) / 3;              % the kernel [1/3 1/3 1/3]
conv([3 1 3 1 3], h, 'same')

ans =
    1.3333    2.3333    1.6667    2.3333    1.3333

conv([1 2], [1 3])               % full convolution = polynomial multiplication

ans =
     1     5     6

% on the whole recording, with an M-sample average:
% x_hat = conv(y, ones(1, M) / M, 'same');
```

```python
import numpy as np

def conv_same(x, h):
    """MATLAB's conv(x, h, 'same'): the centre of the full result."""
    start = len(h) // 2
    return np.convolve(x, h)[start:start + len(x)]

h = np.ones(3) / 3               # the kernel [1/3 1/3 1/3]
print(np.round(conv_same([3, 1, 3, 1, 3], h), 4).tolist())  # [1.3333, 2.3333, 1.6667, 2.3333, 1.3333]
print(np.convolve([1, 2], [1, 3]).tolist())  # [1, 5, 6]

# on the whole recording, with an M-sample average:
# x_hat = conv_same(y, np.ones(M) / M)
```

</CodeTabs>

<Callout tone="orange" label="NumPy's 'same' isn't MATLAB's">
First, leaving out `'same'` returns $N + M - 1$ samples instead of $N$, so subtracting `x - x_hat` in the next lesson will fail with a size error or compare shifted samples. Second, `np.convolve(x, h, mode="same")` exists in NumPy, but for kernels of even length $M$ it starts one sample earlier than MATLAB: on `x = [1, 2, 3, 4]` and `h = [1, 1]`, the full convolution is `[1, 3, 5, 7, 4]`, from which MATLAB's `'same'` takes `[3, 5, 7, 4]` (starting at index `len(h) // 2 = 1`) while NumPy's `mode="same"` takes `[1, 3, 5, 7]`. The small `conv_same` above copies MATLAB's slice for every kernel length.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="sky" label="The same operation, learned">
A [convolutional neural network](/courses/computational-mathematics/multiplying-and-dividing#convolution) does exactly this, with one difference: nobody chooses the kernel by hand. The network learns the weights from data. A 1D convolution layer in a speech or audio model slides learned kernels along the samples just as `conv` does here, and the first layers often end up learning smoothing and edge-finding kernels much like the ones in the stepper.
</Callout>
