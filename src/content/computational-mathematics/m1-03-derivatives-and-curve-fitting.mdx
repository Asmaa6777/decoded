# Derivatives and Curve Fitting

Two more jobs that become a single function call: finding how fast a polynomial changes, and finding the polynomial that best describes messy data.

## Derivatives

The derivative of $ax^n$ is $nax^{n-1}$, and on a coefficient vector that just means multiplying by the powers and dropping the last entry. `polyder` does it for you:

```matlab
p = [1 0 -2 -5];      % x³ - 2x - 5
q = polyder(p)

q =
     3     0    -2     % 3x² - 2
```

Given two polynomials, it can also differentiate their product or quotient directly. Ask for one output and you get the derivative of the product. Ask for two and you get the numerator and denominator of the derivative of the quotient:

```matlab
a = [1 3 5];  b = [2 4 6];

c = polyder(a, b)        % 8  30  56  38

[q, d] = polyder(a, b)   % q = -2  -8  -2
                         % d =  4  16  40  48  36
```

## Fitting a curve to data

Real measurements rarely sit on a neat curve. `polyfit(x, y, n)` finds the degree $n$ polynomial with the smallest **sum of squared errors**, which is called a **least-squares** fit:

$$
\min_{p} \sum_{i} \bigl(p(x_i) - y_i\bigr)^2
$$

```matlab
x = [1 2 3 4 5];
y = [5.5 43.1 128 290.7 498.4];
p = polyfit(x, y, 3)

p =
   -0.1917   31.5821  -60.3262   35.3400
```

<Callout tone="orange" label="Lower error isn't always better">
Five points can always be matched exactly by a polynomial of degree 4. Zero error looks perfect, but it often means the curve has learned the noise in your data and will predict poorly between and beyond the points. This is called overfitting, and it's one of the main problems in machine learning.
</Callout>

## Where you'll meet this

<ApplicationsGrid>
<PracticalApplication title="Calibrating sensors" tool="Embedded systems">
Sensor readings are rarely perfectly linear, so engineers fit a low-degree polynomial to test measurements and apply it in firmware to correct the readings.
</PracticalApplication>
<PracticalApplication title="Training any model" tool="scikit-learn, PyTorch">
Minimising the sum of squared errors is the same goal as linear regression, and the idea of following a derivative downhill is how neural networks learn.
</PracticalApplication>
</ApplicationsGrid>
