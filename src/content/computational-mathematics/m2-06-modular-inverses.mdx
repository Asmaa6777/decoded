# Modular Inverses

Modular arithmetic has no fractions, so how do you divide? You multiply by an **inverse**: a number $x$ with $a \cdot x \equiv 1 \pmod n$. Every [affine cipher](/courses/computational-mathematics/affine-ciphers) later in this course depends on finding one, and so does every RSA key.

## When an inverse exists

If $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, the equation $a \cdot x \equiv b \pmod n$ has a solution, and it's unique modulo $n$. So $a$ has an inverse exactly when it's [coprime](/courses/computational-mathematics/divisibility-and-euclid#greatest-common-divisor) to $n$:

- **Existence:** By Bézout's identity, $\gcd(a, n) = 1$ implies integers $x$ and $y$ satisfy $ax + ny = 1$. Reducing modulo $n$ eliminates $ny$ ($ny \equiv 0$), leaving $a \cdot x \equiv 1 \pmod n$.
- **Uniqueness:** Suppose two integers satisfy $a x_1 \equiv a x_2 \equiv 1 \pmod n$. Then $a(x_1 - x_2) \equiv 0 \pmod n$, so $n \mid a(x_1 - x_2)$. Because $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, $n$ shares no factors with $a$. That forces $n \mid (x_1 - x_2)$, proving $x_1 \equiv x_2 \pmod n$.

For example, 2 has no inverse modulo 4, because $2x$ is always even and can't equal 1 modulo an even number.

When the modulus is a prime $p$, [Fermat's Little Theorem](/courses/computational-mathematics/primality-and-the-totient#fermats-little-theorem) gives a direct formula. Because $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$, multiplying by $a^{-1}$ gives $a^{-1} \equiv a^{p-2} \pmod p$. For composite moduli, the extended Euclidean algorithm provides the general solution.

## The extended Euclidean algorithm

The whole method rests on one fact: if $g = \gcd(a, b)$, there are integers $x$ and $y$ with

$$
a x + b y = g
$$

This relation is known as **Bézout's identity**, or the **Extended Euclidean Representation**.

To find $x$ and $y$, run [Euclid's algorithm](/courses/computational-mathematics/divisibility-and-euclid#the-euclidean-algorithm) down, and then climb back up through substitution. On each row, express the remainder in terms of $a$ and $b$:

| Step | Division | Rearranged | Back substitution | Simplified |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $130 = 2 \cdot 61 + 8$ | $8 = 130 - 2 \cdot 61$ | - | $8 = 1(130) - 2(61)$ |
| 2 | $61 = 7 \cdot 8 + 5$ | $5 = 61 - 7 \cdot 8$ | $61 - 7(130 - 2 \cdot 61)$ | $5 = -7(130) + 15(61)$ |
| 3 | $8 = 1 \cdot 5 + 3$ | $3 = 8 - 1 \cdot 5$ | $(130 - 2 \cdot 61) - 1(-7 \cdot 130 + 15 \cdot 61)$ | $3 = 8(130) - 17(61)$ |
| 4 | $5 = 1 \cdot 3 + 2$ | $2 = 5 - 1 \cdot 3$ | $(-7 \cdot 130 + 15 \cdot 61) - 1(8 \cdot 130 - 17 \cdot 61)$ | $2 = -15(130) + 32(61)$ |
| 5 | $3 = 1 \cdot 2 + 1$ | $1 = 3 - 1 \cdot 2$ | $(8 \cdot 130 - 17 \cdot 61) - 1(-15 \cdot 130 + 32 \cdot 61)$ | $1 = 23(130) - 49(61)$ |

Thus $x = 23$ and $y = -49$ solve $130x + 61y = 1$. Check: $130 \cdot 23 + 61 \cdot (-49) = 2990 - 2989 = 1$.

<ExtendedEuclidVisualizer />

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
[g, x, y] = gcd(130, 61)    % three outputs: g = 1, x = 23, y = -49
```

```python
def extended_gcd(a, b):
    """Returns g, x, y with a*x + b*y = g."""
    if b == 0:
        return a, 1, 0
    g, x, y = extended_gcd(b, a % b)  # run Euclid down...
    return g, y, x - (a // b) * y     # ...and climb back up

print(extended_gcd(130, 61))  # (1, 23, -49)
```

</CodeTabs>

## From there to the inverse

To solve $17x \equiv 1 \pmod{61}$, we run the same procedure for $\gcd(17, 61)$:

1. $61 = 3 \cdot 17 + 10 \implies 10 = 61 - 3 \cdot 17$
2. $17 = 1 \cdot 10 + 7 \implies 7 = 17 - 1 \cdot 10 = 17 - (61 - 3 \cdot 17) = -1(61) + 4(17)$
3. $10 = 1 \cdot 7 + 3 \implies 3 = 10 - 1 \cdot 7 = (61 - 3 \cdot 17) - (-1 \cdot 61 + 4 \cdot 17) = 2(61) - 7(17)$
4. $7 = 2 \cdot 3 + 1 \implies 1 = 7 - 2 \cdot 3 = (-1 \cdot 61 + 4 \cdot 17) - 2(2 \cdot 61 - 7 \cdot 17) = -5(61) + 18(17)$

This gives:

$$
17 \cdot 18 + 61 \cdot (-5) = 1
$$

Now look at this equation modulo 61. The second term is a multiple of 61, so it vanishes and leaves $17 \cdot 18 \equiv 1 \pmod{61}$. So the inverse of 17 is 18.

MATLAB returns the coefficients if you ask for three outputs from `gcd`:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
[g, x, y] = gcd(17, 61)    % g = 1, x = 18, y = -5
inverse = mod(x, 61)       % 18
```

```python
# Since Python 3.8, pow finds the inverse directly with an exponent of -1
print(pow(17, -1, 61))  # 18
```

</CodeTabs>

<Callout tone="sky" label="Negative answers are fine">
If $x$ comes out negative, take `mod(x, n)`. For example, if $x = -5$ with $n = 18$, the same inverse is $13$.
</Callout>

Division modulo a prime protects sensitive training workflows in machine learning. In **federated learning**, thousands of mobile devices train a shared model without transmitting raw user data. Each device sends only model parameter updates. **Secure aggregation** ensures the server learns only the sum of these updates, never an individual update. To tolerate devices dropping offline during training, these systems use **Shamir secret sharing**.

The secret is the intercept $f(0)$ of a line, such as $f(x) = 23 + 17x \pmod{97}$. Participants receive points along the line as shares. A single point reveals nothing because infinitely many lines pass through it. Any two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ determine the line. Setting $x = 0$ in the two-point line equation gives intercept $f(0) = \frac{y_1 x_2 - y_2 x_1}{x_2 - x_1}$. Computing that intercept requires dividing by $x_2 - x_1$, which modulo a prime means multiplying by its modular inverse:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
p = 97;
x1 = 1;  y1 = 40;     % two shares: points on f(x) = 23 + 17x mod 97
x2 = 2;  y2 = 57;

[~, d] = gcd(x2 - x1, p);                   % [~, d] captures Bézout x: the inverse mod p
secret = mod((y1*x2 - y2*x1) * d, p)

secret =
    23
```

```python
p = 97
(x1, y1), (x2, y2) = (1, 40), (2, 57)  # points on f(x) = 23 + 17x, modulo 97

secret = (y1 * x2 - y2 * x1) * pow(x2 - x1, -1, p) % p
print(secret)  # 23
```

</CodeTabs>

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common errors">
- **Seeking an inverse when $\gcd(a, n) \ne 1$:** An inverse exists *only* when $a$ and $n$ are coprime. For instance, $6$ has no inverse modulo $26$ because $\gcd(6, 26) = 2 \ne 1$. In Python, `pow(6, -1, 26)` raises a `ValueError: base is not invertible for the given modulus`.
- **Sign slips during back-substitution:** When substituting expressions into parentheses, take care with double negatives. For example, $17 - 1(61 - 3 \cdot 17) = 17 - 61 + 3 \cdot 17 = -1(61) + 4(17)$, not $+2(17)$.
- **Using floating-point division:** Writing `1 / a % n` computes float division first and destroys integer precision. Always use the extended Euclidean algorithm or `pow(a, -1, n)`.
</Callout>
