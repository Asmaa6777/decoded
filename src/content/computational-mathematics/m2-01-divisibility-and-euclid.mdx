# Divisibility and the Euclidean Algorithm

Every secure connection your browser opens rests on arithmetic with whole numbers. This module builds that arithmetic from the ground up, starting with what it means for one number to divide another.

## Divisibility

A nonzero integer $b$ **divides** $a$ if $a = m \cdot b$ for some integer $m$, meaning division leaves no remainder. We write $b \mid a$ and call $b$ a **divisor** (or factor) of $a$. The vertical bar $\mid$ expresses a relation ("$b$ divides $a$"), unlike a slash $a/b$ which computes a quotient. If $b$ does not divide $a$, we write $b \nmid a$. The positive divisors of 24 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 24.

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
n = 100;
find(mod(n, 1:n) == 0)    % every d from 1 to n that leaves no remainder
% divisors(100) gives the same, with the Symbolic Math Toolbox

ans =
     1     2     4     5    10    20    25    50   100
```

```python
n = 100
print([d for d in range(1, n + 1) if n % d == 0])  # [1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20, 25, 50, 100]
```

</CodeTabs>

Divisibility shapes practical architecture choices in AI. A **vision transformer**, a model used for image recognition, divides each image into square patches and processes them like words in a sentence. The patch size must divide the image width and height evenly. Otherwise, edge patches overflow the border. A 224-pixel image with 16-pixel patches works evenly because $16 \mid 224$, yielding $14 \times 14 = 196$ patches.

## The division algorithm

Divide any integer $a$ by a positive integer $n$ and you get one quotient and one remainder:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= q \cdot n + r, \qquad 0 \le r < n \\
q &= \left\lfloor \frac{a}{n} \right\rfloor
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\lfloor x \rfloor$ is the **floor function**, which returns the largest integer less than or equal to $x$. The rule $0 \le r < n$ ensures the remainder is never negative. That rule is especially important when dividing negative numbers:

- For $a = 11$ and $n = 7$: $11 / 7 \approx 1.57$, so $q = \lfloor 1.57 \rfloor = 1$. The remainder is $r = 11 - 1 \cdot 7 = 4$.
- For $a = -11$ and $n = 7$: $-11 / 7 \approx -1.57$. The floor function rounds down toward $-\infty$, so $q = \lfloor -1.57 \rfloor = -2$. The remainder is $r = -11 - (-2) \cdot 7 = 3$, which satisfies $0 \le r < 7$. Choosing $q = -1$ gives $r = -4$, which violates $r \ge 0$.

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
q = floor(-11 / 7)    % -2
r = mod(-11, 7)       % 3
```

```python
# divmod returns the quotient and the remainder together
print(divmod(-11, 7))  # (-2, 3)
```

</CodeTabs>

Every machine learning training loop uses this division. Models update their parameters on small **batches** of data. Splitting 1000 examples into batches of 32 gives 31 full batches and a leftover batch of 8. That small final batch introduces noisy gradient updates. Because of that variance, libraries like PyTorch offer a `drop_last` option to discard it.

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
full_batches = floor(1000 / 32)   % 31
leftover = mod(1000, 32)          % 8
```

```python
print(divmod(1000, 32))  # (31, 8)
```

</CodeTabs>

## Greatest common divisor

The **greatest common divisor** of $a$ and $b$, written $\gcd(a, b)$, is the largest positive integer that divides both numbers:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\gcd(a, b) = \max \{k \in \mathbb{Z}^+ : {} &k \mid a, \\
&k \mid b\}
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\mathbb{Z}^+$ denotes the set of **positive integers** $\{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$. More formally, a positive integer $c = \gcd(a, b)$ satisfies two conditions: $c$ divides both $a$ and $b$, and every common divisor of $a$ and $b$ also divides $c$. Because greatest common divisors are positive, signs do not change the result:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\gcd(a, b) &= \gcd(|a|, |b|) \\
&= \gcd(-a, b) \\
&= \gcd(a, -b) \\
&= \gcd(-a, -b)
\end{aligned}
$$

For example, $\gcd(60, 24) = \gcd(60, -24) = 12$. By convention, $\gcd(0, 0) = 0$.

Two numbers are **coprime** (or relatively prime) if their only common positive factor is 1, so $\gcd(a, b) = 1$. Neither number needs to be prime. The positive divisors of 8 are 1, 2, 4, 8, while those of 15 are 1, 3, 5, 15. Therefore $\gcd(8, 15) = 1$.

## The Euclidean algorithm

Listing all divisors is too slow for large numbers. Euclid's algorithm relies on one identity:

$$
\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)
$$

Here $a \bmod b$ is the remainder $r$ from the division algorithm. The [next lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/modular-arithmetic#the-mod-operation) explores modular arithmetic in detail. Why does this equality hold? Because $a = qb + r$, we have $r = a - qb$:
- Any common divisor $d$ of $a$ and $b$ divides the difference $r = a - qb$.
- Any common divisor $d$ of $b$ and $r$ divides the sum $a = qb + r$.

Both pairs $(a, b)$ and $(b, r)$ have the same common divisors, so their greatest common divisors are identical. With $r_1$ being the first remainder, each step divides the previous divisor by the previous remainder until the remainder reaches zero:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= q_1 b + r_1 \\
b &= q_2 r_1 + r_2 \\
r_1 &= q_3 r_2 + r_3 \\
&\;\;\vdots \\
r_{n-1} &= q_{n+1} r_n + 0
\end{aligned}
$$

When a remainder reaches zero, the last non-zero remainder is the gcd: $r_n = \gcd(a, b)$.

A geometric layout illustrates this process. Cut as many squares of side $b$ as possible from an $a$-by-$b$ rectangle. Repeat the cut on the leftover strip. The final square tiles the entire rectangle, and its side length equals the gcd:

Written out for $\gcd(1281, 243)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1281 &= 5 \cdot 243 + 66 \\
243 &= 3 \cdot 66 + 45 \\
66 &= 1 \cdot 45 + 21 \\
45 &= 2 \cdot 21 + 3 \\
21 &= 7 \cdot 3 + 0
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\gcd(1281, 243) = 3$.

The same reduction works for $\gcd(247, 143)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
247 &= 1 \cdot 143 + 104 \\
143 &= 1 \cdot 104 + 39 \\
104 &= 2 \cdot 39 + 26 \\
39 &= 1 \cdot 26 + 13 \\
26 &= 2 \cdot 13 + 0
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\gcd(247, 143) = 13$. Indeed, $247 = 13 \times 19$ and $143 = 13 \times 11$.

A simple `while` loop carries out the successive reductions:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
% The Euclidean algorithm in MATLAB
a = 247;  b = 143;
r = mod(a, b);                        % remainder: a - b * floor(a / b)
while r ~= 0
    a = b;
    b = r;
    r = mod(a, b);
end
gcd_result = b

gcd_result =
    13
```

```python
# The Euclidean algorithm in Python
a, b = 247, 143
while b:
    a, b = b, a % b
print(a)  # 13
```

</CodeTabs>

Testing the same reduction on $a = 18$ and $b = 50$: if the smaller number is given first, the first division $18 = 0 \cdot 50 + 18$ automatically swaps them to $(50, 18)$. From there:

$$
\begin{aligned}
50 &= 2 \cdot 18 + 14 \\
18 &= 1 \cdot 14 + 4 \\
14 &= 3 \cdot 4 + 2 \\
4 &= 2 \cdot 2 + 0
\end{aligned}
$$

The last non-zero remainder is 2, so $\gcd(18, 50) = 2$. Likewise, running on 24 and 78 gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
78 &= 3 \cdot 24 + 6 \\
24 &= 4 \cdot 6 + 0
\end{aligned}
$$

The last non-zero remainder is 6, so $\gcd(78, 24) = 6$.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common errors">
- **Confusing $b \mid a$ with division:** $3 \mid 12$ is a relational statement ("3 divides 12"). It does not evaluate to $3/12 = 0.25$ or $12/3 = 4$. Remember that the divisor comes first: $12 \mid 3$ is false.
- **Reporting 0 as the gcd:** The Euclidean algorithm terminates when a remainder reaches 0. The greatest common divisor is the *last non-zero remainder* (or the divisor in that final line). It is never 0 or the quotient.
</Callout>

MATLAB's built-in `gcd` computes this directly, and works elementwise on vectors:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
gcd(65, 70)                                % 5
gcd(247, 143)                              % 13
gcd(18, 50)                                % 2
gcd([250 1050 6022], [180 1150 5622])      % 10  50  2
```

```python
import math
import numpy as np

print(math.gcd(65, 70))  # 5
print(math.gcd(247, 143))  # 13
print(math.gcd(18, 50))  # 2
print(np.gcd([250, 1050, 6022], [180, 1150, 5622]).tolist())  # [10, 50, 2]
```

</CodeTabs>
