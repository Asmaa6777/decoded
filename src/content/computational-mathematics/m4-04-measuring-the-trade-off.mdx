# Project: Measuring the Trade-off

To measure how well a filter works, you must compare its output with the clean voice. In a real noisy recording, you do not have the clean voice. Instead, you build a test case where you know the answer: **record a clean voice $x$, add noise yourself with a fixed seed, and keep $x$ to compare against**. Every noise-removal method is tested this way.

Write $\hat{x}$ ("x-hat") for the estimate of the clean signal after filtering (the same hat notation used for [predictions in linear models](/courses/computational-mathematics/multiplying-matrices#the-rule)). Two measures tell how close $\hat{x}$ is to $x$.

## Error norm: how far off, as one number

$$
\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert = \sqrt{\sum_n \big(x(n) - \hat{x}(n)\big)^2}
$$

Subtract the estimate from the clean signal sample by sample, square each difference so negatives do not cancel positives, add them up and take the square root. That is the length of the error vector $x - \hat{x}$, the [Euclidean norm](/courses/computational-mathematics/norms-and-trace#three-norms-to-know) (`norm(v)` in MATLAB, `np.linalg.norm(v)` in NumPy). **Smaller is better.**

## Signal-to-noise ratio: how loud the voice is next to what's left of the noise

An error norm of $1.11$ does not tell you on its own whether the error is small or large. It is small if the voice has norm $100$, and huge if the voice has norm $1$. So we compare the energy of the clean voice, $\lVert x \rVert^2 = \sum_n x(n)^2$, with the energy of the leftover error, $\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2$. That ratio is the **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)**:

$$
\text{SNR} = 10 \log_{10} \frac{\lVert x \rVert^2}{\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2} \quad \text{in decibels (dB)}
$$

Here $\log_{10}(r)$ is the **base-10 logarithm** (an ordinary [logarithm](/courses/computational-mathematics/discrete-logarithms#logarithms-modulo-a-prime)), the power of $10$ that gives $r$. For example, $\log_{10}(1) = 0$ ($10^0 = 1$), $\log_{10}(10) = 1$ ($10^1 = 10$), and $\log_{10}(100) = 2$ ($10^2 = 100$). Multiplying by $10$ puts the ratio in **decibels (dB)**:

- **0 dB** means the energy ratio is $1$ ($10 \log_{10}(1) = 0$), so the leftover error has the same energy as the voice.
- **+3 dB** means the energy ratio is about $2$, because $\log_{10}(2) \approx 0.301$ and $10 \times 0.301 \approx 3.01$ dB.
- **+10 dB** means the voice has $10$ times the energy of the error ($10 \log_{10}(10) = 10$), and **+20 dB** means $100$ times ($10 \log_{10}(100) = 20$).

**Larger is better.** Because the norms are squared inside the fraction, cutting the error norm $\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert$ in half divides $\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2$ by $2^2 = 4$. That multiplies the energy ratio by $4$ and adds $10 \log_{10}(4) \approx 6.02$ dB to the SNR.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common mistake: 10 vs 20 and squared norms">
Using $x - y$ (the added noise) instead of $x - \hat{x}$ (the leftover error after filtering), or confusing power and amplitude formulas. Decibels for energy or power use $10 \log_{10}(\text{energy ratio})$. Decibels for amplitudes or norms use $20 \log_{10}(\text{amplitude ratio})$. Because energy is norm squared ($\lVert x \rVert^2$), the logarithm property $\log_{10}(a^2) = 2 \log_{10}(a)$ means `10 * log10(norm(x)^2 / norm(x - x_hat)^2)` equals `20 * log10(norm(x) / norm(x - x_hat))`. Leaving out the squares when using `10 * log10` cuts the decibel result in half.
</Callout>

## Worked example

Back to the [five samples](/courses/computational-mathematics/noise-and-averaging#a-five-sample-example): $x = [2\ 2\ 2\ 2\ 2]$, so the signal energy is

$$
\lVert x \rVert^2 = 5 \times 2^2 = 5 \times 4 = 20
$$

**Before filtering ($\hat{x} = y = [3\ 1\ 3\ 1\ 3]$):**

- Error vector: $x - y = [2-3,\ 2-1,\ 2-3,\ 2-1,\ 2-3] = [-1,\ 1,\ -1,\ 1,\ -1]$.
- Squared error norm: $\lVert x - y \rVert^2 = (-1)^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2 = 5$.
- Error norm: $\lVert x - y \rVert = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.2361 \approx 2.24$.
- SNR: $10 \log_{10}(20 / 5) = 10 \log_{10}(4) \approx 10 \times 0.6021 = 6.02 \approx 6.0\text{ dB}$.

**After the 3-sample average ($\hat{x} = [\tfrac43\ \tfrac73\ \tfrac53\ \tfrac73\ \tfrac43] \approx [1.33\ 2.33\ 1.67\ 2.33\ 1.33]$):**

- Error vector: $x - \hat{x} = [\tfrac23,\ -\tfrac13,\ \tfrac13,\ -\tfrac13,\ \tfrac23] \approx [0.67,\ -0.33,\ 0.33,\ -0.33,\ 0.67]$.
- Squared error norm: $\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2 = \frac{4 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 4}{9} = \frac{11}{9} \approx 1.2222 \approx 1.22$.
- Error norm: $\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert = \sqrt{11/9} \approx 1.1055 \approx 1.11$.
- SNR: $10 \log_{10}(20 / (11/9)) = 10 \log_{10}(20 \times \tfrac{9}{11}) = 10 \log_{10}(\tfrac{180}{11}) \approx 10 \times 1.2139 \approx 12.14 \approx 12.1\text{ dB}$.

| | errors $x - \hat{x}$ | $\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2$ | $\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert$ | SNR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| before ($\hat{x} = y$) | −1, 1, −1, 1, −1 | 5.00 | 2.24 | 6.0 dB |
| after (M = 3) | 0.67, −0.33, 0.33, −0.33, 0.67 | 1.22 | 1.11 | 12.1 dB |

The error norm halved (from $2.24$ to $1.11$), which quartered the squared error (from $5.00$ to $1.22$) and raised the SNR by about $6$ dB. The two end samples improved less than the middle ones, from $1$ away to $\tfrac23 \approx 0.67$ away, because the padded zeros past the edges pull them down. On a real recording of 132,300 samples, two edge samples barely matter.

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
x = 2 * ones(1, 5);
y = [3 1 3 1 3];
x_hat = conv(y, ones(1, 3) / 3, 'same');

err = norm(x - x_hat)

err =
    1.1055

snr_db = 10 * log10(norm(x)^2 / norm(x - x_hat)^2)

snr_db =
   12.1388
```

```python
import numpy as np

def conv_same(x, h):
    start = len(h) // 2
    return np.convolve(x, h)[start:start + len(x)]

x = np.full(5, 2.0)
y = np.array([3.0, 1, 3, 1, 3])
x_hat = conv_same(y, np.ones(3) / 3)

err = np.linalg.norm(x - x_hat)
snr_db = 10 * np.log10(np.linalg.norm(x) ** 2 / err ** 2)
print(round(err, 4))  # 1.1055
print(round(snr_db, 4))  # 12.1388
```

</CodeTabs>

<Callout tone="sky" label="The same numbers train AI">
Dividing the squared error $\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2$ by the sample count $N$ gives the [mean squared error](/courses/computational-mathematics/derivatives-and-curve-fitting#derivatives), $\frac{1}{N}\sum_n (x(n) - \hat{x}(n))^2$. Most machine learning models minimise this loss while training. Speech-enhancement networks, such as the noise filters in [real-time calls](/courses/computer-networks/udp-and-ports), train on losses like this and report their results using SNR in decibels.
</Callout>

## The trade-off

Why doesn't the SNR keep rising as $M$ grows? The error $x - \hat{x}$ combines two opposing parts. Let $\bar{x}$ be the moving average of the clean voice and $\bar{e}$ be the moving average of the noise. Then $\hat{x} = \bar{x} + \bar{e}$, so the error vector splits cleanly:

$$
x - \hat{x} = \underbrace{\big(x - \bar{x}\big)}_{\text{signal distortion (blur)}} - \underbrace{\bar{e}}_{\text{residual noise}}
$$

1. **Residual noise $\bar{e}$ shrinks** like $\sigma / \sqrt{M}$ as $M$ grows, because neighbouring independent noise bumps cancel out.
2. **Signal distortion $x - \bar{x}$ grows** as the window $M$ widens. When $M = 1$, $\bar{x} = x$ and distortion is zero; as $M$ grows larger than the voice's fast variations, averaging blurs away true signal features.

The signal below has slow waves, one sharp spike and one fast burst. Increase $M$ and watch the noise shrink, then watch the spike and the burst flatten:

<FilterPlayground />

The small chart underneath runs the filter for every $M$ from 1 to 61 and plots the SNR. At small $M$, increasing $M$ removes more noise than it blurs the signal, so $\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert$ falls and the SNR rises. Past the peak, the window is wide enough to flatten the spike and the fast burst. Each increase in $M$ then blurs the clean signal more than it cuts the remaining noise, so the SNR falls. The violet dot marks the best $M$ for this signal and noise level. When you raise the noise strength $\sigma$, that peak moves to the right: heavier noise makes a wider window worth the extra blur.

**Finding that peak, and explaining why it sits there, is the core of the project.**

## Hear it

You can hear the same trade-off with your ears. Below is a synthesized voice, a 140 Hz tone with 25 overtones shaped into two syllables. Play the noisy version, then filter it with $M = 10$ and $M = 60$:

<HearIt />

At small $M$, the hiss fades while the voice stays clear. At $M = 60$, the hiss is gone, but the voice sounds muffled because the wide window erases the higher overtones. In this player ($F_s = 22{,}050$ Hz, half the CD rate to save browser computation while easily keeping speech frequencies up to $11{,}025$ Hz), the 10th overtone at $10 \times 140 = 1{,}400$ Hz has a cycle of only $22{,}050 / 1{,}400 \approx 16$ samples. Inside a 60-sample window, that overtone completes almost four full cycles, so its peaks and troughs cancel out in the average just like noise.

## Your project

You now have every piece. Here is the full project to run on a real recording.

1. **Record yourself** saying a short sentence, about 3 seconds, and load it as a vector `x`. If the file is stereo (2 columns), keep the first column: in MATLAB use `if size(x, 2) > 1, x = x(:, 1); end`, or in Python use `x = x[:, 0] if x.ndim > 1 else x` (checking dimensions prevents an indexing error on mono recordings).
2. **Add noise** with a fixed seed: `rng(42); y = x + sigma * randn(size(x));`. Start with $\sigma$ around 0.05; recordings are quieter than the examples here.
3. **Filter** with `x_hat = conv(y, ones(1, M) / M, 'same')` for every $M$ from 1 to 61.
4. **Measure** the error norm and the SNR for each $M$, and plot SNR against $M$.
5. **Find the best $M$** and listen at the best $M$, at a much smaller one and at a much larger one. In MATLAB, use `sound(x_hat, Fs)`. In Python, save with `sf.write('filtered.wav', x_hat, Fs)` to play in your audio player, or use `IPython.display.Audio(x_hat, rate=Fs)` in a notebook.
6. **Explain** the shape of the curve in your own words, using the $\sqrt{M}$ rule from the [noise lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/noise-and-averaging#why-averaging-neighbours-removes-noise) and the spike example from the [convolution lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/averaging-is-convolution#what-just-happened).
7. **Repeat** for three different values of $\sigma$ and report how the best $M$ moves.

To go further, try a wider centre-heavy kernel and compare its best SNR with the plain average. You can also try undoing the filter with `deconv` from the [polynomial division lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/multiplying-and-dividing#deconvolution). Polynomial long division divides each step by the leading kernel weight ($1 / (1/M) = M$), so even tiny noise in $y$ is multiplied by $M$ and compounded recursively at every subsequent sample, causing the recovered numbers to explode.
