# Project: Measuring the Trade-off

To say how well a filter worked, you have to compare its output with the clean voice. But in a real recording, the clean voice is exactly what you don't have. So the project cheats on purpose: **record a voice $x$, add noise yourself with a fixed seed, and keep $x$ to compare against**. That's a controlled experiment, and it's how every noise-removal method gets tested.

Write $\hat{x}$ ("x-hat") for the estimate of the clean signal, the recording after filtering. Two measures say how close it is.

## Error norm: how far off, as one number

$$
\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert = \sqrt{\sum_n \big(x(n) - \hat{x}(n)\big)^2}
$$

Subtract the estimate from the truth sample by sample, square each difference so negatives don't cancel positives, add them up and take the square root. That's the length of the error vector, the [2-norm](/courses/computational-mathematics/norms-and-trace#three-norms-to-know). **Smaller is better.**

## Signal-to-noise ratio: how loud the voice is next to what's left of the noise

$$
\text{SNR} = 10 \log_{10} \frac{\lVert x \rVert^2}{\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2} \quad \text{in decibels (dB)}
$$

The fraction compares the strength of the voice with the strength of the leftover error. The **decibel** puts that ratio on a logarithmic scale: every +10 dB means the voice is 10 times stronger relative to the noise, +3 dB means about twice as strong, and 0 dB means the noise is as strong as the voice. **Larger is better.**

## Worked example

Back to the [five samples](/courses/computational-mathematics/noise-and-averaging#a-five-sample-example): $x = [2\ 2\ 2\ 2\ 2]$, so $\lVert x \rVert^2 = 5 \times 2^2 = 20$. After the 3-sample average, $\hat{x} = [1.33\ 2.33\ 1.67\ 2.33\ 1.33]$.

| | errors $x - \hat{x}$ | $\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2$ | $\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert$ | SNR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| before ($\hat{x} = y$) | −1, 1, −1, 1, −1 | 5.00 | 2.24 | 6.0 dB |
| after (M = 3) | 0.67, −0.33, 0.33, −0.33, 0.67 | 1.22 | 1.11 | 12.1 dB |

For example, before filtering: $10 \log_{10}(20 / 5) = 10 \log_{10} 4 \approx 6.0$ dB.

The error halved and the SNR went up by about 6 dB. The two end samples improved less than the middle ones, from 1 away to only 0.67 away, because the zeros past the edges pull them down. On a real recording of 132,300 samples, two edge samples barely matter.

```matlab
x = 2 * ones(1, 5);
y = [3 1 3 1 3];
x_hat = conv(y, ones(1, 3) / 3, 'same');

err = norm(x - x_hat)

err =
    1.1055

snr_db = 10 * log10(norm(x)^2 / norm(x - x_hat)^2)

snr_db =
   12.1388
```

```python
import numpy as np

def conv_same(x, h):
    start = len(h) // 2
    return np.convolve(x, h)[start:start + len(x)]

x = np.full(5, 2.0)
y = np.array([3.0, 1, 3, 1, 3])
x_hat = conv_same(y, np.ones(3) / 3)

err = np.linalg.norm(x - x_hat)
snr_db = 10 * np.log10(np.linalg.norm(x) ** 2 / err ** 2)
print(round(err, 4))  # 1.1055
print(round(snr_db, 4))  # 12.1388
```

</CodeTabs>

<Callout tone="sky" label="The same numbers train AI">
The squared error norm divided by the number of samples is the **mean squared error**, the loss function most machine learning models minimise while training. Speech-enhancement networks, the ones that clean up calls in Zoom and Teams, are trained with losses like it and compared by SNR in decibels, exactly the two measures above.
</Callout>

## The trade-off

Averaging more samples removes more noise, but it also smears every fast, real change. The signal below has slow waves, one sharp spike and one fast burst. Increase $M$ and watch the noise shrink, then watch the spike and the burst flatten:

<FilterPlayground />

The small chart underneath runs the filter for every $M$ from 1 to 61 and plots the SNR. It rises while noise removal wins, peaks, then falls once blurring wins. The violet dot marks the best $M$ for this signal and this noise. Change the noise strength and the peak moves: stronger noise is worth more blur.

**Finding that peak, and explaining why it's there, is the core of the project.**

## Hear it

Numbers are one thing; ears are another. Here's a voice-like buzz, a 140 Hz tone with 25 overtones shaped into two syllables. Play the noisy version, then filter it with $M = 10$, then $M = 60$:

At small $M$ the hiss fades. At large $M$ the hiss is gone, but so is the brightness of the voice: the overtones are fast changes, and averaging smooths them away. That's the trade-off you can hear.

## Your project

You now have every piece. Here's the full project to run on a real recording.

1. **Record yourself** saying a short sentence, about 3 seconds, and load it as a vector $x$. If the file is stereo, keep one column.
2. **Add noise** with a fixed seed: `rng(42); y = x + sigma * randn(size(x));`. Start with $\sigma$ around 0.05; recordings are quieter than the examples here.
3. **Filter** with `x_hat = conv(y, ones(1, M) / M, 'same')` for every $M$ from 1 to 61.
4. **Measure** the error norm and the SNR for each $M$, and plot SNR against $M$.
5. **Find the best $M$** and listen with `sound(x_hat, Fs)` at the best $M$, at a much smaller one and at a much larger one.
6. **Explain** the shape of the curve in your own words, using the √M rule from the [noise lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/noise-and-averaging#why-averaging-neighbours-removes-noise) and the spike example from the [convolution lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/averaging-is-convolution#what-just-happened).
7. **Repeat** for three different values of $\sigma$ and report how the best $M$ moves.

To go further, try the centre-heavy kernel $[\tfrac14\ \tfrac12\ \tfrac14]$ stretched to more weights, and compare its best SNR with the plain average's. Then try undoing a convolution with `deconv`, which the [polynomial division lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/multiplying-and-dividing#deconvolution) introduces, and see how even a little noise ruins an exact undo.
