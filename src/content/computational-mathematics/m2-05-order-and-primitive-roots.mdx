# Order and Primitive Roots

Keep multiplying a number by itself modulo $n$ and something striking happens: the results repeat in a cycle. How long that cycle runs is what separates a weak base from a strong one in cryptography.

## The order of an element

If $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, the **order** of $a$ modulo $n$, written $\operatorname{ord}_n(a)$, is the smallest positive $k$ with

$$
a^k \equiv 1 \pmod n
$$

The Euler-Fermat theorem guarantees such a $k$ exists, and it also tells us the order is at most $\phi(n)$. For $\operatorname{ord}_{16}(3)$:

$$
3^1 \equiv 3,\quad 3^2 \equiv 9,\quad 3^3 \equiv 11,\quad 3^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{16}
$$

So the order is 4. Other names for the same number are the exponent to which $a$ belongs, and the length of the period generated by $a$. Each power is a hop on the ring below, and the sequence closes as soon as it returns to 1:

## Primitive roots

$a$ is a **primitive root** of $n$ if its order is as large as it can be, $\operatorname{ord}_n(a) = \phi(n)$. Its powers then reach every number coprime to $n$.

3 is a primitive root of 7, since its powers run through all six non-zero values:

$$
3^1 \equiv 3,\ 3^2 \equiv 2,\ 3^3 \equiv 6,\ 3^4 \equiv 4,\ 3^5 \equiv 5,\ 3^6 \equiv 1 \pmod 7
$$

2 isn't, because $2^3 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$ cuts it short. The other primitive root of 7 is 5.

If $a$ is a primitive root of a prime $p$, then $a^1, a^2, \dots, a^{p-1}$ are all distinct modulo $p$, and they cover each of $1, \dots, p - 1$ exactly once. The next lessons rely on this.
