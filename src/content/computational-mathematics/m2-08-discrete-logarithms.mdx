# Discrete Logarithms

The [previous lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/fast-exponentiation) showed that $a^k \bmod p$ computes quickly. This lesson examines the inverse problem: finding $k$ when given the result. No general efficient algorithm is known on classical computers, and public-key cryptography relies on that asymmetry.

## Logarithms, modulo a prime

An ordinary logarithm undoes a power: if $y = x^{\log_x y}$, then $\log_x y$ is the exponent. Three rules go with it:

$$
\log_x 1 = 0, \qquad \log_x (yz) = \log_x y + \log_x z, \qquad \log_x (y^r) = r \log_x y
$$

Now take a [primitive root](/courses/computational-mathematics/order-and-primitive-roots#primitive-roots) $a$ of a prime $p$. Its powers hit every value from 1 to $p - 1$ exactly once, so for any $b$ in that range there's a unique exponent $k$ with

$$
b \equiv a^k \pmod p, \qquad 0 \le k \le p - 2
$$

This exponent $k$ is the **discrete logarithm** of $b$ for the base $a$ modulo $p$, denoted $\log_a(b)$ or the **index** $\operatorname{ind}_a(b)$. (Because $a^0 \equiv a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$, discrete logarithms can be chosen from $\{0, 1, \dots, p - 2\}$ or $\{1, 2, \dots, p - 1\}$. Tables often set $\log_a(1) = p - 1$.)

Standard logarithm rules remain valid, but exponents reduce modulo $\phi(p) = p - 1$, [Euler's totient](/courses/computational-mathematics/primality-and-the-totient#eulers-totient-function) of $p$, instead of modulo $p$. Why? Powers cycle every $p - 1$ steps by Fermat's Little Theorem ($a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$). Multiplying two values adds their exponents:

$$
b \cdot c \equiv a^{\log_a b} \cdot a^{\log_a c} = a^{\log_a b + \log_a c} \pmod p
$$

Since exponents on base $a$ cycle with period $p - 1$, we have:

$$
\log_a(bc) \equiv \log_a(b) + \log_a(c) \pmod{p - 1}
$$

## A small example

It also works for some non-prime moduli. For $n = 9$, $\phi(9) = 6$ and 2 is a primitive root:

$$
2^0 = 1,\ 2^1 = 2,\ 2^2 = 4,\ 2^3 = 8,\ 2^4 \equiv 7,\ 2^5 \equiv 5 \pmod 9
$$

Rearranged, that gives a table of logs:

| Number | 1 | 2 | 4 | 5 | 7 | 8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Log | 0 | 1 | 2 | 5 | 4 | 3 |

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
mod(2 .^ (0:5), 9)     % 1 2 4 8 7 5: the powers, in order of exponent
```

```python
# number -> its log, the table above
print({pow(2, k, 9): k for k in range(6)})  # {1: 0, 2: 1, 4: 2, 8: 3, 7: 4, 5: 5}
```

</CodeTabs>

## Try finding one

Pick a base and a target modulo 19. Chords jump across the circle without a smooth pattern, giving no hint about whether a guess is close:

<DiscreteLogSearch />

Modulo 19, the search is small enough to finish by hand. Searching for $b = 11$ with base $a = 2$ gives $2^{12} \equiv 11 \pmod{19}$, so $\log_2(11) = 12$.

For larger numbers, brute force tests successive powers:

- **$\log_3(37) \pmod{101}$:** testing powers of 3 finds $3^{24} \equiv 37 \pmod{101}$, giving the discrete logarithm $k = 24$.
- **$\log_{63}(1454) \pmod{2017}$:** testing powers of 63 modulo the prime 2017 finds $63^{245} \equiv 1454 \pmod{2017}$, giving $k = 245$.

For primes with hundreds of digits, linear search is impossible. Specialized algorithms like baby-step giant-step and the index calculus method improve on brute force, but remain computationally intractable for cryptographic keys.

A brute-force search requires only a few lines of code, illustrating how search time scales with modulus size:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
b = 11;  a = 2;  p = 19;
v = 1;
for k = 0:p-1
    if v == b
        break             % found: a^k is b
    end
    v = mod(v * a, p);
end
k

k =
    12
```

```python
def discrete_log(b, a, p):
    v = 1
    for k in range(p):
        if v == b:
            return k  # found: a^k is b
        v = v * a % p
    return None

print(discrete_log(11, 2, 19))       # 12
print(discrete_log(37, 3, 101))      # 24
print(discrete_log(1454, 63, 2017))  # 245
print(pow(3, 24, 101))               # 37
print(pow(63, 245, 2017))            # 1454
```

</CodeTabs>

## A one-way street

| Direction | Problem | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forward | Compute $a^k \bmod p$ | Fast, about $\log k$ multiplications |
| Backward | Find $k$ from $a^k \bmod p$ | No known fast method on classical computers |

This computational asymmetry enables key agreement over public channels. In **Diffie-Hellman**, two parties agree on a prime $p$ and a base $a$. Each generates a private exponent, $x$ and $y$, and transmits $A = a^x \bmod p$ and $B = a^y \bmod p$ publicly. Both compute $(a^y)^x = a^{xy} = (a^x)^y \pmod p$ to find the shared secret. An eavesdropper intercepting $A$ and $B$ must solve a discrete logarithm to recover $x$ or $y$.

Trace the arithmetic step by step with prime $p = 19$ and base $a = 2$:

1. Alice chooses secret $x = 5$ and computes her public key:
   $$
   A = 2^5 \bmod 19 = 32 \bmod 19 = 13
   $$
2. Bob chooses secret $y = 7$ and computes his public key:
   $$
   B = 2^7 \bmod 19 = 128 = 6 \cdot 19 + 14 \equiv 14 \pmod{19}
   $$
3. Alice receives $B = 14$ and raises it to her secret $x = 5$:
   $$
   B^x \equiv 14^5 \equiv (-5)^5 = -3125 = -165 \cdot 19 + 10 \equiv 10 \pmod{19}
   $$
4. Bob receives $A = 13$ and raises it to his secret $y = 7$:
   $$
   A^y \equiv 13^7 \equiv (-6)^7 = -279936 = -14734 \cdot 19 + 10 \equiv 10 \pmod{19}
   $$

Both compute the same shared key, $10$. An observer intercepting $A = 13$ and $B = 14$ cannot compute $2^{35} \equiv 10 \pmod{19}$ without solving $2^x \equiv 13 \pmod{19}$ or $2^y \equiv 14 \pmod{19}$.

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
p = 19;  a = 2;
x = 5;  y = 7;                    % the two private exponents

powermod(powermod(a, y, p), x, p) % one side: (a^y)^x
powermod(powermod(a, x, p), y, p) % the other side: (a^x)^y, the same 10
```

```python
p, a = 19, 2
x, y = 5, 7  # the two private exponents

print(pow(pow(a, y, p), x, p) == pow(pow(a, x, p), y, p))  # True
```

</CodeTabs>

AI systems depend on this one-way street for protection, even though no model ever computes a discrete log:

- **Secure aggregation.** Phones participating in federated learning use Diffie-Hellman to establish pairwise random masks. Each pair of masks cancels out in the aggregate sum, allowing the server to record the total update without seeing individual contributions.
- **Signed models.** Downloading pretrained weights requires trusting binary files. A digital signature, built on this asymmetric arithmetic, verifies publisher identity and proves that no one has modified the weights.

Contrast this behavior with neural network training. Gradient descent relies on smooth functions where small input adjustments produce small output changes. On the modular ring, powers jump unpredictably without an underlying slope. That absence of smooth gradients prevents continuous optimizers from learning discrete logarithms.

## Integer factorization

There's a second problem of this kind. Given a [composite](/courses/computational-mathematics/primes-and-the-sieve#prime-and-composite) number $N$, find integers $p$ and $q$ with $p \cdot q = N$. Multiplying is easy, but factoring a product of two large primes is not. RSA depends on this one, in the same way that Diffie-Hellman depends on discrete logs.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common errors">
- **Reducing discrete logarithms modulo $p$:** Exponents and logarithms operate in modular arithmetic modulo $\phi(p) = p - 1$, not modulo $p$. For example, adding two discrete logarithms requires reducing modulo $p - 1$.
- **Attempting continuous logarithms:** Using standard math `log(b) / log(a)` fails completely in modular arithmetic. Continuous logarithms require smooth, monotonic curves; modular powers jump pseudo-randomly over $\mathbb{Z}_p$.
- **Swapping base and target:** In $\log_a(b) = k$, $a$ is the base that is raised to the power $k$, and $b$ is the target value ($a^k \equiv b \pmod p$). Confusing them with $\log_b(a)$ or evaluating $a^b \pmod p$ yields unrelated numbers.
</Callout>
