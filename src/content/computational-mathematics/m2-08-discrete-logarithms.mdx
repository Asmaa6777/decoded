# Discrete Logarithms

The [last lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/fast-exponentiation) showed that $a^k \bmod p$ is fast to compute. This lesson is about going backwards, finding $k$ when you know the result. Nobody knows a fast way to do that in general, and public-key cryptography is built on that gap.

## Logarithms, modulo a prime

An ordinary logarithm undoes a power: if $y = x^{\log_x y}$, then $\log_x y$ is the exponent. Three rules go with it:

$$
\log_x 1 = 0, \qquad \log_x (yz) = \log_x y + \log_x z, \qquad \log_x (y^r) = r \log_x y
$$

Now take a [primitive root](/courses/computational-mathematics/order-and-primitive-roots#primitive-roots) $a$ of a prime $p$. Its powers hit every value from 1 to $p - 1$ exactly once, so for any $b$ in that range there's a unique exponent $k$ with

$$
b \equiv a^k \pmod p, \qquad 0 \le k \le p - 2
$$

This $k$ is the **discrete logarithm** of $b$ to the base $a$. The same rules hold, with the exponents worked out modulo $\phi(p)$, [Euler's totient](/courses/computational-mathematics/primality-and-the-totient#eulers-totient-function) of $p$.

## A small example

It also works for some non-prime moduli. For $n = 9$, $\phi(9) = 6$ and 2 is a primitive root:

$$
2^0 = 1,\ 2^1 = 2,\ 2^2 = 4,\ 2^3 = 8,\ 2^4 \equiv 7,\ 2^5 \equiv 5 \pmod 9
$$

Rearranged, that gives a table of logs:

| Number | 1 | 2 | 4 | 5 | 7 | 8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Log | 0 | 1 | 2 | 5 | 4 | 3 |

## A one-way street

| Direction | Problem | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forward | Compute $a^k \bmod p$ | Fast, about $\log k$ multiplications |
| Backward | Find $k$ from $a^k \bmod p$ | No known fast method on classical computers |

## Integer factorization

There's a second problem of this kind. Given a [composite](/courses/computational-mathematics/primes-and-the-sieve#prime-and-composite) number $N$, find integers $p$ and $q$ with $p \cdot q = N$. Multiplying is easy, but factoring a product of two large primes is not. RSA depends on this one, in the same way that Diffie-Hellman depends on discrete logs.

## In Python

The brute-force search takes a few lines of pure Python, and it's also a good way to see the time grow as $p$ does:

```python
def discrete_log(b, a, p):
    v = 1
    for k in range(p):
        if v == b:
            return k
        v = v * a % p
    return None

print(discrete_log(11, 2, 19))  # 12
print({pow(2, k, 9): k for k in range(6)})  # {1: 0, 2: 1, 4: 2, 8: 3, 7: 4, 5: 5}
```

Here's Diffie-Hellman with toy numbers. Both sides reach the same secret without ever sending it:

```python
p, a = 19, 2
x, y = 5, 7

print(pow(pow(a, y, p), x, p) == pow(pow(a, x, p), y, p))  # True
```

## In machine learning

Models don't compute discrete logs, but ML systems depend on this one-way street for protection:

- **Secure aggregation.** Clients in federated learning use Diffie-Hellman to agree on pairs of random masks. Each pair cancels out in the sum, so the server sees the total update and not the individual ones.
- **Model signing.** Downloading pretrained weights means trusting a file. Signatures, which are built on this kind of one-way maths, prove who published it and that nobody altered it.

Note the contrast with gradient descent. A neural network can learn a function because small changes in the input cause small changes in the output. The powers on the ring above jump around with no such pattern, so there's no slope to follow, and that's a large part of why these functions resist attack by learning.
