# Discrete Logarithms

The [last lesson](/courses/computational-mathematics/fast-exponentiation) showed that $a^k \bmod p$ is fast to compute. This lesson is about going backwards, finding $k$ when you know the result. Nobody knows a fast way to do that in general, and public-key cryptography is built on that gap.

## Logarithms, modulo a prime

An ordinary logarithm undoes a power: if $y = x^{\log_x y}$, then $\log_x y$ is the exponent. Three rules go with it:

$$
\log_x 1 = 0, \qquad \log_x (yz) = \log_x y + \log_x z, \qquad \log_x (y^r) = r \log_x y
$$

Now take a [primitive root](/courses/computational-mathematics/order-and-primitive-roots#primitive-roots) $a$ of a prime $p$. Its powers hit every value from 1 to $p - 1$ exactly once, so for any $b$ in that range there's a unique exponent $k$ with

$$
b \equiv a^k \pmod p, \qquad 0 \le k \le p - 2
$$

This exponent $k$ is the **discrete logarithm** of $b$ for the base $a$ modulo $p$, denoted $\log_a(b)$ or the **index** $\operatorname{ind}_a(b)$. (Because $a^0 \equiv a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$, discrete logarithms can be represented in $\{0, 1, \dots, p - 2\}$ or $\{1, 2, \dots, p - 1\}$; tables of discrete logarithms often assign $\log_a(1) = p - 1$.) The same logarithm rules hold, with exponents reduced modulo $\phi(p) = p - 1$, [Euler's totient](/courses/computational-mathematics/primality-and-the-totient#eulers-totient-function) of $p$.

## A small example

It also works for some non-prime moduli. For $n = 9$, $\phi(9) = 6$ and 2 is a primitive root:

$$
2^0 = 1,\ 2^1 = 2,\ 2^2 = 4,\ 2^3 = 8,\ 2^4 \equiv 7,\ 2^5 \equiv 5 \pmod 9
$$

Rearranged, that gives a table of logs:

| Number | 1 | 2 | 4 | 5 | 7 | 8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Log | 0 | 1 | 2 | 5 | 4 | 3 |

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
mod(2 .^ (0:5), 9)     % 1 2 4 8 7 5: the powers, in order of exponent
```

```python
# number -> its log, the table above
print({pow(2, k, 9): k for k in range(6)})  # {1: 0, 2: 1, 4: 2, 8: 3, 7: 4, 5: 5}
```

</CodeTabs>

## Try finding one

Pick a base and a target modulo 19. The chords jump around with no visible pattern, so the search gets no hint about whether it's close:

<DiscreteLogSearch />

Modulo 19 you can finish by hand, where searching for $b = 11$ with base $a = 2$ gives $2^{12} \equiv 11 \pmod{19}$, so $\log_2(11) = 12$.

For larger numbers, brute force tests successive powers:

- **$\log_3(37) \pmod{101}$:** testing powers of 3 finds $3^{24} \equiv 37 \pmod{101}$, giving the discrete logarithm $k = 24$.
- **$\log_{63}(1454) \pmod{2017}$:** testing powers of 63 modulo the prime 2017 finds $63^{245} \equiv 1454 \pmod{2017}$, giving $k = 245$.

With a prime of hundreds of digits, this linear search would never end. Better algorithms exist, such as baby-step giant-step and the number field sieve, but none of them are fast enough at cryptographic sizes.

The brute-force search is a few lines, and it's a good way to watch the time grow as $p$ does:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
b = 11;  a = 2;  p = 19;
v = 1;
for k = 0:p-1
    if v == b
        break             % found: a^k is b
    end
    v = mod(v * a, p);
end
k

k =
    12
```

```python
def discrete_log(b, a, p):
    v = 1
    for k in range(p):
        if v == b:
            return k  # found: a^k is b
        v = v * a % p
    return None

print(discrete_log(11, 2, 19))       # 12
print(discrete_log(37, 3, 101))      # 24
print(discrete_log(1454, 63, 2017))  # 245
print(pow(3, 24, 101))               # 37
print(pow(63, 245, 2017))            # 1454
```

</CodeTabs>

## A one-way street

| Direction | Problem | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forward | Compute $a^k \bmod p$ | Fast, about $\log k$ multiplications |
| Backward | Find $k$ from $a^k \bmod p$ | No known fast method on classical computers |

That gap lets two people agree on a secret in public. In **Diffie-Hellman**, they share a prime $p$ and a base $a$. Each picks a private exponent, $x$ and $y$, and sends the other $a^x$ or $a^y$ in the open. Both can then compute $a^{xy}$, but an eavesdropper who saw only $a^x$ and $a^y$ would need a discrete log to do the same:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
p = 19;  a = 2;
x = 5;  y = 7;                    % the two private exponents

powermod(powermod(a, y, p), x, p) % one side: (a^y)^x
powermod(powermod(a, x, p), y, p) % the other side: (a^x)^y, the same 10
```

```python
p, a = 19, 2
x, y = 5, 7  # the two private exponents

print(pow(pow(a, y, p), x, p) == pow(pow(a, x, p), y, p))  # True
```

</CodeTabs>

AI systems depend on this one-way street for protection, even though no model ever computes a discrete log:

- **Secure aggregation.** Phones taking part in federated learning use Diffie-Hellman to agree, pair by pair, on random masks to add to their updates. Each pair of masks cancels out in the sum, so the server sees the total update and none of the individual ones.
- **Signed models.** Downloading pretrained weights means trusting a large file you can't inspect. A digital signature, built on this kind of one-way maths, proves who published it and that nobody has tampered with it since.

Notice the contrast with how neural networks learn. Gradient descent works because small changes in the input make small changes in the output, so there's always a slope to follow. The powers on the ring above jump around with no such pattern, so there's no slope at all, and that's a large part of why these functions resist being learned.

## Integer factorization

There's a second problem of this kind. Given a [composite](/courses/computational-mathematics/primes-and-the-sieve#prime-and-composite) number $N$, find integers $p$ and $q$ with $p \cdot q = N$. Multiplying is easy, but factoring a product of two large primes is not. RSA depends on this one, in the same way that Diffie-Hellman depends on discrete logs.
