# Project: Noise and Why Averaging Works

**Noise** is everything in a recording you didn't want: hiss from a cheap microphone, a fan, electrical hum. This project uses the simplest model of it, the **additive noise model**: the noise is added to the clean voice, sample by sample.

$$
y(n) = x(n) + e(n)
$$

Here $n$ is the sample number ($1, 2, 3, \dots$), $x$ is the clean voice, $e$ is the noise and $y$ is what the microphone records. All three are vectors of the same length, and this is plain [vector addition](/courses/computational-mathematics/matrices-and-matlab#adding-and-subtracting). Why addition? Sound waves are small changes in air pressure. When two sounds reach the microphone at the same time, their pressure changes add together.

<NoiseAdd />

We hear only the bottom row, $y$. The goal of the project is to recover an estimate of $x$ from $y$.

## A five-sample example

Big recordings hide what is going on, so start with five samples. The clean value is always 2, and the noise alternates $+1$ and $-1$. Adding them sample by sample ($2 + 1 = 3$, $2 + (-1) = 1$, and so on) gives the recorded signal:

| n | clean x(n) | noise e(n) | recorded y(n) = x(n) + e(n) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | +1 | 3 |
| 2 | 2 | −1 | 1 |
| 3 | 2 | +1 | 3 |
| 4 | 2 | −1 | 1 |
| 5 | 2 | +1 | 3 |

The next two lessons reuse this example: **the clean signal is [2 2 2 2 2] and the recorded signal is [3 1 3 1 3]**.

Real noise does not alternate cleanly between $+1$ and $-1$. Instead, each $e(n)$ is drawn at random from a **normal distribution** (also called a **Gaussian distribution**). This distribution has a bell-shaped curve where values near the **mean** (average) are common and large jumps are rare. Here the mean is 0, so positive and negative bumps are equally likely and the noise does not shift the signal up or down on average. The noise strength $\sigma$ (sigma) is the [standard deviation](/courses/computational-mathematics/matrices-and-matlab#adding-and-subtracting), which measures the typical size of a bump. In code, `randn` draws numbers with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, and [multiplying by the scalar](/courses/computational-mathematics/matrices-and-matlab#multiplying-by-a-scalar) $\sigma$ scales their spread to $\sigma$.

<GaussianBellVisualizer />

To make an experiment repeatable, fix the **random seed**, the starting number of the [random number generator](/courses/computational-mathematics/stream-ciphers#how-it-works). Every run then gets the same sequence of noise:

<CodeTabs>

```matlab
x = 2 * ones(1, 5);              % [1 1 1 1 1] scaled by 2 -> [2 2 2 2 2]
e = [1 -1 1 -1 1];               % the noise in the example
y = x + e

y =
     3     1     3     1     3

% realistic noise: random, but the same every run
rng(42);                         % fix the seed
sigma = 0.2;
e = sigma * randn(size(x));      % normal noise, spread sigma
y = x + e;
```

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.full(5, 2)                # the clean signal [2, 2, 2, 2, 2]
e = np.array([1, -1, 1, -1, 1])  # the noise in the example
y = x + e
print(y.tolist())  # [3, 1, 3, 1, 3]

# realistic noise: random, but the same every run
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)  # fix the seed
sigma = 0.2
e = sigma * rng.standard_normal(x.shape)
y = x + e
```

</CodeTabs>

<Callout tone="orange" label="Same seed, different numbers">
MATLAB's `rng(42)` and NumPy's `default_rng(42)` use different generators, so they give different noise. Each is repeatable on its own, but don't expect the two languages, or the interactive pictures here, to match number for number.
</Callout>

## Why averaging neighbours removes noise

Two neighbouring samples at 44,100 Hz are only 22.7 microseconds apart. A 250 Hz voice takes $1/250 = 4{,}000$ microseconds for one cycle, so in 22.7 microseconds it moves less than a hundredth of a cycle. **Neighbours of the voice have almost the same value.** Random noise is drawn [independently](/courses/discrete-mathematics/probability-and-expectation#independence) at every sample, so $e(n)$ tells you nothing about $e(n+1)$. It jumps up and down with no pattern.

So average a few neighbours. Because $y(n) = x(n) + e(n)$, the average of $M$ recorded samples splits into the average of the voice plus the average of the noise:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{y(1) + \cdots + y(M)}{M} &= \frac{x(1) + \cdots + x(M)}{M} \\
&\quad + \frac{e(1) + \cdots + e(M)}{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

The voice values are nearly equal, so their average is still the voice. On the five-sample example, take the first three recorded samples $[3, 1, 3]$. The clean part averages to $(2 + 2 + 2)/3 = 2$, and the noise part averages to $(1 - 1 + 1)/3 = 1/3 \approx 0.33$. Two of the three noise bumps cancel out, so the error shrinks from $1$ to $0.33$ while the clean $2$ stays unchanged. Watch the same cancellation on pure random noise:

<NoiseAveraging />

There is a rule for how much random noise shrinks: **averaging $M$ independent random samples shrinks their standard deviation by a factor of $\sqrt{M}$**. Why $\sqrt{M}$ and not $M$? When you add $M$ independent noise samples, positive and negative bumps partly cancel. Because of that, their [variances](/courses/computational-mathematics/norms-and-trace#the-trace) (squared standard deviations) add up instead of their standard deviations, growing from $\sigma^2$ to $M\sigma^2$. Taking the square root gives a sum with standard deviation $\sqrt{M}\,\sigma$, and dividing by $M$ for the average leaves

$$
\frac{\sqrt{M}\,\sigma}{M} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{M}}
$$

Averaging $M = 9$ samples gives $\sqrt{9} = 3$ times less noise, and averaging $M = 100$ gives $\sqrt{100} = 10$ times less. Because of the square root, cutting the remaining noise in half again takes $2^2 = 4$ times as many samples.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Common mistake">
Expecting the noise to shrink by $M$ instead of $\sqrt{M}$, or forgetting that the noise samples must be independent. Averaging $M = 16$ samples cuts random noise by $\sqrt{16} = 4$, not $16$. Also, if the unwanted sound is a steady 50 Hz electrical hum, neighbouring hum samples have nearly the same value. Averaging a few neighbours will not cancel them.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="sky" label="The same rule, in machine learning">
This $\sqrt{M}$ rule appears wherever data is noisy. Averaging the predictions of $M$ independent models, called an **ensemble**, shrinks their random errors by $\sqrt{M}$. Averaging gradients over a **mini-batch** of $M$ training examples quiets each training step the same way. In the other direction, **diffusion models** (the AI behind image generators) train on $y = x + e$. They add Gaussian noise to a clean image and train a neural network to remove it.
</Callout>

Averaging has a limit, and so does the $\sqrt{M}$ rule: both assume the clean signal does not change across the averaged neighbours.
