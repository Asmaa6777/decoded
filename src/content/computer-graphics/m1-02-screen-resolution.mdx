# Screen Resolution and Pixel Density

A screen is a physical grid of pixels. Its **display resolution** (or **screen resolution**) is how many pixels it has, written like an image's dimensions: columns × rows, width first. The grid a screen physically has is its **native resolution**, and it's the most it can show; a lower setting has to stretch each pixel of the picture over more than one of the screen's.

## Reading a resolution

"1080p" means a screen 1920 pixels wide and 1080 tall. Multiply to count the pixels:

$$
1920 \times 1080 = 2{,}073{,}600 \text{ pixels}
$$

about 2.1 million, or 2.1 megapixels, counted the same way as a [camera's megapixels](/courses/computer-graphics/pixels-and-the-image-grid#dimensions-width--height). Each of those is a tiny light the computer can set to any color, and together they make the picture.

Some common resolutions:

| Name | Resolution | Pixels |
|---|---|---|
| VGA | 640 × 480 | 307,200 |
| HD (720p) | 1280 × 720 | 921,600 |
| Full HD (1080p) | 1920 × 1080 | 2,073,600 |
| QHD (1440p) | 2560 × 1440 | 3,686,400 |
| 4K UHD | 3840 × 2160 | 8,294,400 |

4K has exactly four times the pixels of 1080p: twice as many columns and twice as many rows.

## Aspect ratio

The **aspect ratio** is the shape of the screen: width compared with height. Divide both numbers by their [greatest common divisor](/courses/computational-mathematics/divisibility-and-euclid) to reduce it:

$$
1920 : 1080 \;\xrightarrow{\;\div 120\;}\; 16 : 9
\qquad\qquad
640 : 480 \;\xrightarrow{\;\div 160\;}\; 4 : 3
$$

Old monitors and TVs were 4:3, nearly square. Today's are mostly 16:9 widescreen. A phone held upright is tall and narrow, around 9:19.5.

## Pixel density

A screen's size is sold as its **diagonal** in inches, corner to corner. **Pixel density** is how many pixels sit in one inch of that screen, usually given in PPI. Printers and scanners were measured in DPI first, and for screens a "dot" just means a pixel, so you'll see screen density given in DPI too: the two are used interchangeably. Higher density means smaller pixels, so text and edges look crisper.

The cleanest way to compute it uses the diagonal on both sides. Find how many pixels lie along the diagonal with Pythagoras, then divide by the diagonal's length:

$$
\text{PPI} = \frac{\sqrt{\text{width}^2 + \text{height}^2}}{\text{diagonal in inches}}
$$

For a 24-inch 1080p monitor:

$$
\frac{\sqrt{1920^2 + 1080^2}}{24} = \frac{2203}{24} \approx 92 \text{ PPI}
$$

If you know the physical width instead, you can divide along one side, which gives the same answer because pixels are square:

$$
\text{PPI} = \frac{\text{pixels across}}{\text{width in inches}}
$$

<Callout tone="orange" label="Don't divide by the diagonal alone">
A common slip is "1920 ÷ 24 = 80 PPI". That divides horizontal pixels by a diagonal length, mixing two directions. Pair pixels and inches along the same line: diagonal with diagonal, or width with width.
</Callout>

A classic example: an old 15-inch 640 × 480 monitor. Its diagonal is 800 pixels ($\sqrt{640^2+480^2}$), so its density is $800 \div 15 \approx 53$ PPI, often rounded to "about 50". That same 4:3 screen is 12 inches wide, and $640 \div 12$ gives the same 53.
