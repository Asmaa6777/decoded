# Slope and the Line Equation

A **line-drawing algorithm** answers one question: given two endpoints, which pixels should be lit so the result looks like a straight line between them? Every one of them is built on the equation of a straight line, so start there.

## Slope

Walk up a hill and you feel its steepness: the steeper it is, the more you rise for every step forward. **Slope** puts a number on that. It's how far the line goes up for each step to the right, written $m$:

$$
m = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}
$$

$\Delta$ (delta) just means "change in". For the points $P_1(5, 10)$ and $P_2(11, 14)$:

$$
m = \frac{14 - 10}{11 - 5} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667
$$

So this line rises two-thirds of a pixel for every pixel it moves right.

## What the slope tells you

| Slope | The line | Angle with the x-axis |
|---|---|---|
| $m > 0$ | rises left to right | between 0° and 90° |
| $m < 0$ | falls left to right | between 90° and 180° |
| $m = 0$ | horizontal ($\Delta y = 0$): y never changes | 0° |
| undefined | vertical ($\Delta x = 0$): x never changes, and you can't divide by 0 | 90° |
| $\lvert m \rvert < 1$ | gentle: moves more across than up | under 45° |
| $\lvert m \rvert = 1$ | exactly diagonal | 45° |
| $\lvert m \rvert > 1$ | steep: moves more up than across | over 45° |

The slope is also the tangent of the line's angle: $m = \tan\theta$. A 45° line has slope $\tan 45° = 1$.

<Callout tone="sky" label="The number that matters">
For drawing lines, the most important row is the 1 boundary. Whether $\lvert m \rvert$ is below or above 1 decides which direction the algorithm should step in, as you'll see shortly.
</Callout>

## The slope-intercept equation

Every non-vertical line can be written as

$$
y = mx + c
$$

where $m$ is the slope and $c$ is the **y-intercept**, the y-value where the line crosses the y-axis (at $x = 0$). Once you know $m$, find $c$ by putting either endpoint into the equation and solving:

$$
c = y_1 - m x_1 = 10 - \frac{2}{3} \times 5 = 10 - 3.333 = 6.667
$$

So the line through $P_1$ and $P_2$ is $y = 0.667x + 6.667$. Check it with the other endpoint: $0.667 \times 11 + 6.667 = 14$. ✓

## The naive algorithm: just use the equation

The most direct way to draw a line follows straight from this. (Some notes use the name "naive" for a different method, the one that divides by the number of steps. That one is DDA, and it's covered, with its examples, in the [next lesson](/courses/computer-graphics/dda#example-5-2-2-to-8-5-and-a-homework-line).)

1. Compute $m$ and $c$ from the endpoints.
2. For every whole x from $x_1$ to $x_2$, compute $y = mx + c$.
3. Pixels only exist at whole coordinates, so **round** y and light the pixel $(x, \text{round}(y))$.

Step through it on $P_1(5, 10)$ to $P_2(11, 14)$. The blue dot is the exact point on the line; the pixel lit is the one it rounds to:

<LineStepper algorithm="naive" from={[5, 10]} to={[11, 14]} />

The result:

| x | y = 0.667x + 6.667 | rounded | pixel |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 10 | 10 | (5, 10) |
| 6 | 10.667 | 11 | (6, 11) |
| 7 | 11.333 | 11 | (7, 11) |
| 8 | 12 | 12 | (8, 12) |
| 9 | 12.667 | 13 | (9, 13) |
| 10 | 13.333 | 13 | (10, 13) |
| 11 | 14 | 14 | (11, 14) |

<Callout tone="orange" label="Don't round the intercept">
It's tempting to round $c = 6.667$ to 7 "since pixels are whole numbers". Don't. That moves the entire line up by a third of a pixel, and with $y = 0.666x + 7$ you get pixels (7, 12) and (10, 14) instead of (7, 11) and (10, 13), and the line doesn't even start at (5, 10) exactly. Keep $m$ and $c$ as exact as you can, and round only y, only at the moment you light the pixel.
</Callout>

## Where the naive algorithm breaks

Now try a steep line. Enter `2 1 4 9` (from (2, 1) to (4, 9)) in the boxes above and press **Draw**.

The line rises 8 rows but only crosses 3 columns. The algorithm lights exactly one pixel per column, so it lights 3 pixels for a line that should be 9 long, leaving big gaps. That's the first problem: **stepping along x only works when $\lvert m \rvert \le 1$**. For a steep line, you have to step along y instead and compute x, which means rewriting the equation as $x = (y - c)/m$.

Enter `3 1 3 6` for a vertical line, and the algorithm can't even start, because $\Delta x = 0$ makes the slope a division by zero.

There's a second, quieter problem: **speed**. Every pixel costs a floating-point multiplication ($m \times x$) and an addition, plus a rounding. Multiplication used to be expensive, and a screen draws an enormous number of line pixels.
