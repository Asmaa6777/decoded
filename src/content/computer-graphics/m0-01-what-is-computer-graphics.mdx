# What Computer Graphics Is

Everything you see on a screen right now, these letters included, was drawn by a program that decided the color of every dot. **Computer graphics** is the study of how computers create, store and change pictures, and how they get those pictures onto a display.

This course starts with the smallest piece, a single dot, and works up to the algorithms that decide which dots to light when you ask for a straight line.

## A screen is a grid of dots

Get very close to a screen, or look at it through a magnifying glass, and the smooth picture falls apart into a grid of tiny squares. Each square is a **pixel**, short for *picture element*: the smallest dot a display can control. A pixel shows exactly one color at a time.

Everything else is built from that. Drag the slider down and watch a circle turn into a staircase:

This is the central problem of the whole subject. The shapes we want to draw (circles, lines, letters) are smooth mathematical objects. The screen can only light whole squares. Graphics is mostly the art of choosing the right squares.

## Four words that sound alike

People use these interchangeably, but they mean slightly different things, and exam questions like to test the difference:

- An **image** is any visual content stored digitally: a photo, a scan, a screenshot, a drawing made in Photoshop. Inside a computer, an image is usually a grid of pixel values. File formats such as JPEG, PNG, GIF and BMP are all ways of storing images.
- A **picture** is a single visual representation made by photography, painting, drawing or printing. The word leans towards one standalone piece, the kind you'd frame.
- A **graph** shows data: charts, plots and diagrams that make trends in numbers visible.
- A **photo** (photograph) is an image captured by a camera, digital or film. It records a real scene as faithfully as it can.

So every photo on your phone is an image, but not every image is a photo: a chart you made in a spreadsheet is an image and a graph, and nobody photographed it.

## Interactive and passive graphics

There are two broad kinds of computer graphics, and the difference is who's in control.

**Interactive graphics** run a two-way conversation. You act with a mouse, a touch screen or a game controller, and the program draws a new picture in response, many times a second. Video games, a map you can drag, a button that changes as you hover over it, drawing on a tablet: all interactive.

**Passive** (non-interactive) **graphics** follow their program from start to finish with no input from you. A chart in a PDF, a logo on a business card, an image on a web page and a screen saver are passive. A passive picture can still move. A screen saver animates, but you can't steer it.
