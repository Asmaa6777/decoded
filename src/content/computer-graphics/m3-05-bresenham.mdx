# Bresenham's Line Algorithm

[DDA](/courses/computer-graphics/dda) needs fractions and a rounding for every pixel. Jack Bresenham found a way to draw exactly the same kind of line using **only whole numbers**: additions, subtractions and checking whether a number is negative. That made it much faster on the hardware of the time, and it's still the standard way to draw a line.

## The only real decision

Take a gentle line, slope between 0 and 1. Step x along by 1 each time, as DDA does. After lighting a pixel at $(x_k, y_k)$, the next pixel is in column $x_k + 1$, and there are only two choices for its row:

- **stay** on the same row, $y_k$, or
- **move up** one, to $y_k + 1$.

The line can't jump two rows in one column, because it's gentle. So each step is a yes-or-no question: is the line closer to the upper pixel or the lower one? Equivalently, has the true line passed the **midpoint** between them?

DDA answers this by computing the exact y and rounding. Bresenham keeps a running whole-number score, the **decision parameter** $p$, whose *sign* gives the answer.

## The decision parameter

With $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$ and $\Delta y = y_2 - y_1$:

$$
p_0 = 2\Delta y - \Delta x
$$

Then at each step:

- If $p_k < 0$, the line is still below the midpoint: **stay** on this row, and $p_{k+1} = p_k + 2\Delta y$.
- If $p_k \ge 0$, the line has passed the midpoint: **move up** a row, and $p_{k+1} = p_k + 2\Delta y - 2\Delta x$.

Everything here is a whole number. $2\Delta y$ and $2\Delta y - 2\Delta x$ are computed once at the start, so each step is one comparison and one addition.

## Where the formula comes from

At column $x_k + 1$, the true line is at height $y = m(x_k + 1) + c$. Measure its distance above the lower pixel and below the upper one:

$$
d_1 = y - y_k \qquad d_2 = (y_k + 1) - y
$$

Move up if $d_1 - d_2 \ge 0$, that is, if the line is closer to the upper pixel. Subtracting:

$$
d_1 - d_2 = (y - y_k) - (y_k + 1 - y) = 2y - 2y_k - 1 = 2m(x_k + 1) - 2y_k + 2c - 1
$$

That still contains the fraction $m = \Delta y / \Delta x$. Multiply the whole thing by $\Delta x$, which is positive, so the sign doesn't change, and the fraction disappears. That product is the decision parameter:

$$
p_k = \Delta x\,(d_1 - d_2) = 2\Delta y \cdot x_k - 2\Delta x \cdot y_k + b
$$

where $b = 2\Delta y + \Delta x(2c - 1)$ is the same at every step, so it never needs computing.

**The update rules.** Subtract $p_k$ from $p_{k+1}$. The constant $b$ cancels, and $x$ always goes up by exactly 1:

$$
p_{k+1} - p_k = 2\Delta y\,(x_{k+1} - x_k) - 2\Delta x\,(y_{k+1} - y_k) = 2\Delta y - 2\Delta x\,(y_{k+1} - y_k)
$$

If the pixel stayed on its row, $y_{k+1} - y_k = 0$ and $p$ grows by $2\Delta y$. If it moved up, $y_{k+1} - y_k = 1$ and $p$ grows by $2\Delta y - 2\Delta x$. Those are the two rules.

**The starting value.** The first endpoint $(x_0, y_0)$ is on the line, so $c = y_0 - m x_0$. Putting that into $p_0$, everything except two terms cancels, leaving $p_0 = 2\Delta y - \Delta x$.

The algorithm keeps DDA's decision, but scales it so it never needs a fraction.

## Worked example: (20, 10) to (30, 18)

$\Delta x = 10$, $\Delta y = 8$, so $2\Delta y = 16$ and $2\Delta y - 2\Delta x = -4$.

$p_0 = 2 \times 8 - 10 = 6$.

In the stepper, before each decision the two candidate pixels are outlined in violet with the midpoint between them as a violet dot. After the decision, the one taken turns neon. Watch the orange line: whenever it passes above the dot, $p$ is not negative and the upper pixel wins.

Read the table as: each row's $p$ was produced by the decision written next to it, and that decision is what moved to the pixel on the same row. Eleven pixels, from endpoint to endpoint, with not a single fraction.

## Same line as DDA

Enter `5 4 12 7` and press **Draw**. That's DDA's first example. $\Delta x = 7$, $\Delta y = 3$, $p_0 = 6 - 7 = -1$, and the pixels come out as (5, 4), (6, 4), (7, 5), (8, 5), (9, 6), (10, 6), (11, 7), (12, 7), exactly what DDA gave with the exact slope. Bresenham isn't a different line. It's the same nearest-pixel choice, made without floating point and without drift.

## Steep lines and other directions

The rules above assume $0 \le m \le 1$. The other cases reuse the same idea:

- **Steep lines** ($\lvert m \rvert > 1$): swap the roles of x and y. Step along y every time and use the decision to choose whether x moves. Try `2 1 6 12`.
- **Lines going left or down**: step by −1 instead of +1 in that direction. The decision part is unchanged, because it uses $\lvert\Delta x\rvert$ and $\lvert\Delta y\rvert$.

The C++ in the stepper handles every direction this way.

<Callout tone="orange" label="Exam check">
Memorize three formulas: $p_0 = 2\Delta y - \Delta x$; if $p < 0$ add $2\Delta y$; otherwise add $2\Delta y - 2\Delta x$ and move up. Then always check your last pixel is the endpoint. If it isn't, there's an arithmetic slip in the table.
</Callout>

## DDA against Bresenham

| | DDA | Bresenham |
|---|---|---|
| Arithmetic | floating point | whole numbers only |
| Per pixel | two additions and two roundings | one comparison and one addition |
| Error | grows on long lines as rounding adds up | none: the decision is exact |
| Ease | simpler to understand | needs the decision parameter |

Lists of line algorithms often add a fourth, the **Gupta-Sproull algorithm**. It's Bresenham's algorithm with [antialiasing](/courses/computer-graphics/aliasing#antialiasing-partial-coverage-partial-color) added: as it steps, it also shades the pixels just above and below the line by how far each one is from it, so the line comes out smooth instead of jagged.

Bresenham's algorithm was written for a pen plotter, and it's still how many graphics libraries, printers and embedded displays draw lines. The same decision-parameter trick extends to circles (the midpoint circle algorithm) and ellipses.
</PracticalApplication>
