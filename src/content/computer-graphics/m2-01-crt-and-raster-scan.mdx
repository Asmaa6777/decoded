# The CRT and the Raster Scan

Flat screens replaced them long ago, but the **cathode-ray tube (CRT)** is still where graphics courses start, because the way it drew a picture, one row at a time from top to bottom, became the way every display since is fed. Learn the CRT and the vocabulary of every modern screen falls out of it.

Whatever its technology, the screen part of a computer is called the **display**, the **monitor** or the **video display unit (VDU)**. There are four types to know: the CRT in this lesson, and the LCD, LED and plasma panels in [a later one](/courses/computer-graphics/flat-panels).

## Inside the tube

A CRT is a large sealed glass bottle with the air pumped out. Three things matter:

1. **An electron gun** at the narrow back end fires a thin, focused beam of electrons. A color CRT has three guns, one each for red, green and blue.
2. **Deflection coils** around the neck make magnetic fields that bend the beam up, down and sideways, so it can be aimed at any point on the screen. Some tubes bend it with electric fields from metal plates instead, called **electrostatic deflection**; TVs and computer monitors mostly used magnetic coils.
3. **The screen** at the wide front end is coated on the inside with **phosphor**, a material that glows when electrons hit it. A color screen has tiny red, green and blue phosphor dots grouped together; each group is one pixel.

Where the beam strikes, the phosphor lights up. Fire the beam harder and the spot glows brighter.

## Persistence and refresh

Phosphor doesn't glow forever. It fades within a fraction of a second after the beam leaves. How long it keeps glowing is called its **persistence**.

So the picture can't be drawn once and left. The beam has to keep going back over it, redrawing it before it fades. This is **refreshing** the screen, and the number of complete redraws per second is the **refresh rate**, measured in frames per second or hertz (Hz). CRTs typically refreshed 60 to 85 times a second.

Two rates describe the sweep, and notes often name both:

- The **vertical refresh rate** is how many complete frames are drawn per second, top to bottom. This is the refresh rate above, such as 60 Hz.
- The **horizontal refresh rate** (or scan rate) is how many rows are drawn per second, left to right. It's far higher, because every frame has hundreds of rows: 480 visible rows at 60 Hz is already $480 \times 60 = 28{,}800$ rows a second, and the standard 640 × 480 display ran at about 31,500, because the retrace time counts as rows too.

Both rates are set together by the monitor and the graphics card driving it. Bigger, sharper screens need a faster vertical rate to stay flicker-free, so a common recommendation was at least 60 Hz at 640 × 480 and at least 85 Hz at 1600 × 1200.

Too slow a refresh, and the picture visibly **flickers** as the phosphor fades between visits, which tires the eyes. Larger screens and higher resolutions need faster refresh to cover the whole surface in time.

## The raster scan

A **raster-scan display** sweeps the beam across the screen in a fixed pattern, the same every frame:

1. Start at the top-left corner.
2. Sweep left to right along one row, called a **scan line**, switching the beam on where a pixel should glow and off where it shouldn't.
3. At the end of the row, switch the beam off and swing it back to the left edge of the next row down. This return trip is the **horizontal retrace**.
4. Repeat until the bottom row is done.
5. Switch the beam off and send it back to the top-left corner to start the next frame. This is the **vertical retrace**.

## The frame buffer

Told by what? While the beam sweeps, something has to say, for every position, whether to fire and how brightly. That's the job of the **frame buffer** (also called the **refresh buffer**): an area of memory that holds one value per pixel, the color for every point on the screen.

The display hardware reads the frame buffer row by row, in step with the beam, and the program draws by writing values into it. In the visualizer, the grid of 0s and 1s next to the screen is the frame buffer for that house, one bit per pixel.

The frame buffer's size follows straight from the [image size calculation](/courses/computer-graphics/image-size): resolution × bits per pixel. A 1920 × 1080 screen at 32 bits per pixel needs about 7.9 MB for one frame. It may be its own memory on the graphics card or a reserved part of the computer's main [RAM](/courses/computer-organization/ram-and-rom).

## Scan conversion and the display processor

A program doesn't usually think in pixels. It says "draw a line from here to there" or "fill this circle". Something has to turn those shapes into the right pixel values in the frame buffer. That conversion is called **scan conversion** (or **rasterization**), and the [line-drawing algorithms](/courses/computer-graphics/dda) at the end of this course are exactly that.

To save the CPU from doing all of this, systems add a **display processor** (also called a display processing unit or graphics controller) whose whole job is scan conversion and related work: line styles, filling areas with color, and reading input devices. Today's GPU is the enormous descendant of that idea.
