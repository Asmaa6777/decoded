# Resolution: PPI and DPI

"Resolution" gets used for two different things, and mixing them up is the most common mistake in this topic:

- **Pixel dimensions**: how many pixels an image has, like 1200 × 800. This is fixed by the file.
- **Resolution** in the strict sense: how densely those pixels are packed, **how many pixels per inch**. This decides how big the image looks once it's shown or printed.

The same 1200 × 800 photo can be a postcard or a poster. The pixels don't change; only how tightly they're packed does.

Notes often name the two kinds **image resolution**, the pixel density of an image in PPI, and **screen resolution**, how many pixels a display has. Don't confuse image resolution with **image dimension**, which is the 640 × 480 kind of number: the count of columns and rows. Screen resolution gets [its own lesson](/courses/computer-graphics/screen-resolution).

## PPI: pixels per inch

**PPI (pixels per inch)** counts how many pixels fit along one inch. It describes digital images and screens. More pixels per inch means each pixel is smaller, so the picture is sharper and edges look smoother.

To find the printed size of an image, divide its pixels by the PPI:

$$
\text{inches} = \frac{\text{pixels}}{\text{PPI}}
$$

A 1200 × 800 image at 72 PPI:

$$
\frac{1200}{72} \approx 16.667 \text{ in wide}, \qquad \frac{800}{72} \approx 11.111 \text{ in tall}
$$

The same image at 300 PPI:

$$
\frac{1200}{300} = 4 \text{ in wide}, \qquad \frac{800}{300} \approx 2.667 \text{ in tall}
$$

Raising the PPI from 72 to 300 shrank the print to about a quarter of the width, since $300 \div 72 \approx 4.2$. It's the same file, just with its pixels pushed closer together. If a question wants centimetres, multiply inches by 2.54: 4 inches is 10.16 cm. Try it:

## Working backwards: how many pixels do I need?

Flip the formula when you know the print size you want:

$$
\text{pixels} = \text{inches} \times \text{PPI}
$$

To print a 4 × 6 inch photo at a good quality of 240 PPI, you need

$$
240 \times 4 = 960 \text{ pixels wide}, \qquad 240 \times 6 = 1440 \text{ pixels tall}
$$

A smaller image will either print smaller or get stretched. Stretching shows as blur, because the software has to invent the missing pixels by averaging their neighbors, and averages are softer than real detail.

## The third question: what PPI will I get?

Sometimes you know both the pixels and the print size, and need the quality. Divide pixels by inches:

$$
\text{PPI} = \frac{\text{pixels}}{\text{inches}}
$$

A 1200-pixel-wide photo printed 8 inches wide comes out at $1200 \div 8 = 150$ PPI, fine for a poster seen from across a room but soft up close. So the one formula answers three questions, depending on which two numbers you already have.

<Callout tone="orange" label="More PPI isn't more detail">
Typing a bigger PPI into an image's settings doesn't make it sharper. Without new pixels it only makes the print smaller. Real detail comes from the pixel count; PPI just decides how tightly that detail is packed.
</Callout>

## DPI: dots per inch

**DPI (dots per inch)** belongs to printers. A printer doesn't print pixels. It sprays tiny dots of a few ink colors, and it takes several dots to fake one pixel's color. DPI counts how many of those ink dots the printer can place in an inch. Inkjet printers manage roughly 300 to 720 DPI; laser printers 600 to 2,400.

| | PPI | DPI |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | pixels in a digital image or on a screen | ink dots a printer lays down |
| Belongs to | the image or the display | the printer |
| You set it when | preparing a file | choosing printer quality |

<Callout tone="orange" label="Used interchangeably anyway">
Plenty of software, and plenty of notes, say "DPI" when they mean PPI, especially for screens. On an exam, use PPI for images and screens and DPI for printers, and you'll be right either way.
</Callout>
