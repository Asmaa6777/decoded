# The DDA Line Algorithm

The [naive algorithm](/courses/computer-graphics/slope-and-the-line-equation#the-naive-algorithm-just-use-the-equation) had two problems: gaps in steep lines, and a multiplication for every pixel. The **digital differential analyzer (DDA)** fixes both.

## Idea 1: add instead of multiply

Look at what happens to y as x goes up by one:

$$
y_{k+1} = m(x_k + 1) + c = (m x_k + c) + m = y_k + m
$$

Each new y is just the previous y plus $m$. There's no need to multiply at all: start at the first endpoint and keep adding the slope. Working out each point from the one before is called an **incremental** method, and DDA is the classic example.

## Idea 2: step along the longer direction

To avoid gaps, take one step per pixel along whichever direction the line covers more of, and let the other coordinate change by a fraction each step:

$$
\text{steps} = \max(\lvert \Delta x \rvert, \lvert \Delta y \rvert)
\qquad
x_{\text{inc}} = \frac{\Delta x}{\text{steps}}
\qquad
y_{\text{inc}} = \frac{\Delta y}{\text{steps}}
$$

The longer direction then moves exactly 1 pixel per step (or −1, going backwards), and the shorter one moves by less than 1. Every column (or row) the line passes through gets exactly one pixel, so there are no gaps.

## The three cases

That one formula covers every line, but it's usually taught as three cases, depending on the slope:

| Case | Condition | Steps | x changes by | y changes by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\lvert m \rvert < 1$, gentle | $\lvert \Delta x \rvert$ | 1 | $m$ |
| 2 | $\lvert m \rvert = 1$, diagonal | $\lvert \Delta x \rvert = \lvert \Delta y \rvert$ | 1 | 1 |
| 3 | $\lvert m \rvert > 1$, steep | $\lvert \Delta y \rvert$ | $1/m$ | 1 |

(When the line runs right to left or top to bottom, the 1s become −1s and the increments take the sign of $\Delta x$ or $\Delta y$.)

## The algorithm

1. Compute $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$ and $\Delta y = y_2 - y_1$.
2. $\text{steps} = \max(\lvert\Delta x\rvert, \lvert\Delta y\rvert)$.
3. $x_{\text{inc}} = \Delta x / \text{steps}$ and $y_{\text{inc}} = \Delta y / \text{steps}$.
4. Start at $x = x_1$, $y = y_1$.
5. Repeat steps + 1 times (the +1 is for the starting pixel): light $(\text{round}(x), \text{round}(y))$, then add $x_{\text{inc}}$ to x and $y_{\text{inc}}$ to y.

## Example 1: a gentle line, (5, 4) to (12, 7)

$\Delta x = 7$, $\Delta y = 3$, so $m = 3/7 \approx 0.429$. That's under 1, case 1: 7 steps, x goes up by 1 and y by $3/7$ each time. Step through it:

| k | x | y (exact) | pixel |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 5 | 4 | (5, 4) |
| 1 | 6 | 4.429 | (6, 4) |
| 2 | 7 | 4.857 | (7, 5) |
| 3 | 8 | 5.286 | (8, 5) |
| 4 | 9 | 5.714 | (9, 6) |
| 5 | 10 | 6.143 | (10, 6) |
| 6 | 11 | 6.571 | (11, 7) |
| 7 | 12 | 7 | (12, 7) |

<Callout tone="orange" label="Don't round the slope either">
Many worked solutions round $m$ to 0.4 before starting. Then y goes 4, 4.4, 4.8, 5.2, 5.6, 6, 6.4, 6.8, and it never reaches the endpoint's 7; the last pixel only lands on 7 after rounding. Worse, at x = 11 it gives 6.4 → 6 instead of the true 6.571 → 7, so the drawn line is wrong. Every addition carries the rounding error forward. Keep $m$ as a fraction (3/7) or to several decimals.
</Callout>

## Example 2: a steep line, (5, 7) to (10, 15)

$\Delta x = 5$, $\Delta y = 8$, so $m = 1.6$, over 1: case 3. Now y is the long direction, 8 steps, and x moves by $1/m = 5/8 = 0.625$ each step. Enter `5 7 10 15` above and press **Draw**.

| k | x (exact) | y | pixel |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 5 | 7 | (5, 7) |
| 1 | 5.625 | 8 | (6, 8) |
| 2 | 6.25 | 9 | (6, 9) |
| 3 | 6.875 | 10 | (7, 10) |
| 4 | 7.5 | 11 | (8, 11) |
| 5 | 8.125 | 12 | (8, 12) |
| 6 | 8.75 | 13 | (9, 13) |
| 7 | 9.375 | 14 | (9, 14) |
| 8 | 10 | 15 | (10, 15) |

At k = 4, x is exactly 7.5, halfway between two pixels. This course rounds halves up, so the pixel is (8, 11). (Rounding $1/m$ to 0.6 would give x = 7.4 there and pixel (7, 11), the same carried-error problem as before.) Either neighbor is a reasonable choice for a tie, but pick one rule and stick with it.

## Example 3: diagonals, (3, 5) to (8, 10) and back

$\Delta x = \Delta y = 5$: case 2. Both coordinates go up by exactly 1 each step, so no rounding is needed: (3, 5), (4, 6), (5, 7), (6, 8), (7, 9), (8, 10).

Reverse it, (8, 10) to (3, 5): now $\Delta x = \Delta y = -5$, so both increments are −1 and the same six pixels come out in the opposite order. The formula handles direction by itself, with no special case needed.

## Example 4: horizontal, (2, 2) to (9, 2)

$\Delta y = 0$, so $m = 0$. There are 7 steps, x goes up by 1 and y by 0: (2, 2), (3, 2), … (9, 2), 8 pixels in all. A vertical line works the same way with the roles swapped, which the naive algorithm couldn't do at all.

## What DDA still gets wrong

DDA is simple and gap-free, but it isn't perfect:

- It still uses **floating-point** numbers and **rounds** every pixel, and rounding takes time.
- Adding a rounded $m$ over and over **accumulates error**, so on long lines the computed points drift away from the true line.

<PracticalApplication title="Rasterization" tool="GPUs, game engines">
The incremental trick, "work out the next value from the last with an addition", is everywhere in graphics. GPUs fill triangles by stepping across them incrementally, and DDA itself is used to step through grids, for example to trace a ray across a voxel world or a tile map in classic games.
</PracticalApplication>
